聞而審 / Hearing and Examining

A principle from Lüshi Chunqiu that proper examination of heard information brings fortune, while unexamined hearing is worse than not hearing at all.

English

聞而審 (Wén ér Shěn): Hearing and Examining

This concept originates from Lüshi Chunqiu (呂氏春秋), Chapter 22, in the essay "Yán bù kě yǐ bù chá" (言不可以不察, One Must Not Fail to Examine Words). It states:

> 聞而審則為福矣，聞而不審，不若無聞矣。

Meaning: When one hears something and examines it properly, it becomes a blessing; when one hears without examining, it is better not to have heard at all.

The text further illustrates this through historical examples. Duke Huan of Qi (齊桓公) heard of Guan Zi (管子) through Bao Shu (鮑叔) and carefully examined the recommendation, which led to Qi's hegemony among the lords. Conversely, King Wu of Wu (吳王) heard of King Goujian of Yue (越王句踐) through the treacherous minister Taizai Pì (太宰嚭) and failed to examine it properly, resulting in Wu's destruction. The principle warns against blind acceptance of hearsay and emphasizes that discriminating judgment upon hearing is essential to avoid catastrophe.

中文

聞而審

此語出《[[呂氏春秋]] · 卷二十二 · 察傳》：「聞而審則為福矣，聞而不審，不若無聞矣。」

言傳聞屢經轉述，黑白易位，若聞而不察，其害甚於不聞。齊桓公聞管仲於鮑叔，楚莊王聞孫叔敖於沈尹筮，皆能審察而用之，故國霸諸侯。反是者，吳王聽太宰嚭之言而不審，智伯信張武之說而不察，終致國亡身死。是以聞見之際，不可不慎察也。
