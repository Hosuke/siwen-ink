The Book of De · Chapter Twenty-seven · Assisting Things

English Translation

What is at rest is easy to hold. What has shown no omen yet is easy to plan. What is brittle is easy to split. What is minute is easy to scatter. Act on it before it is; govern it before it is disordered.

A tree two arms round grows from a hair's tip. A nine-story terrace rises from a basket of earth. A height of a hundred fathoms starts from beneath your feet.

Who does it undoes it; who grips it loses it. Hence the sage: non-doing (无為), and so no failure; ungripping, and so no loss. In their undertakings, the people constantly ruin things as they come to completion. Be as careful at the end as at the start: then nothing fails.

Hence the sage desires not-desiring, and does not prize hard-to-get goods; learns not-learning, and repairs the crowd's oversights. He can assist the self-so of the ten thousand things — and dares not contrive.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

Chapter twenty-seven of the Book of De: this chapter takes the title "Assisting Things," and answers to chapter sixty-four of the Wang Bi recension. Assisting the self-so of the ten thousand things is the very core of the sage's non-doing, and the hands-on work of it lies in two places: care at the start, and the heaping of the minute.

"What is at rest is easy to hold; what has shown no omen yet is easy to plan": the silk text breathes a 也 into each clause, and the pace eases open, unhurried, like a man with time. In stillness a thing is easy to hold; before the omen shows it is easy to plan; this is why the sage prizes preparing before the event. Wang Bi drops the 也, and the ease of the breathing is gone. "What is brittle is easy to split; what is minute is easy to scatter": the silk text's 判 and Wang Bi's 泮 pass for each other: both mean to part and melt away.

"A tree two arms round grows from a hair's tip; a nine-story terrace rises from a basket of earth": Wang Bi has "a terrace of nine layers from heaped earth": small variants, one sense. But what matters most is where the two texts' lines part: the silk text says 百仞之高, a height; Wang Bi says 千里之行, a journey. Height is vertical; distance is horizontal. The vertical is the cultivation of De; the horizontal is the galloping of affairs. The silk text stands nearer the root intent of the Dao: look at it long.

"Who does it undoes it; who grips it loses it" — Wang Bi has 為者敗之，執者失之, without the two 之s. The doer lays his will on things, and things refuse it; the gripper will not open his hand, and things leave it. The sage: non-doing, so no failure; ungripping, so no loss: no will laid on, so nothing refuses; no hand closed, so nothing departs. Wang Bi's own note: "the man of highest De uses only the Dao; he does not hold his De as De; he grips nothing and employs nothing: hence he has De, and nothing is left undone."

"In the people's undertakings, they fail, time and again, on the very point of completion": the silk text's 恆 and 亓 become 常 and 幾 in Wang Bi: the failing-when-nearly-done is man's standing disease. Why? At the point of completion, slackness creeps in. Careful at the end as at the start: then nothing fails: keep the first hour's vigilance to the last, and with this one precept the ten thousand things are done.

Then "desiring not-desiring" and "learning not-learning," with which the sage closes the chapter. Desiring not-desiring is the wakeful desirelessness; learning not-learning is the true learning that drops the grip. One road with "doing the not-doing": the learning that turns back on itself. "Assisting the self-so of the ten thousand things": assist, not create; help, not make. Things have their own self-so grain; the sage follows and assists it; contrivance would violate it. The silk text's 弗敢為 is steeper than Wang Bi's 不敢為: 弗 is the word of resolve — not a failure of nerve but a vow. He who is careful at the start diminishes the itch to rush; he who heaps the minute diminishes the dream of speed; he who does not do diminishes the private grip: three diminishings, and the work of assisting things is complete.
