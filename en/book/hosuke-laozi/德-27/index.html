The Book of De · Chapter Twenty-seven · Assisting Things

English Translation

What is at rest is easy to hold […]. […] omen […] is easy to plan […]. […]

[…] tree grows from a hair's tip. A nine-story terrace rises from a basket of earth. A height of a hundred fathoms starts from beneath your feet.

Who does it undoes it; who grips it loses it. Hence the sage: non-doing (无為), […] no failure […]; ungripping, and so no loss. In their undertakings, the people constantly ruin things as they come to completion. Be as careful at the end as at the start: then nothing fails.

Hence the sage desires not-desiring, and does not prize hard-to-get goods; learns not-learning, and repairs the crowd's oversights. He can assist the self-so of the ten thousand things — and dares not contrive.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

This chapter largely follows Manuscript A (MS A), though its opening is badly damaged in both manuscripts; 為之者敗之，執者失之, 則无敗事矣, 是以𦔻人欲不欲 and the last two lines are supplied from Manuscript B (MS B). Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 亓安也易寺也□: Follows MS A; lost in MS B. 寺 is read as 持 "to hold"; Wang Bi reads 其安易持, without the two instances of 也.
  • □□兆□易謀□: Damaged in both. MS A preserves only 兆 and 易謀; 兆, a restoration in the 1980 edition, is read directly by the Jicheng from a fragment newly joined by Chen Jian. Wang Bi reads 其未兆易謀. (Jicheng IV, p. 33, n. 133)
  • □□□□□□ (four lines): The four lines of Wang Bi, 其脆易泮，其微易散；為之於未有，治之於未亂, are lost in both manuscripts. The original editors restored MS A from the space available as 為之於其未有，治之於其未亂, and MS B with a whole passage from 其安也易持 to 治之於其未亂也; the Jicheng, finding that these restorations match no known witness, deletes them and marks lacunae of indeterminate length. The □ counted here merely follow Wang Bi and do not represent the manuscript. (Jicheng IV, p. 33, n. 134; p. 203, n. 123)
  • □□□木 / 作於毫未: 合抱之 is lost in both; 木 survives in MS B. MS A's 作於 was restored as 生於 after the received text in the 1980 edition; the Jicheng, on the strength of the newly joined fragment, reads 作於, which MS B and the Peking University manuscript also have. Wang Bi has 生於. MS A writes 未 for 末 "tip", corrected by the Jicheng as 〈末〉; MS B writes 末. (Jicheng IV, p. 33, n. 135)
  • 九成之臺作於羸土百仁之高: Follows MS A; MS B has 虆 for 羸 and 千 for 仁. 九成 is shared by MS B, Guodian and the Peking University manuscript, while most received texts have 九層. The original editors gloss 作 as "to rise", read 羸 and 虆 as 蔂 "earth basket", and 仁 as 仞 "fathom". MS B's 千 is, in the original note, a mistake for 仁; Gao Ming calls it a miswriting, and the Jicheng suspects that 百千 is an error for 百人 (= 仞). The Peking University manuscript reads 百仞之高 with MS A, as do Yan Zun and the Suizhou text; Wang Bi reads 九層之臺，起於累土；千里之行, replacing "a height of a hundred fathoms" with "a journey of a thousand li". (Jicheng IV, p. 33, nn. 136, 137; p. 203, n. 125)
  • 台於足下: Follows MS A, with 台 read as 始 "to begin"; MS B writes 始. MS A's 下, a restoration in the 1980 edition, is read directly by the Jicheng from a small fragment newly joined by Chen Jian. (Jicheng IV, p. 33, n. 137)
  • 為之者敗之 / 執者失之: Lost in MS A; follows MS B. Wang Bi reads 為者敗之，執者失之. The Jicheng observes that the first half of Wang Bi's chapter 64, from 其安易持 to 始於足下, has no close connection in sense with the second half beginning 為者敗之; in Guodian and the Peking University manuscript they are separate chapters, and in the time of the silk manuscripts they were presumably divided as well, though in MS A this line falls in a lacuna and one cannot tell whether a chapter dot preceded it. (Jicheng IV, p. 32, n. 133)
  • 是以𦔻人无為也□无敗□ / 无執也故无失也: Damaged in both and pieced together: MS B preserves 是以𦔻人无為也, MS A preserves 也, 无敗 and the whole of the next line. Wang Bi reads 是以聖人無為故無敗，無執故無失. In MS A the five graphs 无執也故无 are rewritten over red correction, as the original editors noted; the Jicheng explains that the scribe had first miscopied here words from below, 敗事矣是 and so on, and then corrected them. (Jicheng IV, p. 33, n. 138)
  • 恒於亓成事而敗之: Follows MS A; MS B lacks 事: 恆於亓成而敗之; the Peking University manuscript agrees with MS A. Wang Bi reads 常於幾成而敗之, with 幾成 "nearly complete" for 亓成事. Guodian bundle C has 人之敗也，恆於其且成也而敗之. (Jicheng IV, p. 33, n. 139)
  • 故慎終若始: Follows MS A; MS B reads 故曰慎冬若始, with 冬 read as 終. Wang Bi reads 慎終如始, without 故.
  • 則无敗事矣: MS A preserves only 則; follows MS B. Wang Bi lacks 矣.
  • 是以𦔻人欲不欲: MS A preserves only 欲不欲; follows MS B.
  • 而不貴難得之𦠽: Follows MS A, which writes 貨 "goods" as 𦠽, with 月 (flesh) beside 爲. The original editors note that the Shuowen (貝 radical) has 貝 beside 爲, glossed "possessions; some say this is the old form of 貨", and that the flesh element here is an error. MS B writes 貨. (Jicheng IV, p. 33, n. 140)
  • 而復眾人之所過: Follows MS A; MS B lacks 而.
  • 能輔萬物之自然 / 而弗敢為: In MS A 然 and 而 are lost; follows MS B. Wang Bi reads 以輔萬物之自然，而不敢為, with 以 for 能 and 不 for 弗.

Commentary

Chapter twenty-seven of the Book of De: this chapter takes the title "Assisting Things," and answers to chapter sixty-four of the Wang Bi recension. Assisting the self-so of the ten thousand things is the very core of the sage's non-doing, and the hands-on work of it lies in two places: care at the start, and the heaping of the minute.

"What is at rest is easy to hold. What has shown no omen yet is easy to plan" (manuscript A, 亓安也，易寺也；□□兆□，易謀□; 寺 is read as 持, and by the pattern of the text the damaged graphs should read 亓未兆也，易謀也): the silk text breathes a 也 into each clause, and the pace eases open, unhurried, like a man with time. In stillness a thing is easy to hold; before the omen shows it is easy to plan; this is why the sage prizes preparing before the event. Wang Bi drops the 也, and the ease of the breathing is gone. Wang Bi goes on with four clauses — "what is brittle is easy to melt, what is minute is easy to scatter; act before a thing exists, order it before disorder" — but here both silk manuscripts are lost; the graphs the 1980 editors supplied the Jicheng has struck out as matching no other text, and what the silk text said can no longer be known.

"A tree two arms round grows from a hair's tip; a nine-story terrace rises from a basket of earth" (manuscript A writes 未 for 末, and manuscript B writes 虆 for 羸): Wang Bi has "grows from a hair's tip" with 生 for 作, and "a terrace of nine layers from heaped earth" — small variants, one sense. But what matters most is where the two texts' lines part: the silk text says 百仁之高, a height; Wang Bi says 千里之行, a journey. Height is vertical; distance is horizontal. The vertical is the cultivation of De; the horizontal is the galloping of affairs. The silk text stands nearer the root intent of the Dao: look at it long.

"Who does it undoes it; who grips it loses it" (為之者敗之，執者失之, manuscript B; manuscript A is lost) — Wang Bi has 為者敗之，執者失之, without the 之. The doer lays his will on things, and things refuse it; the gripper will not open his hand, and things leave it. The sage is non-doing, and so does not fail; ungripping, and so does not lose — no will laid on, so nothing refuses; no hand closed, so nothing departs. Wang Bi's own note: "the man of highest De uses only the Dao; he does not hold his De as De; he grips nothing and employs nothing — hence he has De, and nothing is left undone."

"In their undertakings, the people constantly ruin things as they come to completion": the silk text's 恆 and 亓 (so manuscript B; manuscript A writes 恒於亓成事而敗之) become 常 and 幾 in Wang Bi — the failing-when-nearly-done is man's standing disease. Why? At the point of completion, slackness creeps in. "Be as careful at the end as at the start: then nothing fails" — keep the first hour's vigilance to the last, and with this one precept the ten thousand things are done.

Then "desiring not-desiring" and "learning not-learning," with which the sage closes the chapter. Desiring not-desiring is the wakeful desirelessness; learning not-learning is the true learning that drops the grip. One road with "doing the not-doing": the learning that turns back on itself. "Assisting the self-so of the ten thousand things": assist, not create; help, not make. Things have their own self-so grain; the sage follows and assists it; contrivance would violate it. The silk text's 弗敢為 is steeper than Wang Bi's 不敢為: 弗 is the word of resolve. Hence the sage desires not-desiring, and does not prize hard-to-get goods; learns not-learning, and repairs the crowd's oversights; can assist the self-so of the ten thousand things — and dares not contrive. He who is careful at the start diminishes the itch to rush; he who heaps the minute diminishes the dream of speed; he who does not do diminishes the private grip: three diminishings, and the work of assisting things is complete.
