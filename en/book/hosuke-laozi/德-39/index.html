The Book of De · Chapter Thirty-nine · The Master of Killing

English Translation

If the people constantly do not fear death (恆且不畏死), how can killing frighten them?

If the people constantly fear death, then those who act — I can seize and kill them. Who would dare then?

If the people must constantly fear death — then there is, constantly, a master of killing (司殺者).

To kill in the master-of-killing's stead is to hew in the master carpenter's stead. And he who hews in the master carpenter's stead seldom fails to wound his own hand.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

This chapter makes plain that the Way of Heaven cannot be meddled with, and human wit cannot be leaned on. Laozi watched the governance of his age make majesty of punishment and slaughter, not knowing that once the killing machine stirs, Heaven's net falls slack — and no one escapes the harm.

The master of killing is Heaven. He who kills in its stead is a man. A man can kill in Heaven's stead — but cannot keep his own hand unwounded: the standing calamity of every age.

The silk text says 若民恆且不畏死, "if the people constantly do not fear death," where Wang Bi has simply 民不畏死. The silk text's extra 恆且 — "long and further" — says their not-fearing is no matter of a single morning: it is the rule lost, and the people grown used to it. This answers the Great Learning's "what the people love, love": the people's leanings are the mirror of governance. Wang Bi's compression loses the care in Laozi's phrasing.

Again the silk text: 若民恆是畏死, "if the people do still fear death," where Wang Bi writes 若使民常畏死. 是 is "this"; 常 is "constant" — a small shade between the texts, both saying the people constantly fear death.

As for 而為者, "those who act": silk manuscript A has no 奇, "depraved"; manuscript B writes 畸, and Wang Bi 奇. With 奇 the net narrows — only the wicked are executed; without it, whoever so acts stands in the killing line. Foreknowing is the flower of the Dao — to insert 奇 is presumption and foreknowing, not the Dao's purity.

The last section, 若民恆且必畏死 against Wang Bi's blunt "there is always a master of killing to do the killing," marks the parting of the texts most clearly. The silk text first says the people's fear of death is an accomplished fact, and only then comes to the master of killing — the phrasing careful step by step; Wang Bi points straight at the executioner, steeper and hastier. The crux of governing is to hold the middle without leaning. If they do not yet fear, transform them; once rule has brought them to compulsory fear and resorts to killing, the Dao is already lost. Only at the point of becoming, not yet become, is the hour when transformation works.

The last line, 希不傷亓手: the silk text writes 希不, Wang Bi 希有不. 希不 — "few there are who do not"; 希有不 — "few there are who can avoid." A small difference of cadence, one verdict: he who chops in the master carpenter's stead almost never escapes wounding his own hand — the warning is plain. The Way of Heaven is utmost justice; giving life and taking life never miss; a ruler who takes Heaven's place in dealing reward and punishment is like one chopping with the adze in the master carpenter's stead — how could he not wound his hand?

Therefore the sage's governing lies in clear stillness and setting himself straight, letting the people transform of themselves — not in piling up executions to build majesty. The sage puts himself behind, and the people gladly push him forward. Not contending, hence without fault; he who delights in killing can never have his way in all-under-heaven. The best rule makes the people sufficient in themselves; the next best leaves them undisturbed; ruling them by slaughter is the lowest — and wounds the ruler in return. Govern by killing and you end in killing: such is Laozi's deep warning.
