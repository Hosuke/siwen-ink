The Book of De · Chapter Eleven · Non-doing

English Translation

The student gains by the day; the hearer of the Dao (道) diminishes by the day. Diminish, and diminish again, down to non-doing (无為 wuwei — not doing nothing, but doing nothing contrived): non-doing, and nothing left undone.

To take all-under-heaven, be constantly without affairs. Once there are affairs, one is not fit to take all-under-heaven.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

"The student gains by the day; the hearer of the Dao diminishes by the day": the crux of the chapter is the parting of these two roads, learning and the Dao. The student seeks outward among things, adding daily to what he knows; the man of the Dao turns back inward to the heart, paring daily at what he grips. Gaining piles up presence and makes breadth; diminishing lets go the grip and makes emptiness. The two do not harm each other — they differ in where they end: learning culminates in exhaustive knowing; the Dao culminates in non-doing.

Three differences between the texts matter. First, the silk text writes 為學者、聞道者 with the agent-marker 者, which Wang Bi trims away. The silk text names the man who does the thing: "the hearer of the Dao diminishes" means one must already have caught word of the Dao before diminishing can even be spoken of; the order of practice is clear. Second, the silk text has 聞道, "hearing the Dao," for Wang Bi's 為道, "working at the Dao": to force diminishing before hearing is dry withering; "hearing" keeps the precondition — first hear, then diminish, the sequence of entering the Dao laid bare. Third, the silk text's 无為 and 恆无事 against Wang Bi's 無為 and 常以無事: differing graphs, one sense.

Heshang Gong's "Wang Zhi" chapter explains daily diminishing: "passions, desires, and outward ornament dwindle day by day." Wang Bi, commenting on "the highest De does not hold itself as De": "the man of highest De uses only the Dao; he does not hold his De as De, grips nothing, employs nothing, and so has De and leaves nothing undone." Read together, what is diminished is not appetite alone: one diminishes even the heart that wants to diminish. The Zhuangzi's "Da Zong Shi" speaks of "leaving form and letting go of knowing," calling this "sitting in forgetfulness": forgetting too is diminishing; and when nothing is left to diminish, the ground of non-doing is reached. The Great Commentary says the sages "plumb the deepest and study the incipient": the deep is diminishing at its utmost; the incipient is the fine edge of 无 — one pattern with this chapter.

The way to take all-under-heaven lies in being without affairs. It is not taken by arms, by cleverness, by contrived doing, but by being without affairs. The moment there are affairs, the hundred clans look sideways, all-under-heaven shakes, and it cannot be taken. The Huainanzi's "Zhu Shu Xun" says, "The ruler's art is to dwell in the work of non-doing and carry out the teaching without words": this exactly.

Ah — the learning that adds has an end somewhere; the work that diminishes has none. When thoughts are spent, the Dao is pure; the Dao pure, non-doing leaves nothing undone. Here the sage completes an emperor's work — let the student mark it.
