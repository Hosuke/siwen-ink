The Book of De · Chapter Eleven · Non-doing

English Translation

The student gains by the day; the hearer of the Dao (道) diminishes by the day. Diminish, and diminish again, down to no-[…]: […] doing, and nothing not […].

When the sage (𦔻人, read 聖人) takes all-under-heaven, he is constantly without affairs. Once there are affairs, he is no longer fit to take all-under-heaven.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

Manuscript A (MS A) is badly damaged in this chapter, so the silk text follows Manuscript B (MS B), keeping lacunae common to both. Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 為學者日益: Follows MS B. MS A keeps only 為; the restoration below it follows MS B in including 者. Wang Bi reads 為學日益, without 者.
  • 聞道者日云: Follows MS B; 云 is read as 損 "to diminish". MS A keeps 道; the original editors restored the graph above it as 聞 after MS B, but since the Guodian slips, the Peking University manuscript and the received texts all read 為道 "working at the Dao", the Jicheng judges 聞 or 為 undecidable and gives □. Wang Bi reads 為道日損. (Jicheng IV, p. 17, n. 53)
  • 云之有云: Follows MS B. The 云 at the end of the previous line carries a repetition mark that also opens this line; it is written out here. 有 is read as 又 "again". Wang Bi reads 損之又損.
  • 以至於无□ / □為而无不□: Damaged in both. MS A read 以至於无﹦為﹦而无不為: a fragment spanning three lines, newly joined by Chen Jian, keeps in its middle column 而无不 and the tail of the repetition mark at the lower right of 為, which the Jicheng reads directly. MS B ends 為也, read from the mirror-image offset and the new fragment; the 1980 edition restored 為矣, but the particle is 也. Wang Bi reads 以至於無為。無為而無不為. (Jicheng IV, pp. 17–18, n. 54; p. 200, n. 40)
  • 𦔻人之取天下恆无事: Follows MS B; 𦔻 is read as 聖 "sage". The 1980 edition restored 將欲 before 取天下 after the Fu Yi and other texts; Chen Jian, guided by the offset, joined a small piece bearing 𦔻人之, proving that restoration wrong. The reading "the sage's taking of all-under-heaven" is found in no other witness. MS A reads □□取天下也恒: the two graphs before 取 are lost (restored 將欲 in 1980, left as lacunae in the Jicheng), and MS A has 也 after 下. Wang Bi reads 取天下常以無事. (Jicheng IV, p. 18, n. 54; p. 200, n. 41)
  • 及亓有事也: Follows MS B; lost in MS A. Wang Bi reads 及其有事.
  • 有不足以取天下矣: Follows MS B; 有 is read as 又. The original editors restored this line from the Fu Yi text; the Jicheng reads 有不 and 下矣 from the offset on the backing silk, confirming the restoration. Wang Bi reads 不足以取天下, without 又 and 矣. (Jicheng IV, p. 18, n. 55; p. 200, n. 43)

Commentary

"The student gains by the day; the hearer of the Dao diminishes by the day": the crux of the chapter is the parting of these two roads, learning and the Dao. The student seeks outward among things, adding daily to what he knows; the man of the Dao turns back inward to the heart, paring daily at what he grips. Gaining piles up presence and makes breadth; diminishing lets go the grip and makes emptiness. The two do not harm each other — they differ in where they end: learning culminates in exhaustive knowing; the Dao culminates in non-doing.

Three differences between the texts matter. First, manuscript B reads 為學者日益，聞道者日云 (云 read as 損; manuscript A is damaged here), with the agent-marker 者 twice, which Wang Bi trims away. The silk text names the man who does the thing: "the hearer of the Dao diminishes" means one must already have caught word of the Dao before diminishing can even be spoken of; the order of practice is clear. Second, manuscript B has 聞道, "hearing the Dao," for Wang Bi's 為道, "working at the Dao" (in manuscript A the graph above 道 is lost, and the Jicheng, since the Guodian slips and the Peking University manuscript both read 為道, does not take it for 聞): to force diminishing before hearing is dry withering; "hearing" keeps the precondition — first hear, then diminish, the sequence of entering the Dao laid bare. Third, the silk text's 无為 and 恆无事 against Wang Bi's 無為 and 常以無事: differing graphs, one sense.

Heshang Gong's "Wang Zhi" chapter explains daily diminishing: "passions, desires, and outward ornament dwindle day by day." Wang Bi, commenting on "the highest De does not hold to De": "the man of highest De uses only the Dao; he does not hold his De as De, grips nothing, employs nothing, and so has De and leaves nothing undone." Read together, what is diminished is not appetite alone: one diminishes even the heart that wants to diminish. The Zhuangzi's "Da Zong Shi" speaks of "leaving form and letting go of knowing," calling this "sitting in forgetfulness": forgetting too is diminishing; and when nothing is left to diminish, the ground of non-doing is reached. The Great Commentary says the sages "plumb the deepest and study the incipient": the deep is diminishing at its utmost; the incipient is the fine edge of 无 — one pattern with this chapter.

The way to take all-under-heaven lies in being without affairs. It is not taken by arms, by cleverness, by contrived doing, but by being without affairs. The moment there are affairs, the hundred clans look sideways, all-under-heaven shakes, and it cannot be taken. The Huainanzi's "Zhu Shu Xun" says, "The ruler's art is to dwell in the work of non-doing and carry out the teaching without words": this exactly.

Ah — the learning that adds has an end somewhere; the work that diminishes has none. When thoughts are spent, the Dao is pure; the Dao pure, non-doing leaves nothing undone. Here the sage completes an emperor's work — let the student mark it.
