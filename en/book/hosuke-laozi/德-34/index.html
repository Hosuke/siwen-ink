The Book of De · Chapter Thirty-four · The Use of Arms

English Translation

The masters of arms have a saying: I dare not play the host, but play the guest; I do not advance an inch, but fall back (芮) a foot.

This is called: march, yet seem not to march; raise an arm, yet seem armless; hold a weapon, yet seem weaponless — and so, no enemy.

No calamity is greater than having no match (无適). Having no match comes close to losing my treasures.

So when armies are raised and evenly matched — it is the sorrowing side that wins.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

This chapter follows Manuscript A (MS A) throughout. Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng). The original editors remark that MS A's text of this chapter is full of scribal errors; the graphs are recorded as they stand and corrected in the notes below.

  • 用兵有言曰吾不敢為主而為客: Follows MS A; Manuscript B (MS B) writes 又 for 有, read as 有. Wang Bi reads 用兵有言：吾不敢為主而為客.
  • 吾不進寸而芮尺: Follows MS A. The original editors note that for MS A's 吾不 all other texts have 不敢; MS B and the Peking University manuscript read 不敢進寸而退尺, as do the received texts. MS A writes 芮 where MS B and Wang Bi have 退 "to fall back". (Jicheng IV, p. 35, n. 168)
  • 是胃行无行 / 襄无臂 / 執无兵: Follows MS A; MS B writes 攘 "to bare the arm", and MS A's 襄 is read as 攘. In MS A, 執 was struck through in red. Wang Bi has an extra line, 扔無敵, after 攘無臂 and before 執無兵. (Jicheng IV, p. 35, n. 170)
  • 乃无敵矣: Follows MS A; MS B lacks 矣. The Jicheng points out that 矣 is absent from all other texts, including MS B and the Peking University manuscript; by adding it MS A seems to take 乃 in its ordinary sense ("then") rather than as 扔, though this sits awkwardly with the next line, 禍莫大於无敵. Wang Bi lacks this line and instead has 扔無敵 above. (Jicheng IV, p. 35, n. 169)
  • 𠪾莫於於无適无適斤亡吾吾葆矣: Follows MS A, which writes 𠪾莫於於无﹦適﹦斤亡吾吾葆矣, with repetition marks after 无適. The original editors note that 大於 is miswritten 於於 (the first 於 struck through in red but not corrected) and that 吾葆 has a superfluous 吾. 𠪾 is 禍 "calamity" — on the plate 厤 above 心, though the Jicheng prints a form with 咼 and 㤅; 適 is read as 敵 "match, enemy", 斤 as 近 "near to", 葆 as 寶 "treasure". MS B reads 禍莫大於無﹦敵﹦近亡吾𤥯矣; at its 亡 a graph was first spoiled and then circled for deletion, printed 〇 in the Jicheng. (Jicheng IV, p. 35, n. 170; p. 203, n. 136)
  • 无適: MS A has 无適, MS B 無敵, as does the Peking University manuscript; most received texts have 輕敵 "taking the enemy lightly", Fu Yi alone 無敵, and Gao Ming holds that 無敵 is original and 輕敵 a later alteration. Wang Bi reads 禍莫大於輕敵，輕敵幾喪吾寶. (Jicheng IV, p. 35, n. 169)
  • 故稱兵相若: Follows MS A; MS B, the Peking University manuscript and the received texts all have 抗 for 稱 (the Peking University manuscript writing 亢). MS A's 若 is struck through in red; the original editors gloss 若 as "to match" and suspect that the received 加 is a miswriting of 如; MS B, the Peking University manuscript, Fu Yi and several Dunhuang manuscripts all read 相若. Wang Bi reads 故抗兵相加. (Jicheng IV, p. 35, n. 171)
  • 則哀者勝矣: Follows MS A; MS B reads 而依者朕□, with 依 read as 哀 "grieving" and 朕 as 勝, its last graph lost. Wang Bi reads 哀者勝矣, without 則.

Commentary

Yaomiao says: on the use of arms, Laozi has spoken with care. "I dare not play the host, but play the guest": to play the host is to strike first and force the issue — exalting strength; to play the guest is to move after the other, answering the enemy as he comes — prizing the soft. He who strikes first seizes by intent and loses the power to answer; he who answers moves with the enemy and so never misses. This stands flat against the military men's doctrine of preemptive strike: Laozi wins by never going first — that is the crux. "I do not advance an inch, but fall back a foot" (manuscript A; manuscript B and Wang Bi both read 不敢進寸而退尺, "I dare not advance an inch, but retreat a foot"): manuscript A writes 芮, manuscript B and Wang Bi 退. 芮 and 退 are close in sound and should be loans for each other; or, taking 芮 at face value, it carries shrinking inward — more gathered-in than retreat. Not gaining an inch but yielding a foot: the desire for an inch's advance is not worth a foot's retreat from harm — the utmost of the soft within.

"Marching with no march, baring no arm, wielding no weapon" (襄, read as 攘, "to bare") — one thread of sense. Marching yet seeming not to march; an arm raised yet seeming armless; a weapon held yet seeming weaponless — seemingly absent, really present. The enemy sees the shape but not the substance; he would strike and finds no purchase. This is the invisible army. "No enemy" does not mean no enemy exists — it means no form to spy out, no track to follow, so the enemy dares not offend. The silk text closes the series with 乃无敵, "and so, no enemy" (manuscript A adds 矣; manuscript B lacks it); the Wang Bi recension reads 扔無敵 and places it before 執無兵. The Jicheng notes that no other text has 矣, and that manuscript A, in adding it, seems to take 乃 in its ordinary sense — but then the line clashes with the next, "no calamity is greater than having no match"; read 乃 as 扔, and it falls into the same pattern as "marching with no march, baring no arm, wielding no weapon." Yaomiao ventures: read as 扔, the four lines run alike, each an image of the seemingly absent that is really present; read with manuscript A as a closing phrase, it says that thus one has no enemy in all-under-heaven, and the next line at once warns of the calamity of having no match — the two readings may stand together.

Then "no calamity is greater than having no match" — manuscript A writes 𠪾莫於於无適 (𠪾 is 禍, "calamity"; it lacks 大 and writes 於 twice, likely a copyist's slip), manuscript B 禍莫大於無敵 — against Wang Bi's 輕敵, "taking the enemy lightly." Here the two texts diverge most widely, and most readers miss it. 適 is a match, a peer. Having no match means none in all-under-heaven can stand against you. Why should that be calamity? Unmatched, one grows proud; proud, one grows careless; careless, one loses vigilance; vigilance lost, one suffers the very harm. "Having no match comes close to losing my treasures" (manuscript A has 无適斤亡吾葆, though it actually writes 吾吾葆 with a redundant 吾; 斤 read as 近, 葆 as 寶) — the loss of nurturing love, of thrift, of not daring to stand first: the three treasures die of having no match. Lightness toward the enemy is the fruit; having no match is the root. Wang Bi names the fruit; the silk text goes straight to the root — here it runs deeper. Among the three treasures of the previous chapter, nurturing love is the first: nurturing love makes one brave; the nurturing heart dead, bravery has nowhere to grow — hence "close to losing."

"So when armies are raised and evenly matched, it is the sorrowing side that wins." The sorrowing one is neither fearful nor weak: his heart holds the aching fellow-feeling, and he does not spend death lightly. When strength is equal, the side that pities uses arms with care and measure; the side hot for blood, however strong, must lose. This is "nurturing, one can be brave," enacted in the art of war. The sorrowing one is he who does not take the enemy lightly. Ah — arms are instruments of ill omen; the sage uses them only when he must, prizing calm detachment, finding no glory in them. Know having-no-match for the calamity it is, and you know the three treasures for treasures; know the sorrowing side for the winner, and you know nurturing softness for the root — governing the self and governing the world, one thread through all.
