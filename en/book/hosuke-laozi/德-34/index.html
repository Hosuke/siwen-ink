The Book of De · Chapter Thirty-four · The Use of Arms

English Translation

The masters of arms have a saying: I dare not play the host, but play the guest; dare not advance an inch, but fall back (芮) a foot.

This is called: march, yet seem not to march; raise an arm, yet seem armless; hold a weapon, yet seem weaponless — and so, no enemy.

No calamity is greater than having no match (无適). Having no match comes close to losing my treasures.

So when armies are raised and evenly matched — it is the sorrowing side that wins.

Commentary

Yaomiao says: on the use of arms, Laozi has spoken with care. "I dare not play the host, but play the guest": to play the host is to strike first and force the issue — exalting strength; to play the guest is to move after the other, answering the enemy as he comes — prizing the soft. He who strikes first seizes by intent and loses the power to answer; he who answers moves with the enemy and so never misses. This stands flat against the military men's doctrine of preemptive strike: Laozi wins by never going first — that is the crux. "Dare not advance an inch, but fall back a foot": the silk text writes 芮, Wang Bi writes 退. 芮 carries shrinking inward — more gathered-in than retreat. Not gaining an inch but yielding a foot: the desire for an inch's advance is not worth a foot's retreat from harm — the utmost of the soft within.

"Marching with no march, baring no arm, wielding no weapon" — one thread of sense. Marching yet seeming not to march; an arm raised yet seeming armless; a weapon held yet seeming weaponless — seemingly absent, really present. The enemy sees the shape but not the substance; he would strike and finds no purchase. This is the invisible army. "No enemy" does not mean no enemy exists — it means no form to spy out, no track to follow, so the enemy dares not offend. The silk text closes the series with the four words 乃无敵矣, "and so, no enemy"; the Wang Bi recension recasts them as 扔無敵 and folds them into the middle of the series — the closing force is lost.

Then "no calamity is greater than having no match" — against Wang Bi's 輕敵, "taking the enemy lightly." Here the two texts diverge most widely, and most readers miss it. 適 is a match, a peer. Having no match means none in all-under-heaven can stand against you. Why should that be calamity? Unmatched, one grows proud; proud, one grows careless; careless, one loses vigilance; vigilance lost, one suffers the very harm. "Having no match comes close to losing my treasures" — the loss of nurturing love, of thrift, of not daring to stand first: the three treasures die of having no match. Lightness toward the enemy is the fruit; having no match is the root. Wang Bi names the fruit; the silk text goes straight to the root — here it runs deeper. Laozi takes nurturing love for a treasure: nurturing love makes one brave; the nurturing heart dead, bravery has nowhere to grow — hence "close to losing."

"So when armies are raised and evenly matched, it is the sorrowing side that wins." The sorrowing one is neither fearful nor weak: his heart holds the aching fellow-feeling, and he does not spend death lightly. When strength is equal, the side that pities uses arms with care and measure; the side hot for blood, however strong, must lose. This is "nurturing, one can be brave," enacted in the art of war. The sorrowing one is he who does not take the enemy lightly. Ah — arms are instruments of ill omen; the sage uses them only when he must, prizing calm detachment, finding no glory in them. Know having-no-match for the calamity it is, and you know the three treasures for treasures; know the sorrowing side for the winner, and you know nurturing softness for the root — governing the self and governing the world, one thread through all.
