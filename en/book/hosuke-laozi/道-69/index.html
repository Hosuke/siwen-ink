The Book of Dao · Chapter Twenty-five · Undivided at Its Making

English Translation

There is a thing, undivided at its making (有物昆成), born before heaven and earth.

Silent, ah, empty, ah (蕭呵漻呵) — it stands alone and does not change. It can be the mother of heaven and earth.

I do not know its name: I call it, by courtesy name, the Dao (字之曰道); and, forced to give it a name, I call it the Great (大).

The Great is called casting-forth (筮); casting-forth is called far; far is called turning-back (反).

The Dao is great; heaven is great; earth is great; and the king too is great.

In the state (國中) there are four greats — and the king holds one place among them.

Man patterns on earth; earth patterns on heaven; heaven patterns on the Dao; the Dao patterns on the self-so (道法自然).

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

The text follows Manuscript A (MS A); the lines 蕭呵漻呵獨立而不𪻞, 大曰筮, 遠曰反 and 道大, and the last three lines, damaged in MS A, are supplied from Manuscript B (MS B). Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 有物昆成: The manuscripts agree; the Jicheng reads 昆 as 混 "undivided, merged". Wang Bi has 有物混成.
  • 蕭呵漻呵獨立而不𪻞: Follows MS B. MS A reads 繡何繆何獨立□□□, with 何 read as 呵; the three graphs for "and does not change" are lost (end of MS A, col. 140). The Jicheng reads MS A's 繡 and 繆 and MS B's 蕭 and 漻 alike as 寂 "silent" and 寥 "empty". MS B's last graph is 𪻞 (王 beside 亥), read as 改 "to change"; Gao Ming's transcription gives 孩, but on the plate the left element is clearly 王. Wang Bi has 寂兮寥兮，獨立而不改, followed by a line, 周行而不殆, which neither silk manuscript has.
  • 可以為天地母: The manuscripts agree; Wang Bi has 可以為天下母.
  • 吾未知其名: Follows MS A; MS B reads 吾未知亓名也, ending in 也. Wang Bi has 吾不知其名.
  • 吾強為之名曰大: The manuscripts agree; Wang Bi has 強為之名曰大, without 吾.
  • 大曰筮: In MS A the repetition mark after 大 is lost and restored by the Jicheng; the line follows MS B. 筮 is read as 逝 "to pass on"; Wang Bi has 大曰逝.
  • 筮曰遠 / 遠曰反: 筮曰遠 follows MS A. MS A's 遠 lay within a restoration in the 1980 edition and is read directly by the Jicheng from a small newly joined fragment; the repetition mark below it is lost and is treated as restored. 遠曰反 is lost in MS A and follows MS B, whose 反 is read as 返 "to return". (Jicheng IV, p. 52, n. 98)
  • 道大: Lost in MS A; follows MS B. Wang Bi has 故道大.
  • 王亦大 / 而王居一焉: Both manuscripts write 王 "the king", as the plates, the 1980 edition and Gao Ming's transcription all agree; transcriptions that give 人亦大 and 而人居一焉 are mistaken. Wang Bi has 王亦大 and 而王居其一焉.
  • 國中有四大: The manuscripts agree; the Jicheng reads 國 "state" as 域 "realm". Wang Bi has 域中有四大.
  • 地法天 / 天法道 / 道法自然: In MS A the repetition mark after 人法地 is lost and only 法天﹦法 survives; the rest is supplied from MS B, which links the clauses with repetition marks: 人法地﹦法天﹦法道﹦法自然.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

This chapter gives the most complete account of the Dao's substance and function in the whole book; a student must not skim it.

The silk text writes 有物昆成, "a thing undivided at its making," where the Wang Bi recension writes 混成. The Jicheng reads 昆 as 混; I venture that it may also be read as written. 昆 is undivided wholeness — the time before the Dao was cut open, when the ten thousand things were one lump with no seam. 混 already smacks of mixture; 昆 is simply what it is of itself. "Undivided at its making" shows the Dao born before heaven and earth, whole of itself, not pieced together from anything. The Great Ancestral Teacher says: "the Dao has feeling and faithfulness, no doing and no form; its own root, its own stock — before there was heaven and earth, from of old it stood firm." Just this.

Next, 繡何繆何 (so manuscript A, 何 read as 呵; manuscript B has 蕭呵漻呵), "woven, ah, wound round, ah," where Wang Bi has 寂兮寥兮, "silent, ah, empty, ah." The Jicheng reads 繡 and 蕭 as 寂, 繆 and 漻 as 寥, and then the silk text and Wang Bi agree. Taken as written, silent-and-empty is the look of vacant stillness; woven-and-wound is the look of threads interlaced — different faces, both saying the Dao cannot be sought by form or name. 繡 like silk woven through silk, 繆 like cord coiled on cord: through all-under-heaven the Dao runs, nowhere it does not reach, nothing it does not enter — interwoven, coursing round, its use is vast.

"I call it, by courtesy name, the Dao; forced to give it a name, I call it the Great" — the sage naming under protest. The book's first chapter says, "the Dao that can be spoken of is not the constant Dao": he forces the courtesy name, forces the name Great — and this is no arbitrary invention. Every name is borrowed; let the reader get the meaning and forget the words.

"The Great is called casting-forth (筮); casting-forth is called far; far is called turning-back." The Wang Bi text puts 逝 in place of 筮: "the Great is called passing; passing is called far; far is called turning-back." The Jicheng reads 筮 as 逝. Taken as written, 筮 is the diviner's work — casting the yarrow, pushing the reckoning forth; 逝, passing, also passes as a reading. For the Dao to be Great it must go out; going out, it goes far; going far, it turns back — coursing round without fail, ending and so beginning. The Book of Changes: "no level that does not tilt; no going that does not return" — the law is here.

Wang Bi writes 域中有四大, "in the realm there are four greats," where the silk text writes 國中, "in the state" — the Jicheng reads 國 as 域, and then it agrees with Wang Bi; taken as written, the sense is a little narrower. The realm is boundless; the state has borders — for the Dao's sake, the realm is the better. But the point of the four greats is one: the Dao is great, heaven is great, earth is great, and the king too is great — both silk manuscripts write 王, as Wang Bi does. The king is mankind's representative: that man can stand level with heaven-and-earth's greatness — only by the king as its representative.

"Man patterns on earth; earth patterns on heaven; heaven patterns on the Dao; the Dao patterns on the self-so" — the order of the four patternings. Man patterns on earth, taking its thick De that favors none; earth patterns on heaven, taking its covering-and-bearing that favors none; heaven patterns on the Dao, taking its accomplishing without ruling; and the Dao patterns on the self-so — not "nature" as the natural world, but the so-of-itself: that by which the Dao is what it is. Even the Dao has something it patterns on — the self-so is the Dao of the Dao. Here is the eye of the chapter.

The Dao in all-under-heaven: great, far, wrapping the cosmos — and coming home, always, to the self-so. The sage knows the turning-back; the hundred clans use it daily and never know. Let the student gather this law of the circle, and the point of the Dao's birthing heaven and earth is more than half understood.
