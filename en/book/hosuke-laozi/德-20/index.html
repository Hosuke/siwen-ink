The Book of De · Chapter Twenty · Ordering the State

English Translation

Order the state by the straight (正); use troops by the irregular (畸); take all-under-heaven by being without affairs. How do I know it is so?

The more taboos and avoidances in all-under-heaven, the poorer the people. The more sharp tools the people hold, the murkier the state. The more cleverness men have, the more freak things arise. The more laws and statutes flourish, the more thieves and robbers there are.

Hence the sage's saying: I practice non-doing (无為), and the people transform of themselves. I love stillness, and the people set themselves straight. I am without affairs, and the people enrich themselves. I desire not-desiring (欲不欲), and the people of themselves are unhewn wood.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

Ordering the state, using troops, taking all-under-heaven: three in a row, each deeper than the last. To order the state by the straight is to establish its constant; to use troops by the irregular is to answer the exceptional; to take all-under-heaven by being without affairs is to keep to the transforming. Without the constant a state cannot be ordered; without the exceptional an army cannot answer; and short of being without affairs, all-under-heaven cannot be taken. As the old teaching has it, the sage sets himself straight and only then acts — himself straight, and the people transform of themselves. The sense runs through this chapter.

The silk text says 以畸用兵 where Wang Bi has 以奇用兵. 畸 means what departs from the norm: the Shuowen glosses it "leftover field," the odd plot outside the grid, extended to whatever stands opposite the straight; it is not the 奇 of the cunning stratagem. Laozi holds war ill-omened, a thing the sage takes up only when he must — and even then he does not build merit on deceit; the straight way remains the root. That is the excellence of the graph 畸. 奇 already verges on fraud.

The harms of having-affairs Laozi counts in four. Many taboos under heaven, and the people, hemmed at every turn, sink daily into poverty: one. Many sharp tools among the people, arms flourish, contention rises, and the state darkens by the day: two. Much cleverness in men, freak things multiply, the people dazzled by novelty and the common ways rotting: three. Laws and edicts flourished, the net drawn fine, the people fearing punishment; fear breeds fraud, and fraud breeds robbers: four. All four issue from contrivance above, none from the self-so of the Way of Heaven. As the histories record of Cao Can governing by the Huang-Lao art: "he prized clear stillness, and the people settled of themselves" — the proof is on the record.

Hence the sage's saying: I practice non-doing, and the people transform of themselves; I love stillness, and the people set themselves straight; I am without affairs, and the people enrich themselves; I desire not-desiring, and the people of themselves are unhewn wood. Four clauses, one thread, gathered up in being without affairs. And the fourth deserves the finest weighing: the silk text's 我欲不欲, "I desire not-desiring," against Wang Bi's 我無欲, "I am without desire." To desire not-desiring is a wakeful desire — desiring what should be desired, refusing what should be refused — and so its transforming power spreads of itself. Mere desirelessness may be numbness; desiring-not-desiring is awakening, and the desirelessness of the awakened is why the people's plainness is pure. Here the silk text runs deeper than Wang Bi.

Being without affairs is not doing not-one-thing; it is not doing for oneself, not doing recklessly. The sage holds the One as his law and keeps the constant of being without affairs — will all-under-heaven not come home to him? The Great Learning says "the noble man uses his utmost everywhere" — and is that utmost anything but the utmost of being without affairs? Alas that today's governors reverse it: prohibitions multiply and the people grow poorer by the day; ingenuity flourishes and fraud fattens by the day; statutes gleam and robbers throng by the day: contrivance devouring non-doing. One long sigh.
