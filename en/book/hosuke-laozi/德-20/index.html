The Book of De · Chapter Twenty · Ordering the State

English Translation

Order the state by the straight (正); use troops by the irregular (畸); take all-under-heaven by being without affairs. How do I know it is so?

The more taboos and avoidances in all-under-heaven, the poorer the people. The more sharp tools the people hold, the murkier the state. The more cleverness men have, the more freak things arise. The more the prized things of the law (法物) are put on show, the more thieves and robbers there are.

Hence the sage's saying: I practice non-doing (无為), and the people transform of themselves. I love stillness, and the people set themselves straight. I am without affairs, and the people enrich themselves. I desire not-desiring (欲不欲), and the people of themselves are unhewn wood.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

This chapter follows Manuscript A (MS A); where MS A is damaged (吾何以知亓然也才, 夫天下多忌諱, 而盜賊多又, the line beginning 是以𦔻人之言曰 and the last two lines) it is supplied from Manuscript B (MS B), and □□物茲章 is damaged in both. Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 以正之邦: Follows MS A, which opens with a chapter-division dot; MS B writes 國 for 邦. 之 is read as 治 "to order"; Wang Bi reads 以正治國.
  • 以畸用兵: Both manuscripts agree. The received texts all read 以奇用兵 and the Peking University manuscript has 倚; all are read as 奇 "irregular". (Jicheng IV, p. 28, n. 108)
  • 以无事取天下: Follows MS A; MS B writes 無 for 无.
  • 吾何以知亓然也才: Follows MS B; 才 is read as 哉. MS A keeps only 吾何 and 也𢦏, 𢦏 likewise being read as 哉. The Peking University and Guodian manuscripts lack the final 哉. Most received texts add 以此 "by this" after the line, Wang Bi reading 吾何以知其然哉？以此; both silk manuscripts, like Yan Zun and a few other received texts, lack it, and Gao Ming holds that it does not belong here. (Jicheng IV, p. 28, n. 109)
  • 夫天下多忌諱: Follows MS B; in MS A 多忌 is lost and 諱 is read directly by the Jicheng. Wang Bi lacks 夫.
  • 而民彌貧: Follows MS A, with a check mark after the line. MS A writes 彌 with 弓 beside 璽, MS B as 㣆; both are read as 彌 "the more".
  • 民多利器 / 而邦家茲昏: Follows MS A; 茲 is read as 滋 "the more". In MS B there is damage after 利器, where 而 must also have stood; its 國, bracketed as a restoration in 1980, is read directly from a fragment stuck to the backing silk, and its 茲, restored as 滋 in 1980, is read from the offset. MS B writes 國 for 邦; Wang Bi reads 國家滋昬. (Jicheng IV, p. 28, n. 111; p. 202, n. 88)
  • 人多知: Both manuscripts agree. The original editors restored MS B as 人多智慧 from the Fu Yi text; the Jicheng reads 人多知 from the offset on the backing silk and rejects the old restoration. Guodian and the Peking University manuscript write 智; Wang Bi reads 人多伎巧. (Jicheng IV, p. 28, n. 112; p. 202, n. 89)
  • 而何物茲起: Follows MS A; MS B reads 而奇物茲起, bracketed as a restoration in 1980 and now read from the offset. Wang Bi reads 奇物滋起. Qiu Xigui, drawing on the Peking University manuscript, argues that MS A's 何物, the Guodian 戓勿 and the 奇物 of MS B and the received texts all stand for an original 苛物 "vexatious things". (Jicheng IV, pp. 28–29, n. 112; p. 202, n. 90)
  • □□物茲章: Damaged in both. MS A is badly damaged and was restored in full by the original editors from MS B and the received texts; MS B lacks only its first graph, reading □物茲章, 茲章 being read as 滋彰 "the more displayed". Guodian and the Peking University manuscript begin with 法物, as do Heshang Gong and some other texts; most received texts have 法令 "laws and edicts", and Wang Bi reads 法令滋彰. (Jicheng IV, p. 29, n. 113)
  • 而盜賊多又: Follows MS B; 又 is read as 有. MS B's 多又, restored as 多有 in 1980, is read from the offset. MS A keeps only 盜賊. Wang Bi reads 盜賊多有, without 而. (Jicheng IV, p. 202, n. 91)
  • 是以𦔻人之言曰我无為而民自化: Follows MS B; 𦔻 is read as 聖 "sage", restored in 1980 and now read from the offset. The first half is lost in MS A, which reads 我无為也而民自化, with 也 after 為. Wang Bi reads 故聖人云：我無為而民自化. (Jicheng IV, p. 202, n. 92)
  • 我孜靜而民自正: Follows MS A; 孜 is read as 好 "to love". MS B writes 好, as does Wang Bi.
  • 我无事而民自富 / 我欲不欲而民自樸: Follows MS B. MS A reads 我无事民□□ in the first line, without 而, and has lost the second entirely. Wang Bi reads the second line 我無欲而民自樸.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

Ordering the state, using troops, taking all-under-heaven: three in a row, each deeper than the last. To order the state by the straight is to establish its constant; to use troops by the irregular is to answer the exceptional; to take all-under-heaven by being without affairs is to keep to the transforming. Without the constant a state cannot be ordered; without the exceptional an army cannot answer; and short of being without affairs, all-under-heaven cannot be taken. As the old teaching has it, the sage sets himself straight and only then acts — himself straight, and the people transform of themselves. The sense runs through this chapter.

The silk text says 以畸用兵 where Wang Bi has 以奇用兵. 畸 means what departs from the norm: the Shuowen glosses it "leftover field," the odd plot outside the grid, extended to whatever stands opposite the straight; it is not the 奇 of the cunning stratagem. Laozi holds war ill-omened, a thing the sage takes up only when he must — and even then he does not build merit on deceit; the straight way remains the root. That is the excellence of the graph 畸. 奇 already verges on fraud.

The harms of having-affairs Laozi counts in four. Many taboos under heaven, and the people, hemmed at every turn, sink daily into poverty: one. Many sharp tools among the people, arms flourish, contention rises, and the state darkens by the day: two. Much cleverness in men, freak things multiply, the people dazzled by novelty and the common ways rotting: three. Prized things of the law ever more on show (法物滋彰), the edicts drawn fine, the people fearing punishment; fear breeds fraud, and fraud breeds robbers: four. All four issue from contrivance above, none from the self-so of the Way of Heaven. As the histories record of Cao Can governing by the Huang-Lao art: "he prized clear stillness, and the people settled of themselves" — the proof is on the record.

Hence the sage's saying: I practice non-doing, and the people transform of themselves; I love stillness (我孜靜; manuscript B: 好靜), and the people set themselves straight; I am without affairs, and the people enrich themselves; I desire not-desiring, and the people of themselves are unhewn wood. Four clauses, one thread, gathered up in being without affairs. And the fourth deserves the finest weighing: the silk text's 我欲不欲, "I desire not-desiring," against Wang Bi's 我無欲, "I am without desire." To desire not-desiring is a wakeful desire — desiring what should be desired, refusing what should be refused — and so its transforming power spreads of itself. Mere desirelessness may be numbness; desiring-not-desiring is awakening, and the desirelessness of the awakened is why the people's plainness is pure. Here the silk text runs deeper than Wang Bi.

Being without affairs is not doing not-one-thing; it is not doing for oneself, not doing recklessly. The sage holds the One as his law and keeps the constant of being without affairs — will all-under-heaven not come home to him? The Great Learning says "the noble man uses his utmost everywhere" — and is that utmost anything but the utmost of being without affairs? Alas that governors age after age reverse it: prohibitions multiply and the people grow poorer by the day; ingenuity flourishes and fraud fattens by the day; statutes gleam and robbers throng by the day: contrivance devouring non-doing. One long sigh.
