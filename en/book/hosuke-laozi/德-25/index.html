The Book of De · Chapter Twenty-five · The Pouring-place

English Translation

The Dao (道) is where the ten thousand things pour (注): the treasure (𤥯, read 寶) of the good man, and what even the not-good man holds and keeps (𤥯, read 保).

Fine words can be traded in the market; honored conduct can be offered in congratulation to others (賀人). As for a man's not-being-good: what discarding could there be?

So when the Son of Heaven is enthroned and the three ministers installed: though the arm-clasped jade disc go ahead of the four-horse team, that is not so good as sitting where you are and advancing this.

Why did the ancients prize this? Did they not say: seek, and by it you find; be guilty, and by it you are spared? Therefore it is the prized of all-under-heaven.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

This chapter follows Manuscript A (MS A), except that the first graph of the opening line, lost in MS A, is supplied from Manuscript B (MS B). Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 道者萬物之注也: The first graph, 道, is lost in MS A and supplied from MS B. Both manuscripts have 注 "pouring-place", the Peking University manuscript 椯, Wang Bi 奧 "inner corner". The original editors read 注 as 主 "master", citing Zheng Xuan's gloss on the Liji ("Liyun"): "奧 is like 主". (Jicheng IV, p. 32, n. 124)
  • 善人之𤥯也 / 不善人之所𤥯也: MS A writes both graphs as 𤥯 (王 beside 呆), which the Jicheng reads first as 寶 "treasure", then as 保 "to keep"; MS B has 𤥯 in the first line and 保 in the second. Wang Bi reads 善人之寶，不善人之所保.
  • 美言可以市 / 尊行可以賀人: MS A, MS B and the Peking University manuscript agree exactly, all with 賀; the 1980 edition, following the received text, read 賀 as 加 "to bestow upon", and the Jicheng retains that reading. Wang Bi has 尊行可以加人. The Jicheng further suspects that 市 here is the same as the 市 of MS B's 市（持）而𤥯之 in Wang Bi's chapter 67 and should likewise be read 持 "to hold to", balancing 加人. (Jicheng IV, p. 32, n. 125)
  • 人之不善也: In both manuscripts the 人 of 賀人 carries a repetition mark, to be read 賀人，人之不善也; the 人 that opens this line comes from that mark.
  • 何棄□有: Damaged in both. In MS A, 棄 is read directly by the Jicheng, and the graph after it is lost (Wang Bi has 之); MS B preserves only 何.
  • 故立天子 / 置三卿: The manuscripts agree, except that MS B miswrites 卿 as 鄉; Wang Bi and the Peking University manuscript have 三公 "three dukes" for 三卿 "three ministers". MS B's 故, a restoration in the 1980 edition, is read directly from the mirror-image offset on the backing silk in the Jicheng. (Jicheng IV, p. 32, n. 126; p. 203, n. 116)
  • 雖有共之璧以先四馬: The manuscripts agree, as does the Peking University manuscript. The received texts have 拱璧 "a disc clasped in both arms" without 之; since all the excavated texts have 之, the Jicheng suspects that 共 is a proper name, 共璧 being comparable to 和璧 "the disc of Bian He" or 隨珠 "the pearl of Sui". Wang Bi reads 雖有拱璧以先駟馬. MS B's 共之, restorations in the 1980 edition, are read directly from the offset in the Jicheng. (Jicheng IV, p. 32, n. 127; p. 203, n. 117)
  • 不善坐而進此: Follows MS A; MS B has 若 for 善, and MS A's 善 should be read as 若 "like". Wang Bi reads 不如坐進此道; neither silk manuscript, nor the Peking University manuscript, has 道 after 此. (Jicheng IV, p. 32, n. 128)
  • 古之所以貴此者何也: Follows MS A. The 1980 edition restored MS B as 之所以貴此道者何也; from the offset the Jicheng confirms seven graphs, 之所以貴此 and 何也. The single graph between 此 and 何 is unclear in the offset: it does not look like MS A's 者 and may be 道, but this cannot be settled, so it is printed as □. Wang Bi reads 古之所以貴此道者何. (Jicheng IV, p. 32, n. 128; p. 203, n. 118)
  • 不胃求以得: The manuscripts agree; 胃 is read as 謂 "to say". The original editors' note records 曰 in Yan Zun and Wang Bi and 不日以求得 in Heshang Gong; the Peking University manuscript also has 曰. Wang Bi reads 不曰以求得, with 求 and 以 transposed. (Jicheng IV, p. 203, n. 119)
  • 有罪以免輿: Follows MS A; MS B writes 與 for 輿, and both are read as the final particle 歟. Wang Bi has 有罪以免邪.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

The Dao is to the ten thousand things what the downhill is to water: the hundred streams going home to the hollow. The chapter's first line: the silk text has 萬物之注, "where the ten thousand things pour"; the Wang Bi recension has 奧, "the inner corner." 注 is flow reaching everything; 奧 is a thing stored away and hard to know. 奧 makes the Dao a hidden mystery; 注 makes it the moving water of rivers and seas. The silk text's sense is far the stronger: Laozi's Dao is no esoteric doctrine but the operation of the self-so: the place all things stream toward, which nothing can dam. Those who read Wang Bi and take the Dao for an arcanum are misled by the commentator, not by the classic.

The Dao: the good man treasures it, and the not-good man treasures it too. The good man holds the Dao as treasure; the man not good also knows to prize it. The Dao's covering is universal, indifferent to a man's worth or want of it. Confucius, speaking of the merely good man, says he "does not tread in the footprints, nor does he enter the room": well endowed, yet never going deep. Laozi draws no such line: one body, one kindness. Confucius is strict in demand and deep in requirement; Laozi is wide in embrace and forgiving in expectation: here the two houses part, and what they seek comes in the end to one.

"Fine words can be traded in the market; honored conduct can grace other men": both silk manuscripts (and the Peking University slips) write 賀, "to grace with congratulation," where Wang Bi writes 加, "to bestow upon." The first editors read 賀 as 加, after the received text, and the Jicheng follows them; Yaomiao ventures that 賀 may be read as it stands. To congratulate is a courtesy between equals, near the Daoist's harmony; to bestow is a favor from above, near the Confucian's charity. The silk text's graph better fits the equality of Laozi's Dao. If even words can fetch a price and conduct can be offered in congratulation, then in all-under-heaven there is no discardable man.

"Enthrone the Son of Heaven, install the three ministers": the silk text says 三卿, Wang Bi 三公: minister is the greater office, duke the loftier title; the silk text stays near the practical, Wang Bi near the nominal. The jade disc borne ahead of the four-horse team is the costliest of ceremonies — and not so good as sitting still and advancing this (不善坐而進此, 善 read as 若; Wang Bi: 不如坐進此道): the inward substance against the outward show. Discs and horses make a rite; the Dao makes the root. What makes the Son of Heaven the Son of Heaven is his getting of the Dao, not his receiving of jade.

"Why did the ancients prize this? Did they not say: seek, and by it you find; be guilty, and by it you are spared." The effect of getting the Dao: the seeker finds, the guilty goes free. Not that the Dao trades in profit: when the Dao runs through, inside and outside both run through: within, one finds without seeking; without, one is pardoned though guilty. Get the One, and all-under-heaven comes straight — is that not this? Hence: the prized of all-under-heaven. All-under-heaven prizes the Dao not for its strangeness but for its constancy; not for what it is outside but for what it is within; not because it is the corner, but because it is the pouring-place.
