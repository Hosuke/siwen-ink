The Book of Dao · Chapter Nineteen · Unhewn Wood and Plain Silk

English Translation

Cut off sageliness, cast out knowing (絕聖棄知), and the people profit a hundredfold.

Cut off humaneness (仁 ren), cast out rightness (義 yi), and the people return to filial love and nurturing (孝茲).

Cut off cleverness, cast out profit, and there are no more thieves.

These three sayings, as formulations, are not enough, so let them have that to which they belong:

Wear plain silk, hold unhewn wood (見素抱樸). Less of self, fewer desires. Cut off learning — and no sorrow (絕學无憂).

Commentary

Yaomiao says: the point of this chapter, for the Daoist, is returning to the root; for the way of rule, non-doing. The silk text writes 絕聖棄知, "cut off sageliness, cast out knowing," the same as the Wang Bi recension. Cut them, the machine-heart leaves, and the people receive the bounty. Laozi's intent is dropping the burden of cleverness, not demolishing the sage's De.

The second line: 絕仁棄義, "cut off humaneness, cast out rightness" — silk and Wang Bi alike. Its meaning: cut off the enforced names of humaneness and rightness, and the people of themselves return to their native filial love and nurturing. 孝茲 is 孝慈 — out of nature's own endowment, not a name humaneness and rightness could enforce. Filial love and nurturing come out of the native nature — just this. Humaneness and rightness cut, the root-heart returns of itself — does filial love wait for prizes to come into being? And this runs in one thread with the last chapter's "the great Dao disused — whence humaneness and rightness?"

"Cut off cleverness, cast out profit, and there are no more thieves": cleverness is the ingenious gadget, profit the itch for goods — the two sources of theft. Cut them and thievery has nowhere to grow: plain enough. But then "these three sayings, as formulations, are not enough": the three cuttings are all demolition, nothing built. Demolish without building, and there is nothing to come home to, so let them have that to which they belong. Wear plain silk, hold unhewn wood, to set the outside straight; less of self, fewer desires, to keep the inside whole — the work of leaving that and taking this. And "cut off learning — and no sorrow": these four graphs stand, in the Wang Bi text, at the next chapter's head — but by sense they belong at this chapter's close. The learning is the learning of the age's scholars; the sorrow is the distinguishing heart. Cut it away, and one is one with the Dao — sitting above, and the people feel no weight: what then is there to sorrow over?

"Working at the Dao, diminish daily" — this chapter is the daily-diminishing's working manual. Sageliness and knowing, humaneness and rightness, cleverness and profit, the learning of the age — all are contrived ornament, nothing native. Wearing plain silk, holding unhewn wood, less of self, fewer desires, cutting off learning without sorrow — these are the way back to plainness and the unhewn. Ah — in the rule of the highest De's non-doing, above, the name of exalting worthies is not practiced; below, the arts of cleverness are not pursued; the hundred clans transform of themselves and do not even know it: the utmost of governance. Let the wind of exalting worthies blaze and the arts of cleverness flourish, and the people contend for name and rank while villainy sprouts — can we not watch the mirror?
