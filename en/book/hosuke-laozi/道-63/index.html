The Book of Dao · Chapter Nineteen · Unhewn Wood and Plain Silk

English Translation

Cut off sageliness, cast out knowing (絕聲棄知 — 聲 read as 聖), and the people profit a hundredfold.

Cut off humaneness (仁 ren), cast out rightness (義 yi), and the people return to filial devotion and nurturing love (畜茲, read as 孝慈).

Cut off cleverness, cast out profit, and there are no more thieves.

These three sayings, as formulations, are not enough, so let them have that to which they belong:

Wear plain silk, hold unhewn wood (見素抱樸). Less of self (厶, read as 私), fewer desires.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

From 絕聲棄知 to 此三言也以為文未足 the text follows Manuscript A (MS A); from 故令之有所屬見素抱樸 onward MS A is damaged and the text is supplied from Manuscript B (MS B). Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 絕聲棄知: Follows MS A, whose 聲 is read as 聖 "sage" and 知 as 智 "knowing". MS B writes 聖 as 𦔻 (耳 beside 口), likewise read as 聖. Wang Bi has 絕聖棄智. (Jicheng IV, pp. 41, 206)
  • 民利百負: Follows MS A, with 負 read as 倍 "-fold"; MS B reads 而民利百倍, opening with 而. Wang Bi has 民利百倍.
  • 民復畜茲: Follows MS A; the Jicheng reads 畜茲 as 孝慈 "filial devotion and nurturing love". MS B reads 而民復孝茲, again with 而. Wang Bi has 民復孝慈.
  • 此三言也以為文未足: The manuscripts agree; Wang Bi has 此三者以為文不足, with 者 for 言也 and 不 for 未.
  • 故令之有所屬見素抱樸: In MS A everything after 抱 is lost; 樸 is supplied from MS B. Wang Bi has 故令有所屬：見素抱樸, without 之.
  • 少厶而寡欲: Lost entirely in MS A and taken from MS B. The 1980 edition transcribed MS B's 厶 directly as 私; the Jicheng keeps the original 厶, read as 私 "self". Wang Bi has 少私寡欲, without 而. (Jicheng IV, p. 209, n. 28)
  • 絕學无憂: In both manuscripts these words run straight on into 唯與呵 with no chapter mark. MS A has lost 絕學無 and keeps only 憂; MS B is intact. Following the Wang Bi chapter division, the line is placed at the head of the next chapter (silk ch. 64).

Commentary

The point of this chapter, for the Daoist, is returning to the root; for the way of rule, non-doing. Manuscript A writes 絕聲棄知, manuscript B 絶𦔻棄知 — manuscript A regularly writes the sage, 聖人, as 聲人, and manuscript B always writes 聖 as 𦔻; both are read as 聖 — "cut off sageliness, cast out knowing," the same sense as the Wang Bi recension's 絕聖棄智. Cut them, the machine-heart leaves, and the people receive the bounty. Laozi's intent is dropping the burden of cleverness, not demolishing the sage's De.

The second line: 絕仁棄義, "cut off humaneness, cast out rightness" — silk and Wang Bi alike. Its meaning: cut off the enforced names of humaneness and rightness, and the people of themselves return to their native filial devotion and nurturing love. In 民復孝茲 (so manuscript B; manuscript A writes 畜茲), 孝茲 is 孝慈 — out of nature's own endowment, not a name humaneness and rightness could enforce. Filial devotion and nurturing love come out of the native nature — just this. Humaneness and rightness cut, the root-heart returns of itself — do filial devotion and nurturing love wait for prizes to come into being? And this runs in one thread with the last chapter's "When the great Dao falls into disuse — thereupon there is humaneness and rightness."

"Cut off cleverness, cast out profit, and there are no more thieves": cleverness is the ingenious gadget, profit the itch for goods — the two sources of theft. Cut them and thievery has nowhere to grow: plain enough. But then "these three sayings, as formulations, are not yet enough" (文未足; Wang Bi writes 文不足): the three cuttings are all demolition, nothing built. Demolish without building, and there is nothing to come home to, so let them have that to which they belong. Wear plain silk, hold unhewn wood, to set the outside straight; less of self, fewer desires, to keep the inside whole — the work of leaving that and taking this. And "cut off learning — and no sorrow": these four graphs stand, in the Wang Bi recension, at the next chapter's head. Manuscripts A and B alike run them straight on into the 唯與呵 that follows, with no chapter mark, and the collation notes follow Wang Bi's chapter order in assigning them to the next chapter. Yet weighed by sense, these four graphs carry straight on from "wear plain silk, hold unhewn wood, less of self, fewer desires" (見素抱樸，少厶而寡欲), one thread with the three cuttings — the reader may well read them together with this chapter. The learning is the learning of the age's scholars; the sorrow is the distinguishing heart. Cut it away, and one is one with the Dao — sitting above, and the people feel no weight: what then is there to sorrow over?

"Working at the Dao, diminish daily" — this chapter is the daily-diminishing's working manual. Sageliness and knowing, humaneness and rightness, cleverness and profit, the learning of the age — all are contrived ornament, nothing native. Wearing plain silk, holding unhewn wood, less of self, fewer desires, cutting off learning without sorrow — these are the way back to plainness and the unhewn. Ah — in the rule of the highest De's non-doing, above, the name of exalting worthies is not practiced; below, the arts of cleverness are not pursued; the hundred clans transform of themselves and do not even know it: the utmost of governance. Let the wind of exalting worthies blaze and the arts of cleverness flourish, and the people contend for name and rank while villainy sprouts — can we not watch the mirror?
