The Book of De · Chapter Forty · Prizing Life

English Translation

The people hunger because so much of their food is taken in tax — hence they hunger.

The hundred clans fall into disorder because those above act with an end in view (有以為) — hence the disorder.

The people take death lightly because life is sought too richly — hence they take death lightly.

Only those who make no project of living (无以生為) — they are worthy, and they truly prize life (是賢貴生).

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

Manuscript A (MS A) is complete in this chapter, and the silk text follows it throughout. Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 人之飢也: Both manuscripts agree; MS A opens with a chapter-division dot. Wang Bi reads 民之饑.
  • 以亓取食𨓚之多也: Follows MS A; MS B reads 以亓取食𨁑之多, without 也. MS A's 𨓚 has 辵 with 兑, MS B's 𨁑 has 足 with 兑: two structures of the same word, both read as 稅 "tax". Wang Bi reads 以其上食稅之多, with 上 "those above" but without 取 "to take".
  • 是以飢: Both manuscripts agree; Wang Bi reads 是以饑.
  • 百姓之不治也以亓上有以為也: Follows MS A; the final 也 is read directly by the Jicheng. MS B writes 生 for 姓 and has 之 after 上. Wang Bi reads 民之難治，以其上之有為.
  • 是以不治: Follows MS A; in MS B the first graph is lost. Wang Bi reads 是以難治.
  • 民之巠死 / 是以巠死: Follows MS A; MS B writes 輕 for both instances of 巠, which is read as 輕 "light". MS B also has 也 after 民之輕死.
  • 以亓求生之厚也: Both manuscripts agree; Wang Bi reads 以其上求生之厚, with an added 上.
  • 夫唯无以生為者 / 是賢貴生: Both manuscripts agree; Wang Bi reads 夫唯無以生為者，是賢於貴生, with an added 於 "than".

Commentary

This chapter sets out three failings in turn and returns them to non-doing — the point is plain. Men's hunger: too much is taken in food-tax (𨓚, read as 稅). The hundred clans' disorder: those above them do too much with an end in view. The people's taking death lightly (巠死): they seek too lavish a life, thwarting their own life. In the silk text the first and last lines both lack 上, "those above" (Wang Bi has it in both); only the middle line speaks of 亓上, "those above them." Taking too much in food-tax comes, to be sure, from those above; but seeking too lavish a life is found above and below alike — the people too make much of their own lives, and so make light of their own deaths. Taking much runs counter to husbandry — husbandry being the cherishing of essence and spirit, the gathering-in of cleverness, the refusal to waste; now those above do the reverse, and so the people are straitened. For those above to act with an end in view is the opposite of "working at the Dao, diminish daily": when doing heaps up, the governed fall into disorder — such is the grain of things. As for seeking too lavish a life, the Huainanzi's "Tai Zu Xun" says, "Fattening the flesh, filling the belly, feeding the appetites — these are the lesser part of nourishing life." Seeking too much, the appetites know no measure; crossing nature and going against pattern, it harms the very life — men do not set out to take death lightly; they come to take it lightly by their own doing.

The silk text's 是以不治, "hence the disorder," against the received texts' 是以難治, "hence they are hard to govern." Yaomiao ventures the silk text is spare and complete: 不治 already holds the sense of hard-to-govern; no graph need be changed — changing it would tilt the meaning.

The last line: 夫唯无以生為者，是賢貴生 — the silk text lacks the 於 of the received "worthier than prizing life." Yaomiao holds the silk reading deeper and rounder. In his reading, 賢貴生 means that those who make no project of living can truly prize life. "Worthier than prizing life" divides the two and ranks making no project of living above prizing life. To turn that comparison into opposition is to miss their wholeness. The way of non-doing prizes wholeness, not distinction: making no project of living and prizing life are not two — only where they are one undivided thing is life truly prized.

So what makes those above harm the people — taking much, doing much, seeking too lavish a life — is in every case the excess of acting with an end in view. The sage-king's way: diminish and diminish again, down to non-doing — taking little, doing nothing contrived, not prizing his own life. This is called the turn-back. And non-doing is not doing nothing: it is not laying one's private intent on things. Nothing laid on, and the hundred clans fulfill themselves, the ten thousand things transform of themselves — and all-under-heaven is at peace.
