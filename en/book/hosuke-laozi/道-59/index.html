The Book of Dao · Chapter Fifteen · Never Full

English Translation

Of old, those good at working the Dao were minute and subtle, darkly far-reaching (微眇玄達) — deep beyond marking. Just because they are beyond marking, I strain to sketch their look:

Cautious, ah (與呵) — as if crossing a winter stream. Hesitant, ah — as if fearing the neighbors on four sides. Solemn, ah — like a guest. Yielding, ah — like ice about to melt. Unshaped, ah — like unhewn wood. Murky, ah — like turbid water. Dignified, ah — like one bathing clean (浴).

Turbid — yet in its nature it slowly clears. At rest — yet once stirred, it slowly comes alive.

He who keeps this Dao does not desire fullness. Only because he does not desire fullness can he stay worn (敝) — and never become complete.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

The Dao cannot be gotten by form or name, cannot be spied out by cleverness. Those of old who were good at working the Dao had a De minute and subtle, darkly far-reaching — deep, far, beyond marking. The silk text's 微眇玄達 against Wang Bi's 微妙玄通: one graph apart, the depths differ. 達 is going through and arriving — straighter than 通, "passing": there is nowhere the Dao does not reach, and reaching is the Dao's own end.

Being beyond marking, I strain to sketch their look. Cautious, as if crossing a winter stream; hesitant, as if fearing the neighbors on four sides; solemn, like a guest; yielding, like ice about to melt; unshaped, like unhewn wood; murky, like turbid water; dignified, like one bathing clean. Seven faces, all showing a De that bends low and gathers inward, never showing itself — the point being that the man of the Dao takes up residence in no single shape.

The chapter's two great variants come down to these. First, 濁而情之余清 against Wang Bi's 孰能濁以靜之徐清. Wang Bi's is a question — roundabout in wording, deep in meaning; the silk text states the effect flatly — plain in wording, true in meaning. 情 is the self-so of things; 靜 is the work of stilling. Turbid, yet able to be stilled: by its nature it slowly clears — the effect of light born inside the empty. The Doctrine of the Mean: "reach centered harmony, and heaven and earth take their places, the ten thousand things are nourished" — the same law.

Second, 能敝而不成 against 蔽不新成 — one graph apart, and the senses are a sky apart. 敝 is worn, frayed, the self-so of decline; 不成 is never completing. "Diminish and diminish again, down to non-doing": diminished to where nothing is left to diminish — that is 敝 at its utmost, and it is no word of decline but the real ground of the highest De's non-doing. Only because he does not desire fullness can he "reach utmost emptiness, keep the natures and their traces" — can stay worn and never complete. As for those hustling after the new among names and ranks: they do not know that fullness cannot last and the new cannot be leaned on. The world gallops and does not know where to stop — and in the end the full invite loss, and the new wears out fast. Only those of old, good at working the Dao, hold unhewn wood and keep the chaos whole, worn to the end of their days and never complete — this is the gate of the Dao.
