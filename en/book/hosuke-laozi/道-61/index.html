The Book of Dao · Chapter Seventeen · Barely Known to Be There

English Translation

The highest kind: those below merely know he is there (下知有之). The next kind: they love him and sing his praises. The next: they fear him. The lowest: they despise him.

When good faith is not enough, mistrust follows (案有不信). Hesitant, ah (猶呵) — how he prizes his words.

The work completed, the thing done — and the hundred clans say: we are self-so (我自然).

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

This chapter sets out the four climates of governance, and takes non-doing self-so as the ancestor. The highest kind of rule lies in non-doing. The lord dwells in non-doing, and the people merely know there is a lord, knowing nothing of government — why? Because the one above is calm, bland, empty, and silent: no words spoken, and the transformation completes itself; no decrees issued, and all-under-heaven orders itself. The people hum in their joy, unaware that Yao and Shun are even their lords — the effect of the hundred clans' self-so (自然). "The highest kind: those below merely know he is there" — nothing added, nothing taken away, nothing the people can put a name to: the very head of the unspoiled wind.

The next kind already has doings. Then it falls by grades: they love him and sing his praises; they fear him; they despise him. From love down to resentment, from fear down to contempt — the decline of the way of rule. The highest kind of rule: the work completed, the thing done, and the hundred clans all say, we are so of ourselves. Good faith and wordlessness are the chapter's other crux: when good faith above runs short, mistrust below follows — principle answering like an echo. The sage knows this, and so, slow and hesitant, he weighs his words. Unhurried, weighing his words, working and never tiring — this shows that weighing words is not saying nothing: it is care.

The work completed, the thing done, and the hundred clans say "we are self-so": the highest De does not hold to De, the work is done and not dwelt in, so the people, knowing nothing of the merit above, attribute it to their own self-so. Mencius: "the people move toward the good daily, and do not know who makes them so" — this. The sage patterns on water, benefiting the ten thousand things and keeping still: without desires, he can complete the rule of non-doing, and the people never know how it was done.

The silk text and Wang Bi differ in small words, not in the great point. The silk text's 案 is Wang Bi's 焉, both particles; 猶呵 and 悠兮 are both sighs of admiration, the sound shifted over the ages. And the silk text's 成功遂事 against Wang Bi's 功成事遂 — the order of words, one sense. The highest point never changed with the letters. The Dao's not walking the world comes of the many who have doings; rule's losing of De comes of those who cannot weigh their words. Let the student grasp this, and the rule of non-doing can be seen again today.
