The Book of Dao · Chapter Seventeen · Barely Known to Be There

English Translation

The highest kind: those below merely know he is there (下知有之). The next kind: they love him and sing his praises. The next: they fear him. The lowest: they despise him.

When good faith is not enough, mistrust follows (案有不信). Hesitant, ah (猷呵) — how he prizes his words.

The work completed, the thing done — and the hundred clans say: we are self-so (我自然).

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

Manuscript A (MS A) is largely intact in this chapter; only the first two graphs of 猷呵亓貴言也 are lost and supplied from Manuscript B (MS B). Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 大上下知有之: Follows MS A; the Jicheng reads 大 as 太 "highest". MS B reads 大上下知又□, with 之 lost. MS B's 大 was bracketed as a restoration in the 1980 edition; the Jicheng, finding its upper strokes still legible, reads it directly. The surface at 又 is damaged, but Gao Ming's enlarged photograph shows no dot above it and the 1980 edition also reads 又, which the Jicheng takes as 有. Wang Bi has 太上，下知有之. (Jicheng IV, p. 209, n. 27)
  • 其次親譽之: Follows MS A; in MS B 亓 is read directly by the Jicheng and 次 is lost. MS A writes 其 and MS B 亓 throughout. Wang Bi has 其次，親而譽之, with an extra 而.
  • 其次畏之 / 其下母之: The manuscripts agree; 母 is read as 侮 "to despise". Wang Bi has 其次 three times, 其次，畏之；其次，侮之, where the silk text has 其下 "the lowest" for the last.
  • 信不足 / 案有不信: Follows MS A; MS B writes 安 for 案. Wang Bi has 信不足焉，有不信焉.
  • 猷呵亓貴言也: Follows MS B, whose 猷 is read as 猶 "hesitant"; MS A reads □□其貴言也, and the Jicheng restores the two lost graphs as 猶呵. Wang Bi has 悠兮其貴言, without 也.
  • 成功遂事: The manuscripts agree; Wang Bi has 功成事遂.
  • 而百省胃我自然: Follows MS A, whose 省 the Jicheng reads as 姓 "clan"; MS B reads 而百姓胃我自然. Wang Bi has 百姓皆謂我自然, without 而 but with 皆 "all".

Commentary

This chapter sets out the four climates of governance, and takes non-doing self-so as the ancestor. The highest kind of rule lies in non-doing. The lord dwells in non-doing, and the people merely know there is a lord, knowing nothing of government — why? Because the one above is calm, bland, empty, and silent: no words spoken, and the transformation completes itself; no decrees issued, and all-under-heaven orders itself. The people hum in their joy, unaware that Yao and Shun are even their lords — the effect of the hundred clans' self-so (自然). "The highest kind: those below merely know he is there" — nothing added, nothing taken away, nothing the people can put a name to. This is called the very head of the unspoiled wind.

The next kind already has doings. Then it falls, grade by grade, into the four kinds: they love him and sing his praises; they fear him; they despise him. From love down to resentment, from fear down to contempt — the decline of the way of rule. The highest kind of rule: the work completed, the thing done, and the hundred clans all say, we are self-so. Good faith and wordlessness are the chapter's other crux: when good faith above runs short, mistrust below follows — principle answering like an echo. The sage knows this, and so, slow and hesitant, he weighs his words. Unhurried, weighing his words, working and never tiring — this shows that weighing words is not saying nothing: it is care.

The work completed, the thing done, and the hundred clans say "we are self-so": the highest De does not hold to De, the work is done and not dwelt in, so the people, knowing nothing of the merit above, attribute it to their own self-so. Mencius: "the people move toward the good daily, and do not know who makes them so" — this. The sage patterns on water, benefiting the ten thousand things and keeping still: without desires, he can complete the rule of non-doing, and the people never know how it was done.

The silk text and Wang Bi differ in small words, not in the great point. The silk text's 案 is Wang Bi's 焉 — near in sound and one in sense, meaning "thereupon" (manuscript B writes 安). The original editors' note says the received texts mistook 焉 for a sentence-final particle, attached it to the line before, and so added a second 焉 to the line after. 猷呵 and 悠兮 are both sighs of admiration, the sound shifted over the ages. And the silk text's 成功遂事 against Wang Bi's 功成事遂 — the order of words, one sense. The highest point never changed with the letters. The Dao's not walking the world comes of the many who have doings; rule's losing of De comes of those who cannot weigh their words. Let the student grasp this, and the rule of non-doing can be seen again today.
