The Book of De · Chapter Ten · Knowing All-under-Heaven

English Translation

Without stepping out the door, one knows all-under-heaven. Without peering through the window, one knows the Way of Heaven.

The farther one goes out, the less one knows.

Hence the sage knows without traveling, names without seeing — and, refusing to do (弗為), completes.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

The point of this chapter is the essential of the sage's beholding of the Dao: it does not lean on outward galloping. Door and window are the openings of going out and coming in; ear and eye are the passes between inner and outer. When ear and eye are spent on sound and color, and the spirit is worn out on outward watching, there is no master within — and the very means of knowing is lost. "Without stepping out the door, one knows all-under-heaven" does not mean never walking, never seeing; it means not taking walking and seeing for the ability itself.

Compare the two texts. The silk text writes 亓出也彌遠，亓知也彌少; Wang Bi normalizes 亓 to 其: a small graph, a small shade. The silk text's 亓出 points at a man's own galloping into outer business. But the place to dwell is the close: silk manuscript B ends 弗為而成 (manuscript A has 不), where Wang Bi has 不為而成. 弗 is the graph of prohibition, heavier than 不. The sage does not merely happen not to do; he has resolved not to do. Passive not-doing and willed refusal are two different things; only the willed refusal is the practice of the Dao (道). This answers the teaching of putting oneself behind: because he does not contend, no one in all-under-heaven can contend with him.

The going out is not the body's going out; it is the heart's. When the heart gallops after things the spirit scatters, and as the spirit scatters, what is known shrinks by the day. Read this with "the student gains daily; the hearer of the Dao diminishes daily": the student piles up things, the man of the Dao pares down the heart; the piling grows more tangled the further out it goes, the paring clearer the further home it returns. The Great Commentary on the Changes says, "still and unmoving; stirred, it goes through": the true image of the sage's knowing-without-traveling. No uncanny power is meant: when the heart is one with the Dao, nothing fails to answer. The Zhuangzi's "sitting in forgetfulness" (it means forgetting oneself) says the same: forget yourself and the Dao gathers; the Dao gathered, nothing is unknown.

Ah — seeking the Dao is not amassing knowledge but stopping it; knowing the Dao is not far travel but near beholding. The Dao is near, and men seek it far; the matter is easy, and men hunt it in the difficult — the standing disease of students old and new, and the reason Laozi warns again and again. The sage sees this through, empties himself, and roams the world — and the affairs of all-under-heaven, the patterns of the ten thousand things, are known through and through.
