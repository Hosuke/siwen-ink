The Book of De · Chapter Ten · Knowing All-under-Heaven

English Translation

Without stepping out the door, one knows all-under-heaven. Without peering through the window, one knows the Way of Heaven.

The farther one goes out, the less one knows.

Hence the sage knows without traveling, names without seeing — and, refusing to do (弗為), completes.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

The first six lines of this chapter follow Manuscript A (MS A); the second half is damaged in both manuscripts, and 亓知𥸀□, □□而名 and 弗為而成 follow Manuscript B (MS B). Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 不出於戶 / 以知天下: Both manuscripts agree; MS B's 以, bracketed as a restoration, is read directly in the Jicheng from the mirror-image offset on the backing silk. Wang Bi reads 不出戶，知天下. (Jicheng IV, p. 200, n. 37)
  • 不規於牖 / 以知天道: Follows MS A; 規 is read as 窺 "to peer". In MS A the right side of 規 is written 頁, and 牖 has a form that resists exact transcription; the Jicheng nonetheless prints 規 and 牖. MS B writes 窺 as a graph with 見 as signific and 圭 as phonetic, and its 牖 is lost. Wang Bi reads 不闚牖，見天道. (Jicheng IV, p. 16, n. 49)
  • 亓出也彌遠: Follows MS A, which alone among the witnesses has 也 after 出. MS B reads 亓出𥸀遠者; the Peking University manuscript and the received texts lack 者, as MS A does. MS A writes 彌 with the 弓 component and 遠 with 彳; MS B writes 彌 as 𥸀, with the bamboo top. Wang Bi reads 其出彌遠. (Jicheng IV, p. 16, n. 50)
  • 亓知𥸀□: Follows MS B, whose last graph is lost; the 1980 edition restored 尠 from the Fu Yi text, and the Jicheng restores 少 "little", as for MS A. In MS A the three graphs after 亓 are lost. Wang Bi reads 其知彌少. (Jicheng IV, p. 200, n. 38)
  • □□□人弗□□□: Damaged in both. The 1980 edition restored 是以聖人不行而知; Chen Jian joined a fragment near the heads of lines 21–23 of MS A, whose line-21 portion bears 人弗, which the Jicheng reads directly, replacing the restored 人不 with 人弗. The line is wholly lost in MS B; its negative, restored as 不 in 1980, is restored as 弗 in the Jicheng after the Peking University manuscript. Wang Bi reads 是以聖人不行而知. (Jicheng IV, p. 16, n. 51; p. 200, n. 39)
  • □□而名: Follows MS B, whose first two graphs are lost. Traces of MS A's 而 survive and the Jicheng reads it directly; whether its negative was 不 or 弗 cannot be decided, so it is given as □; the final graph is restored as 名 after the original editors. Gao Ming restored 明 from the Han Feizi chapter "Yu Lao", which the Jicheng finds insufficiently grounded; the Peking University manuscript has 命. Wang Bi reads 不見而名. (Jicheng IV, p. 16, n. 51)
  • 弗為而成: Follows MS B. The original editors read the graph before 為 in MS A as 弗 on the model of MS B; the Jicheng finds that the surviving stroke cannot belong to 弗 and is the end of the right-falling stroke of 不, prints 不為而□, and removes a small, illegible fragment that had been mounted to its left without warrant. MS B and the Peking University manuscript read 弗為而成, the received texts 不為而成, and a few, including Heshang Gong, 無為. (Jicheng IV, p. 16, n. 52)

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

The point of this chapter is the essential of the sage's beholding of the Dao: it does not lean on outward galloping. Door and window are the openings of going out and coming in; ear and eye are the passes between inner and outer. When ear and eye are spent on sound and color, and the spirit is worn out on outward watching, there is no master within — and the very means of knowing is lost. "Without stepping out the door, one knows all-under-heaven" does not mean never walking, never seeing; it means not taking walking and seeing for the ability itself.

Compare the two texts. Silk manuscript A writes 亓出也彌遠; manuscript B, 亓出𥸀遠者，亓知𥸀□ (𥸀 is 彌), its last graph lost; Wang Bi normalizes 亓 to 其: a small graph, a small shade. The silk text's 亓出 points at a man's own galloping into outer business. But the place to dwell is the close: silk manuscript B ends 弗為而成 (manuscript A has 不為而□), where Wang Bi has 不為而成. 弗 is the graph of prohibition, heavier than 不. The sage does not merely happen not to do; he has resolved not to do. Passive not-doing and willed refusal are two different things; only the willed refusal is the practice of the Dao (道). This answers the teaching of putting oneself behind: because he does not contend, no one in all-under-heaven can contend with him.

The going out is not the body's going out; it is the heart's. When the heart gallops after things the spirit scatters, and as the spirit scatters, what is known shrinks by the day. Read this with "the student gains daily; the one who works at the Dao diminishes daily": the student piles up things, the man of the Dao pares down the heart; the piling grows more tangled the further out it goes, the paring clearer the further home it returns. The Great Commentary on the Changes says, "still and unmoving; stirred, it goes through": the true image of the sage's knowing-without-traveling. No uncanny power is meant: when the heart is one with the Dao, nothing fails to answer. The Zhuangzi's "sitting in forgetfulness" (it means forgetting oneself) says the same: forget yourself and the Dao gathers; the Dao gathered, nothing is unknown.

Ah — seeking the Dao is not amassing knowledge but stopping it; knowing the Dao is not far travel but near beholding. The Dao is near, and men seek it far; the matter is easy, and men hunt it in the difficult — the standing disease of students old and new, and the reason Laozi warns again and again. The sage sees this through, empties himself, and roams the world — and the affairs of all-under-heaven, the patterns of the ten thousand things, are known through and through.
