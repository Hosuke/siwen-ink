The Book of Dao · Chapter Twenty-eight · Constant De

English Translation

Know the male (雄), keep to the female (雌) — be the valley-stream of all-under-heaven. Be the valley-stream of all-under-heaven, and constant De does not leave. Constant De does not leave, and one returns to the newborn (嬰兒).

Know the white (白), keep to disgrace (辱) — be the bathing-water of all-under-heaven (浴). Be the bathing-water of all-under-heaven, and constant De is sufficient. De sufficient, and one returns to unhewn wood (樸).

Know the white, keep to the black (黑) — be the measure of all-under-heaven. Be the measure of all-under-heaven, and constant De does not err. Constant De does not err, and one returns to the limitless (无極).

Unhewn wood, split, becomes vessels; the sage, using it, becomes the chief of officers. Great fashioning makes no cuts (大制无割).

Commentary

Yaomiao says: this chapter wraps the sage's work of returning to the root in three pairs of knowing-and-keeping, and its crux is the subtle function of non-doing.

Male and female, white and disgrace, white and black — all are names that stand in pairs. The world knows the male as worth coveting and covets it; knows the white as shining and flaunts it. The sage is otherwise: he knows the male and keeps to the female; knows the white and keeps to disgrace, keeps to the black. Keeping means not leaving; not leaving is constant De entire.

The differences between silk text and Wang Bi must be watched. The silk text writes 知亓白守亓辱, "know the white, keep to disgrace," where Wang Bi has 知其榮守其辱, "know the glory, keep to the disgrace." 榮 is outer dazzle; 辱 is inner bearing. The silk text's 白 runs deeper: white is the unstained root-truth, bright and undyed; keeping to disgrace is darkening oneself on purpose, gladly taking soot. Knowing white while keeping to disgrace — holding the use of the dim at the root of the bright — fits the dialectical point most exactly. The text in the Heshang Gong commentary also reads "keep to the black, be the measure of all-under-heaven" — knowing white and keeping black, precisely by not shining oneself can one be the world's measure. The white is the root-heart's clear brightness, not a glory flashed outside; the disgrace is a gathered, humble self-veiling, not real filth. Knowing light and keeping soot, knowing brightness and keeping dark — this is how the sage's non-doing makes De whole.

The silk text reads 為天下浴, "be the bathing-water of all-under-heaven," where the Wang Bi recension reads 為天下谷, "be the valley of all-under-heaven." Here Yaomiao takes 浴 in the sense of washing: the sage knows white and keeps to disgrace as a bathing that cleanses his own De — not merely taking soot in. Washed, and then clear: this is the sense of "constant De is sufficient."

Three returns, three different homes. Returning to the newborn: the newborn is soft and supple, the original wholeness of De. Returning to unhewn wood: the wood never carved, going back to the chaos of the great beginning. Returning to the limitless: the limitless is the Dao's own substance — empty, vast, past measuring. All are the work of returning to the root and making the true whole.

It closes with "unhewn wood, split, becomes vessels; the sage, using it, becomes the chief of officers. Great fashioning makes no cuts." The Dao covers and holds the ten thousand there-ares as heaven and earth remain silent and the four seasons pass. 散 is dividing out; 割 is cutting apart. Not cutting is not slicing all-under-heaven up with the cleverness of contrivance. The Book of Changes says "no thought, no doing — still and unmoving" — the sage embodying this finds the wood split into vessels without the wholeness lost, uses it as chief of officers without his De being hurt. That is the great fashioning.

Keeping the black in deep silence, returning to the unhewn and making the true whole — the sage's way with all-under-heaven is simply: no cutting.
