The Book of Dao · Chapter Twenty-eight · Constant De

English Translation

Know the male (雄), keep to the female (雌) — be the valley-stream of all-under-heaven. Be the valley-stream of all-under-heaven, and constant De does not leave. Constant De does not leave, and one returns to the newborn (嬰兒).

Know the white (日, read as 白), keep to disgrace (辱) — be the bathing-water of all-under-heaven (浴). Be the bathing-water of all-under-heaven, and constant De is sufficient. Constant De sufficient, and one returns to unhewn wood (樸).

Know the white, keep to the black (黑) — be the measure of all-under-heaven. Be the measure of all-under-heaven, and constant De does not err. Constant De does not err, and one returns to the limitless (无極).

Unhewn wood, split, becomes vessels; the sage, using it, becomes the chief of officers. Great fashioning makes no cuts (大制无割).

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

The chapter largely follows Manuscript A (MS A); 恆德乃足 and 復歸於樸, and the closing lines 樸散則為器 and 𦔻人用則為官長, are damaged in MS A and supplied from Manuscript B (MS B). Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng). In the Dao section MS A writes 其, MS B 亓.

  • Order of the stanzas: Both manuscripts run 知其雄, then 知其白 (日) 守其辱, then 知其白守其黑, so that the "keep to disgrace" stanza precedes the "keep to the black" stanza. In Wang Bi the "black" stanza comes first and the "disgrace" stanza second, and it reads 知其榮，守其辱 "know the glory, keep to disgrace".
  • 為天下溪: Follows MS A; MS B writes 雞, read as 溪 "valley-stream". Wang Bi has 谿.
  • 恒德不雞: Follows MS A. MS A miswrites 離 "leave" as a graph that resembles 雞 without being 雞; the Jicheng traces its form and reads it as 離. MS B has 离. Wang Bi reads 常德不離. (Jicheng IV, p. 53, n. 111)
  • Repetition marks in MS A: In 恒德不雞 the scribe omitted the repetition mark after 德, and in 為天下浴 the one after 浴; nor is there a mark after either 恒. The Jicheng treats these as scribal omissions and supplies them in 〖﹦〗. The present text keeps the supplied marks so that the lines read as intended. (Jicheng IV, p. 53, nn. 111, 113)
  • 復歸嬰兒: Follows MS A, which lacks 於. MS B reads 復歸【於嬰】兒; its 歸 and 兒, bracketed as restorations in the 1980 edition, are read directly by the Jicheng from traces of their upper parts. Wang Bi has 復歸於嬰兒. (Jicheng IV, p. 210, n. 45)
  • 知其日: Follows MS A. The graph is 日, a flat frame with one inner stroke and no tick above, on both the color and the black-and-white plates; this is not the result of damage. Gao Ming's transcription records 日; the 1980 edition and the Jicheng read it directly as 白 "white". The present text keeps the original form, read as 白. MS B reads 【知】亓白, with 知 lost. Wang Bi has 知其榮.
  • 為天下浴: Follows MS A; both manuscripts write 浴, read as 谷 "valley". Above 浴 the MS B scribe first miswrote 恆, then circled it out. Wang Bi has 為天下谷. (Jicheng IV, p. 210, n. 46)
  • 恆德乃足 / 恆德乃足 / 復歸於樸: MS A preserves only 恒德乃; 足 and 復歸於樸 are lost and supplied from MS B.
  • 知其: Follows MS A, which omits 白 after 知其; MS B reads 知亓白, as do the received texts. (Jicheng IV, p. 53, n. 114)
  • 恒德不貣: Both manuscripts write 貣, which the Jicheng reads as 忒 "to err"; Wang Bi has 常德不忒.
  • 樸散則為器: MS A reads 楃散, 楃 being read as 樸 "unhewn wood"; the rest is lost and supplied from MS B.
  • 𦔻人用則為官長: In MS A 聖 is lost; the line follows MS B, which writes 𦔻 (耳 beside 口), read as 聖 "sage". Wang Bi reads 聖人用之，則為官長.
  • 夫大制无割: The manuscripts agree; Wang Bi has 故大制不割.

Commentary

This chapter wraps the sage's work of returning to the root in three pairs of knowing-and-keeping, and its crux is the subtle function of non-doing.

Male and female, white and disgrace, white and black — all are names that stand in pairs. The world knows the male as worth coveting and covets it; knows the white as shining and flaunts it. The sage is otherwise: he knows the male and keeps to the female; knows the white and keeps to disgrace, keeps to the black. Keeping means not leaving; not leaving is constant De entire.

The differences between silk text and Wang Bi must be watched. The silk text writes 知亓白守亓辱, "know the white, keep to disgrace" (so manuscript B, its 知 lost; manuscript A writes 知其日守其辱 — 日, "sun," for 白: the graph is intact, only without the top tick — and is read as 白), where Wang Bi has 知其榮守其辱, "know the glory, keep to the disgrace." 榮 is outer dazzle; 辱 is inner bearing. The silk text's 白 runs deeper: white is the unstained root-truth, bright and undyed; keeping to disgrace is darkening oneself on purpose, gladly taking soot. Knowing white while keeping to disgrace — holding the use of the dim at the root of the bright — fits the dialectical point most exactly. The text in the Heshang Gong commentary also reads "keep to the black, be the measure of all-under-heaven" — knowing white and keeping black, precisely by not shining oneself can one be the measure of all-under-heaven. The white is the root-heart's clear brightness, not a glory flashed outside; the disgrace is a gathered, humble self-veiling, not real filth. Knowing light and keeping soot, knowing brightness and keeping dark — this is how the sage's non-doing makes De whole.

The silk text reads 為天下浴, "be the bathing-water of all-under-heaven" (the 1980 editors read 浴 as 谷), where the Wang Bi recension reads 為天下谷, "be the valley of all-under-heaven." Here Yaomiao reads 浴 as written, taking it in the sense of washing: the sage knows white and keeps to disgrace as a bathing that cleanses his own De — not merely taking soot in. Washed, and then clear: this is the sense of "constant De is sufficient."

Three returns, three different homes. Returning to the newborn: the newborn is soft and supple, the original wholeness of De. Returning to unhewn wood: the wood never carved, going back to the chaos of the great beginning. Returning to the limitless: the limitless is the Dao's own substance — empty, vast, past measuring. All are the work of returning to the root and making the true whole.

It closes with "unhewn wood, split, becomes vessels; the sage, using it, becomes the chief of officers. Great fashioning makes no cuts." The Dao covers and holds the ten thousand there-ares as heaven and earth remain silent and the four seasons pass. 散 is dividing out; 割 is cutting apart. Not cutting is not slicing all-under-heaven up with the cleverness of contrivance. The Book of Changes says "no thought, no doing — still and unmoving" — the sage embodying this finds the wood split into vessels without the wholeness lost, uses it as chief of officers without his De being hurt. That is the great fashioning.

Keeping the black in deep silence, returning to the unhewn and making the true whole — the sage's way with all-under-heaven is simply: no cutting.
