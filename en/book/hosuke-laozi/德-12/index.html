The Book of De · Chapter Twelve · The Good of De

English Translation

The sage is constantly without heart of his own: he takes the hundred clans' heart as his heart.

To the good he is good; to the not-good he is also good […]: this is the good of De. The trustworthy he trusts; the untrustworthy he also trusts: this is the trust of De.

The sage, in all-under-heaven, draws his breath in and in (翕翕), and for all-under-heaven muddles his heart into one. The hundred clans are all bound to their ears and eyes; the sage […] them all (聖人皆□之: the graph is lost in both manuscripts; the 1980 editors restored 咳, "to laugh as an infant," which the Jicheng does not keep).

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

Manuscript A (MS A) is badly damaged in the first half of this chapter, which therefore largely follows Manuscript B (MS B); 善者善之 and everything from 聖人之在天下 on follow MS A, and lacunae common to both are kept. Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 𦔻人恆无心: Follows MS B. The line is wholly lost in MS A, where the original editors restored it from MS B. MS B's 𦔻, restored as 聖 in the 1980 edition, is read directly in the Jicheng from the mirror-image offset on the backing silk and taken as 聖 "sage". Wang Bi reads 聖人無常心 "the sage has no constant heart". (Jicheng IV, p. 18, n. 56; p. 200, n. 44)
  • 以百眚之心為心: Follows MS B; 眚 is read as 姓, "the hundred clans". In MS A the trace above 以 cannot belong to 心 and may be the remains of 乃, so the Jicheng inserts a lacuna between the restored 心 of the previous line and 以; MS A's 姓 is read directly from a newly joined fragment. Wang Bi reads 以百姓心為心. (Jicheng IV, p. 18, n. 57)
  • 善者善之: Follows MS A; MS B keeps only 善. Wang Bi reads 善者，吾善之; neither silk manuscript has 吾 "I" in this line or the three that follow.
  • 不善者亦善□: Follows MS A, whose last graph is lost; the whole line is lost in MS B.
  • 德善也: Follows MS B. The 1980 edition restored the graph before 善也 in MS B as 得 after the Fu Yi and other texts; a fragment stuck to the backing silk, bearing most of 德 and the right side of 善, belongs here, and the Jicheng reads 德. In MS A the graph is lost; it was probably 德, though 得 cannot be ruled out, and the Jicheng gives □. Wang Bi reads 德善. (Jicheng IV, p. 18, n. 58; p. 200, n. 45)
  • 信者信之 / 不信者亦信之: Follows MS B. MS A keeps 亦信, formerly bracketed as a restoration and now read directly from a newly joined small fragment. Wang Bi reads 信者，吾信之；不信者，吾亦信之. (Jicheng IV, p. 18, n. 58)
  • 德信也: Follows MS B. The 1980 edition glossed 德 as 得; the Jicheng, as a precaution, gives no gloss. MS A keeps 信也, with the graph above it given as □. Wang Bi reads 德信. (Jicheng IV, p. 18, n. 58; p. 200, n. 46)
  • 聖人之在天下翕翕焉: Follows MS A; the Jicheng reads 聖人 directly. MS A writes 翕 with a repetition mark, here written out; on the color plate the graph has 合 above 羽, with no heart radical, so the earlier transcription 愉 was wrong. MS B reads 𦔻人之在天下也欲﹦焉, with 也 and with 欲欲 for 翕翕. Wang Bi reads 聖人在天下歙歙.
  • 為天下渾心: Follows MS A; lost in MS B. Wang Bi reads 為天下渾其心.
  • 百姓皆屬耳目焉: Follows MS A. MS B reads □生皆注亓□□□, with 生 for 姓 and 注 "poured toward" for 屬 "fastened". Wang Bi reads 百姓皆注其耳目.
  • 聖人皆□之: Damaged in both. In MS A the Jicheng reads 皆 and 之 directly; of the graph between them only a very little ink at the left survives, which is not the 口 component and cannot be confirmed as 亥. The original editors read 咳 after the Fu Yi text; the Jicheng gives □, and the color plate does not bear out 咳. For MS B the 1980 edition restored 人皆咳之; the Jicheng reads 人, 皆 and 之 directly from the offset, the graph between being unclear there, and gives □. Wang Bi reads 聖人皆孩之. (Jicheng IV, p. 19, n. 60; p. 200, n. 47)

Commentary

"The sage is constantly without heart of his own; he takes the hundred clans' heart as his heart": the headline of the chapter, and the utmost rule of Laozi's politics.

"Constantly without heart" is no idiot blankness; it is the no-heart that empties the self to answer things. Empty of self, hence without private intent; without private intent, hence without partial feeling; without partial feeling — and the hearts of all-under-heaven come to him of themselves. This is what makes the sage a sage. The sage's heart is empty and calm, holding nothing fixed or necessary. But the silk text writes 恆无心, "constantly without heart," where Wang Bi has 無常心, "without a constant heart": the word order differs, and so does the sense. Constantly-without-heart: the heart's substance is forever empty, no moment not 无. Without-a-constant-heart: a heart with no fixed holding, which might yet shift. The first is final; the second falls short; the silk text plainly runs deeper. Read it against the Great Learning's "what is stored in the self, not extended to others": 恕 extends from self to others, but the sage dissolves even the self, and takes the hundred clans' heart directly as his own, with no remainder of doer and done-to.

To the good he is good, to the not-good he is also good: this is the good of De. The trustworthy he trusts, the untrustworthy he also trusts: this is the trust of De. Good and not-good are met as one; trust and no-trust are answered with sincerity. This is no promiscuous tolerance: it is the Dao showing itself. The sage does not grip the names "good" and "evil"; he watches only whether a man can come home. The one who returns is good; the one not yet returned is not despised — he is met like a naked babe and led, until he turns back to the Dao of himself. That is goodness at its utmost. Water benefits the ten thousand things and keeps still; the sage transforms the hundred clans and discards none: one gauge.

"The sage, in all-under-heaven, draws his breath in and in, and for all-under-heaven muddles his heart into one" (manuscript A; manuscript B writes 欲欲 for 翕翕, and Wang Bi 歙歙): 翕翕 is the look of breath gathered inward; 渾心 is making the hearts of all-under-heaven turn whole and one. The hundred clans are ten-thousandfold; the sage holds the One to steer them. Ears and eyes gallop daily; the sage muddles them back toward home. Manuscript A says 百姓皆屬耳目焉, with 屬, "fastened" — the clans' hearts tied up in the appetites of ear and eye. Manuscript B and Wang Bi write 注, "poured toward," a shallower sense. The silk text's graph carries more.

"The sage receives them all as 咳": manuscript A reads 聖人皆□之 — 皆 and 之 are read from their traces, but the graph between survives only as broken strokes; the 1980 editors restored it as 咳, and the 2014 Jicheng leaves it unread (manuscript B likewise has □人皆□之). If we follow the old reading, 咳 is the sound of an infant's laugh, motion before speech is formed; Wang Bi's 孩 is the babe's state, stillness before knowing. The sage looks on the people of all-under-heaven as each at the moment of its first birth — no division of worthy and unworthy, no blame for their sinking, only the glad hand drawing them out. Ah — the world's disease is having likes and hates; the sage is open and wholly impartial. The world's disease is drawing distinctions; the sage is good to the not-good and trusts the untrustworthy. The De of not contending is at its fullest here; the hundred clans come home of themselves, as the newborn to its mother — and what has he done?
