The Book of De · Chapter Twelve · The Good of De

English Translation

The sage is constantly without heart of his own: he takes the hundred clans' heart as his heart.

To the good he is good; to the not-good he is also good: this is the good of De. The trustworthy he trusts; the untrustworthy he also trusts: this is the trust of De.

The sage, in all-under-heaven, draws his breath in and in (歙歙), and for all-under-heaven muddles his heart into one. The hundred clans are all bound to their ears and eyes; the sage meets them all as infants at their first laugh (咳).

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

"The sage is constantly without heart of his own; he takes the hundred clans' heart as his heart": the headline of the chapter, and the utmost rule of Laozi's politics.

"Constantly without heart" is no idiot blankness; it is the no-heart that empties the self to answer things. Empty of self, hence without private intent; without private intent, hence without partial feeling; without partial feeling — and the hearts of all-under-heaven come to him of themselves. This is what makes the sage a sage. The sage's heart is empty and calm, holding nothing fixed or necessary. But the silk text writes 恆无心, "constantly without heart," where Wang Bi has 無常心, "without a constant heart": the word order differs, and so does the sense. Constantly-without-heart: the heart's substance is forever empty, no moment not 无. Without-a-constant-heart: a heart with no fixed holding, which might yet shift. The first is final; the second falls short; the silk text plainly runs deeper. Read it against the Great Learning's "what is stored in the self, not extended to others": 恕 extends from self to others, but the sage dissolves even the self, and takes the hundred clans' heart directly as his own, with no remainder of doer and done-to.

To the good he is good, to the not-good he is also good: this is the good of De. The trustworthy he trusts, the untrustworthy he also trusts: this is the trust of De. Good and not-good are met as one; trust and no-trust are answered with sincerity. This is no promiscuous tolerance: it is the Dao showing itself. The sage does not grip the names "good" and "evil"; he watches only whether a man can come home. The one who returns is good; the one not yet returned is not despised — he is met like a naked babe and led, until he turns back to the Dao of himself. That is goodness at its utmost. Water benefits the ten thousand things and keeps still; the sage transforms the hundred clans and discards none: one gauge.

"The sage, in all-under-heaven, draws his breath in and in, and for all-under-heaven muddles his heart into one": 歙歙 is the look of breath gathered inward; 渾心 is making the hearts of all-under-heaven turn whole and one. The hundred clans are ten-thousandfold; the sage holds the One to steer them. Ears and eyes gallop daily; the sage muddles them back toward home. Silk manuscript A says 百姓皆屬耳目焉: 屬, "fastened": the clans' hearts tied up in the appetites of ear and eye. Manuscript B and Wang Bi write 注, "poured toward," a shallower sense. The silk text's graph carries more.

"The sage receives them all as 咳": the silk text's 咳 is the sound of an infant's laugh, motion before speech is formed; Wang Bi's 孩 is the babe's state, stillness before knowing. The sage looks on the people of all-under-heaven as each at the moment of its first birth — no division of worthy and unworthy, no blame for their sinking, only the glad hand drawing them out. Ah — the world's disease is having likes and hates; the sage is open and wholly impartial. The world's disease is drawing distinctions; the sage is good to the not-good and trusts the untrustworthy. The De of not contending is at its fullest here; the hundred clans come home of themselves, as an infant to its mother — and what has he done?
