The Book of Dao · Chapter Twelve · For the Belly

English Translation

The five colors make men's eyes blind. Galloping afield for the winter game-offering (田臘) drives men's hearts mad. Hard-to-get goods make men's conduct stumble. The five flavors make men's mouths go dull. The five tones make men's ears deaf.

Hence the sage's governing: for the belly (為腹), not for the eye. He leaves that and takes this.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

Man is born between heaven and earth: eyes to see with, ears to hear with, mouth to taste with, heart to know with — all received with his qi, the self-so of things. But the world, bewitched by what it meets, chases things and never returns, and so loses its true root. Laozi grieves at this, and so counts out the harms of the five, to wake those who come after.

"The five colors make men's eyes blind" — the eyes do not blind themselves; colors block them. "The five tones make men's ears deaf" — the ears do not deafen themselves; tones daze them. "The five flavors make men's mouths go dull" — the mouth does not dull itself; flavors steal it away. "The chase of field and hunt drives men's hearts mad" — the heart does not madden itself; things sweep it off. "Hard-to-get goods make men's conduct stumble" — conduct does not stumble itself; goods weigh it down. In all five, excess displaces the native: not damage to the organ, but a blinding of taste, a distortion of hearing, a numbing of the palate. The Zhuangzi: "when the machine-heart lodges in the breast, pure wholeness is not complete" — this. Common men take one color for the eye, one sound for the ear, one flavor for the mouth, drown in them past all measure, and so are driven by things without ever knowing it.

The silk text uses 使, "make," where Wang Bi uses 令 — the depths show themselves. 使 is to drive: things can press men into service; 令 is to occasion: things stir men and men bewitch themselves. The driven reading runs deeper, the occasioned shallower — here the silk text wins. And the silk text writes 田臘 where the received texts have 田獵, "field hunting": 臘 is the cured meat of the winter hunt — the far chase brought home, cooked and offered, the affair grown large and long-drawn, all the more able to sweep the heart away. Galloping in the field chase, vying with creatures — that is hurrying your own fault home.

"Hence the sage's governing: for the belly, not for the eye. He leaves that and takes this." The belly is what the inside receives — empty and able to receive, full and not brimming; the eye is what the outside shows — chasing and never returning, dazzled and never returning. For-the-belly seeks real sufficiency; for-the-eye seeks the thrill of surfaces. The silk text carries the two graphs 之治, "governing," which the received texts drop — and the sense widens: "the sage is for the belly, not the eye" is only the work of self-rule; "the sage's governing" takes in the governing of the age as well — the sense grows large. The Doctrine of the Mean speaks of "the noble man's timeliness of the mean": timely-meant is not gripping one end — and the sage's for-the-belly-not-for-the-eye is the way of timely-mean itself: taking the real, dropping the flower; keeping unhewn wood, cutting the false. "Water benefits the ten thousand things and keeps still": the sage patterns on water — in dwelling, good at the ground and not vying for height; in heart, the deep pool and not competing for depth — is that not the sense of for-the-belly?

Looked at closely, Laozi's chapter is no mere call to abstinence — it is a call to measure at the root. The organ's use lies in its measure, not in its fullness; fullness only loses the organ's native power. Know where to stop, and you do not chase; not chasing, you are not bewitched; not bewitched, the belly fills of itself and the eye empties of itself. Emptying the heart, filling the belly — that is not plugging ears and eyes; it is returning to the root to keep the true whole. "The sage desires not-desiring": he desires what men do not desire, and does not desire what men desire — that is how the sage differs from the crowd. Ah — the world chases the tip and forgets the root, gallops far and loses what is near, never knowing that the five colors' dazzle is no match for one silence's wholeness, that the five tones' pleasure is no match for soundlessness's subtlety. This age takes stimulation for joy — sound, light, color, flavor all at their peak — and the heart grows emptier, the want grows bigger, the feeling grows duller. What harm could be greater? The sage is for the belly and not the eye — not merely to rein in desire: it is the crux of recovering nature itself.
