The Book of Dao · Chapter Seven · No Self

English Translation

Heaven is long, earth is lasting. That heaven and earth can be long and lasting is because they do not live for themselves — and so they live long.

Hence the sage bends his person low (芮其身) — and his person is in front; sets his person aside — and his person endures.

Is it not because he has no self (无私) that he can complete his self?

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

The chapter follows Manuscript A (MS A) throughout, except that 不以亓无私輿, which lacks one graph in MS A, follows Manuscript B (MS B). Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 天地之所以能長且久者: Follows MS A; MS B agrees. MS A's 長 is read directly by the Jicheng. Wang Bi has 天地所以能長且久者, without 之.
  • 以其不自生也: Follows MS A; MS B reads 以亓不自生也. In the Dao section MS A writes 其, MS B 亓. Wang Bi lacks 也.
  • 是以聲人芮其身而身先: Follows MS A, whose 芮, with 內 as phonetic, the Jicheng reads as 退 "to draw back", as also in 功述身芮 (MS A, col. 107). MS B reads 退亓身, writing 退 as 𢓴, which the Jicheng explains as an abbreviation of 復, an archaic form of 退; it is given here as 退. MS A writes 聲人, MS B 𦔻人; both are read as 聖人 "the sage". Wang Bi has 是以聖人後其身而身先; both silk manuscripts carry the sense "draw back", whereas the Peking University manuscript and the received texts all have 後 "put behind". (Jicheng IV, p. 46, n. 28)
  • 外其身而身存: Follows MS A. In MS B this line is preceded by the six interpolated graphs 外亓身而身先, which the 1980 editors marked as an interpolation. The Jicheng keeps their marking, but observes that the scribe may have slipped back, after the second 身, to the 先 of the preceding line, so that the interpolation is properly 先外亓身而身, and the first 外 inside the marks is not itself interpolated. (Jicheng IV, p. 208, n. 8)
  • 不以亓无私輿: Follows MS B. MS A reads 不以其无□輿, with 私 lost. The Jicheng reads 輿 as the question particle 歟. Wang Bi has 非以其無私邪.
  • 故能成其私: Follows MS A; MS B reads 故能成亓私.

Commentary

The crux of this chapter is the two graphs 无私, "no self," and its hinge is the phrase 不自生, "not living for themselves." Heaven and earth are the greatest of things; the sage is the pattern of the myriad people — and that heaven and earth last, that the sage completes his self, both root in one "not." Not self-living, not owning his person, not pursuing his private ends: and so no loss, no blight, nothing uncompleted.

Heaven and earth give birth to the ten thousand things: the Dao is that by which things are so, and things owe it their lives — yet heaven and earth never claim the merit. Later the book says "water benefits the ten thousand things and keeps still": the heart of heaven and earth is like water's nature. Not contending, hence without fault — not-contending is the Dao's use, and not-self-living is the substance of not-contending. The world knows only that living is lovely; it does not know the harm of self-living. For self-living requires a self; a self makes fences; fences make contention; contention makes things multiply their troubles and dwindle by the day. The Changes: "no level that does not tilt" — this.

Now the silk text against Wang Bi: 芮其身, "bends his person back," against 後其身, "puts his person behind." 芮 the Jicheng reads as 退, "to draw back," and manuscript B in fact writes 退; one may also keep its sense of curving. To bend the body below others, to yield the first place — the sense runs deeper than 後. Putting one's person behind is mere order of precedence, first others then self; but 芮 is to curl the whole person down to carry all-under-heaven — not just standing back, but bending oneself to give way. "He puts his person behind and his person is in front" still speaks in terms of rank; the sense of 芮 speaks of heart and posture together — one layer deeper. Yet Wang Bi's 後 has an unhurried breath worth savoring. In sum: retreating is not retreating — retreat as advance is the hinge of the Dao.

Again: the silk text's 不以亓无私輿 against Wang Bi's 非以其無私邪 — both are questions; 輿 (read as 歟) and 邪 alike are the question's breath. The two differ only in diction — 不以亓 against 非以其 — a hair of style, one sense. The plainer Laozi spells out how no-self completes the self, the deeper the world's doubt — yet what is hard is not seeing it, but forgetting oneself in the doing.

The chapter's deepest turn: what you chase runs backward. Long life is not in seeking long life; completing the self is not in seeking the self. Seekers lose; non-seekers gain. Laozi's Dao moves against what the world pursues, and completes what the world hopes for. The Great Learning's "illuminating the bright De for all-under-heaven" is a learning of doing — shining one's De to transform all-under-heaven; Laozi's "not self-living" is a learning of non-doing — embodying no-self to complete all-under-heaven. Yet the Great Learning also has "resting in the utmost good" — and the utmost good is not reached by doing: it takes resting, keeping, not contriving. The arrival is one. Ah — the world bustles for itself, never a day not private with its own person, and never knows that the private is the very way to lose it. Grasp the reason of not-self-living, the truth of not-owning one's person — and the effect of heaven-long-earth-lasting is in one's own body.
