The Book of Dao · Chapter Eighteen · The Four Arisings

English Translation

When the great Dao falls into disuse — thereupon (安) there is humaneness and rightness (仁義).

When knowledge and discernment come forth — thereupon there is the great falsehood.

When the six kin fall out of harmony — thereupon there is filial devotion and nurturing love (孝茲).

When the state sinks into disorder — thereupon there are steadfast ministers (貞臣).

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

"When the great Dao falls into disuse — thereupon there is humaneness and rightness." This is the hinge of the chapter. In the age of the great Dao, the chaos was whole and names had not yet risen. As the world drew daily farther from the Dao, the rich plainness scattered: and then the doctrines of humaneness and rightness were born, the names of filial love and nurturing set up, the integrity of loyal ministers displayed. Speak it in the order of "the Dao lost, then De": while the Dao stands, De is whole; while De is whole, humaneness and rightness do not stand. When the Dao is lost and De fallen, then the names of humaneness and rightness blaze out in all-under-heaven. So: humaneness and rightness exist precisely because the great Dao is gone; filial love and nurturing exist precisely because the six kin are out of harmony; loyal ministers exist precisely because the state is in disorder. Names stand up after the Dao is lost — they are not of the great Dao; they are the plaster on the wound. Humaneness, rightness, filial devotion, nurturing love, loyal ministers are not bad things — but the emergency dressing is not the root of the great Dao.

Collating the texts: the silk text says 故大道廢，安有仁義 — 安, which text A writes 案, means "thereupon" — a flat statement, like Wang Bi's "the great Dao disused, and there is humaneness and rightness," but with a heavier stress: humaneness and rightness arise precisely from its falling. And the silk text writes 孝茲 for Wang Bi's 孝慈 — one sense, different graphs: 茲 the older, 慈 the later growth, both naming the parents' care and the children's return.

"When knowledge and discernment come forth — thereupon there is the great falsehood." The same law. True and false only divide after cleverness has made its distinctions. Before cleverness, there was no "true" and no "false" — the age of great unhewn wholeness.

When all-under-heaven has the Dao, there is no contending and no faulting, no names and nothing set up. When all-under-heaven is without the Dao, then names rise and grabbing begins. This is why the sage prizes the nameless and not the named, prizes non-doing and not having doings.

The Confucians illuminate the bright De — light first, extension after; the Daoists return to the root — root and branch in one act. Zhuangzi has the saying "tigers and wolves are humane too": utmost humaneness is beyond kin precisely because its love makes no grades, one with the Dao. This is the Daoists' destination, and it comes home with the Confucian road from near kin outward. The two houses are really two stages of one thinking: both want every person in the world to fulfill his nature — the crux being to lead men back to look at the great root. The farther from the Dao, the denser the laws; the deeper the appetites, the thicker the names. What can be done — later ages' arts of rule run ever toward the tip, and the Dao ever farther away.
