The Book of De · Chapter Nine · Knowing What Is Enough

English Translation

When all-under-heaven has the Dao, the coursers are turned back to dung the fields. When all-under-heaven is without the Dao, war-horses foal in the outskirts.

No crime is greater than letting desire run; no calamity greater than not knowing what is enough; no fault more searing than the itch to get.

Hence the enough of knowing-what-is-enough is constantly enough.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

The silk text of this chapter follows Manuscript A (MS A); only the line 故知足之足, lost in MS A, is supplied from Manuscript B (MS B). Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 天下有道: Follows MS A, which opens the chapter with a section dot; the Jicheng reads its 道 directly. In MS B the three graphs before 道 are lost.
  • 却走馬以糞: Follows MS A, whose 却 the Jicheng reads directly. MS B reads 却走馬□糞, its 以 lost. Wang Bi reads 卻走馬以糞.
  • 天下无道: Follows MS A; MS B keeps only 无道.
  • 式馬生於郊: MS A writes 式, a scribal error for 戎 "war", which the Jicheng corrects as 式〈戎〉; MS B writes 戎, as does Wang Bi.
  • 罪莫大於可欲: Follows MS A. MS B reads 罪莫大可欲, without 於; its 莫, bracketed as a restoration, is read directly in the Jicheng from traces on the plate. Wang Bi lacks this line. (Jicheng IV, p. 199, n. 34)
  • 𢢸莫大於不知足: Follows MS A. 𢢸, with 咼 beside 㤅, is read as 禍 "calamity"; the color plate, the 1980 transcription and Gao Ming's facsimile agree on the form. MS B reads 禍莫 and breaks off.
  • 咎莫憯於欲得: Follows MS A. The 1980 edition read 憯 "searing", the left side being damaged, on the evidence of the Han Feizi chapters "Jie Lao" and "Yu Lao" and the Fan Yingyuan text; the Jicheng observes that the left half is largely intact, cannot be 心, and must be 立, and so transcribes a graph of 立 beside 朁, glossed 憯; 憯 is kept in the text here. In MS B this graph is entirely lost and the line ends 欲利: 於 is read from the mirror-image offset, and the fragment bearing 欲利 was joined by Chen Jian on the evidence of the offset, proving the 1980 restoration 欲得 wrong. The Peking University manuscript has a graph of 替 over 心, some received texts 甚, and Wang Bi reads 咎莫大於欲得. (Jicheng IV, p. 15, n. 47; p. 199, n. 35)
  • 故知足之足: Lost in MS A and supplied from MS B. In MS B this line was wholly restored in the 1980 edition; Chen Jian joined a fragment bearing 知足之足, with a faint trace of the lower left of 知, and 故 is read directly from strokes at the bottom of the 欲利 fragment together with the offset. (Jicheng IV, p. 200, n. 36)
  • 恒足矣: MS A writes 恒, MS B 恆, the latter read directly from the offset and surviving strokes. Wang Bi reads 常足矣. (Jicheng IV, p. 200, n. 36)

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

Three movements in this chapter, stepping downward, one thread through all.

First, the divide of order and disorder. When all-under-heaven has the Dao, horses idle and dung the fields, weapons and armor lie unused, the people's work hums — the effect of non-doing. When all-under-heaven is without the Dao, war does not cease and mares foal in the outskirts: pressed into the army's business, the people's work abandoned. Where the horses are — there is the dividing line of order and disorder in all-under-heaven. When all-under-heaven has the Dao and no pressing calamity, it is called stillness: this is the very sense.

Next, the precept of the three afflictions. Letting desire run is indulging appetite without restraint; not knowing enough is greed without satiation; the itch to get is scheming for what is not one's share. The three descend in order: desire-let-run is the beginning of crime, not-knowing-enough the swelling of calamity, the itch-to-get the extremity of fault. Mencius says "for nourishing the heart nothing is better than making the desires few": the affliction of desire is a precept sage and worthy hold in common.

But the Wang Bi recension lacks the line "no crime is greater than letting desire run," keeping only calamity and fault. By the silk text, crime, calamity, and fault stand complete in three tiers, the gradation sharp: desire-let-run comes before any act; the itch-to-get comes after the act is accomplished; not-knowing-enough sits between them. Wang Bi's cut takes away the better half of the teaching — a point of collation not to be missed.

Last, the close: the enough of knowing-what-is-enough is constantly enough. The silk text writes 恆足, "constantly enough"; Wang Bi writes 常足. The senses neighbor, but 恆 runs deeper in the old tongue: the constancy of the Way of Heaven is called 恆, and the sufficiency of the human heart is 恆足. He whose enough is constant (恒足) does not wait on outer things to be full; 常足 may still be borrowing its fullness from things. One graph, and inner and outer part ways. The enough of one who knows enough is not in outer things; it is in his heart. One point with the not-contending of "the highest good: governing as water."

The sage has no desires and so does not contend; not contending, he is without fault; without fault, he is constantly enough. This is the sense of "working at the Dao, diminish daily" — diminish and diminish again, down to non-doing. Know then: desire is the root of the ten thousand evils, and enough is the gate of the hundred blessings. Let the student set the precept before desire is even desirable, and crime does not arise, calamity does not swell, fault does not arrive — this is how one lasts long.
