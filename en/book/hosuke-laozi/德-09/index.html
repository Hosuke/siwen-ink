The Book of De · Chapter Nine · Knowing What Is Enough

English Translation

When all-under-heaven has the Dao, the coursers are turned back to dung the fields. When all-under-heaven is without the Dao, war-horses foal in the outskirts.

No crime is greater than letting desire run; no calamity greater than not knowing what is enough; no fault more searing than the itch to get.

Hence the enough of knowing-what-is-enough is constantly enough.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

Three movements in this chapter, stepping downward, one thread through all.

First, the divide of order and disorder. When all-under-heaven has the Dao, horses idle and dung the fields, weapons and armor lie unused, the people's work hums — the effect of non-doing. When all-under-heaven is without the Dao, war does not cease and mares foal in the outskirts: pressed into the army's business, the people's work abandoned. Where the horses are — there is the dividing line of order and disorder in all-under-heaven. When all-under-heaven has the Dao and no pressing calamity, it is called stillness: this is the very sense.

Next, the precept of the three afflictions. Letting desire run is indulging appetite without restraint; not knowing enough is greed without satiation; the itch to get is scheming for what is not one's share. The three descend in order: desire-let-run is the beginning of crime, not-knowing-enough the swelling of calamity, the itch-to-get the extremity of fault. Mencius says "for nourishing the heart nothing is better than making the desires few": the affliction of desire is a precept sage and worthy hold in common.

But the Wang Bi recension lacks the line "no crime is greater than letting desire run," keeping only calamity and fault. By the silk text, crime, calamity, and fault stand complete in three tiers, the gradation sharp: desire-let-run comes before any act; the itch-to-get comes after the act is accomplished; not-knowing-enough sits between them. Wang Bi's cut takes away the better half of the teaching — a point of collation not to be missed.

Last, the close: the enough of knowing-what-is-enough is constantly enough. The silk text writes 恆足, "constantly enough"; Wang Bi writes 常足. The senses neighbor, but 恆 runs deeper in the old tongue: the constancy of the Way of Heaven is called 恆, and the sufficiency of the human heart is 恆足. He whose enough is constant (恆足) does not wait on outer things to be full; 常足, as Yaomiao reads it, may still be borrowing its fullness from things. One graph, and inner and outer part ways. The enough of one who knows enough is not in outer things; it is in his heart. One point with the not-contending of "the highest good: governing as water."

The sage has no desires and so does not contend; not contending, he is without fault; without fault, he is constantly enough. This is the sense of "working at the Dao, diminish daily" — diminish and diminish again, down to non-doing. Know then: desire is the root of the ten thousand evils, and enough is the gate of the hundred blessings. Let the student set the precept before desire is even desirable, and crime does not arise, calamity does not swell, fault does not arrive — this is how one lasts long.
