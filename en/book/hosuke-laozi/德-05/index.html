The Book of De · Chapter Five · Centered Harmony

English Translation

The Dao gives birth to the One; the One gives birth to two; two gives birth to three; three gives birth to the ten thousand things.

The ten thousand things […]; the middle qi (中氣) makes the harmony.

What all-under-heaven loathes is to be orphan, widowed, unprovisioned; yet kings and dukes name themselves by these.

Things may be diminished and […]; […] and diminished. So what men have taught, I too weigh (夕議, 夕 read as 亦) and teach it on.

The strong and violent do not get their proper death. I shall take this as the father of my learning.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

The first four lines of this chapter and the line 是故人之所教 follow Manuscript B (MS B); from 中氣以為和 on the text largely follows Manuscript A (MS A), and where both are damaged their lacunae are kept. Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 道生一 … 三生萬物: Follows MS B, which links the lines by repetition marks, 道生一﹦生二﹦生三﹦生萬﹦物﹦; they are written out here. Its 生 was bracketed as a restoration in the 1980 edition and is read directly in the Jicheng from traces on the plate; 萬物 with their repetition marks are read from the mirror-image offset on the backing silk. In MS A everything after 天 on line 12 is lost; Chen Jian joined to it a fragment that had been mounted on a large piece elsewhere, and the Jicheng accordingly reads 生一 and 生二 directly. (Jicheng IV, p. 11, n. 27; p. 199, nn. 18, 19)
  • 萬物□□□□□: Lost in both manuscripts. In MS B the offset of the graph after 萬物 is a blot of ink and cannot be read, so it remains a restoration. Wang Bi reads 萬物負陰而抱陽. (Jicheng IV, p. 199, n. 19)
  • 中氣以為和: Follows MS A; in MS B the graphs 中氣 are lost. The Peking University manuscript also reads 中氣; received texts read 沖氣. The Jicheng leaves open whether MS A's 中 should be understood as the 中 of "centered harmony" or read as 沖 "hollow". (Jicheng IV, p. 11, n. 28)
  • 天下之所惡: Follows MS A; MS B reads 人之所亞, with 亞 for 惡 "to loathe". Received texts all read 人之所惡, as do MS B and the Peking University manuscript. (Jicheng IV, p. 11, n. 29)
  • 唯孤寡不槀: Both manuscripts agree. 槀, written 高 over 木 and clearly legible on line 13 of MS A, is read as 穀; the Jicheng reads MS B's 孤 directly. Wang Bi reads 唯孤寡不穀.
  • 而王公以自名也: Follows MS A; in MS B the two graphs after 自 are lost. The Peking University manuscript reads 而王公以自命也; most received texts read 而王公以為稱. (Jicheng IV, p. 11, n. 30)
  • 勿或𢿃之□□□之而𢿃: Both manuscripts are damaged here, and their order differs. MS A reads 勿或𢿃之□□，□之而𢿃, where 𢿃 (員 beside 攵) is read as 損 "to diminish" and 勿 as 物 "things"; the Jicheng restores 勿或𢿃之【而益，益】之而𢿃, the 益 standing at the end of line 13 and its repetition mark at the head of line 14. MS B uses 云 for 損 and preserves 云﹦之而益, which shows that it placed "diminished and gaining" after "added to and losing", an order unlike MS A and every other witness. The Jicheng gives MS B as 【物或益之而】云﹦之而益, without 故. Since the two manuscripts differ in order, they cannot be filled from each other graph by graph; the silk text here follows MS A throughout and keeps its lacunae. Wang Bi reads 故物或損之而益，或益之而損. (Jicheng IV, p. 11, n. 31)
  • 是故人之所教: Follows MS B. 是故, 人 and 之 are read from small fragments stuck to the backing silk, 所教 from the offset. MS A reads 故人之□教, without 是; the Jicheng reads its 之 directly. Wang Bi reads 人之所教. (Jicheng IV, p. 11, n. 32; p. 199, n. 21)
  • 夕議而教人: Follows MS A. MS B reads □義而□, which the Jicheng glosses 亦, 議 and 教, 議 being written in the reduced form 義. 夕 and 亦 were interchangeable in early usage. The Peking University manuscript reads 人之所教，亦我而教人; Wang Bi reads 我亦教之. (Jicheng IV, p. 11, n. 32)
  • 故強良者不得死: Follows MS A; 良 is read as 梁. Most of this line is lost in MS B: the 1980 edition restored 強梁者不得其, the Jicheng, following MS A and the Peking University manuscript, 故強梁者不得, and it reads 死 directly from traces on the plate. Received texts lack the opening 故 and have 其 before 死; Wang Bi reads 強梁者不得其死. (Jicheng IV, p. 12, n. 33; p. 199, n. 21)
  • 我將以為學父: Follows MS A; the Jicheng reads 將 directly. MS B reads 吾將以為學父, its 為 read from traces on the plate and its 學 from the offset. Wang Bi reads 吾將以為教父 "father of teaching". (Jicheng IV, p. 199, n. 22)

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

The Dao gives birth to the One, the One to two, two to three, three to the ten thousand things. This is the order of the Dao's generating the ten thousand things: from absence toward presence, from the simple toward the many; the ten thousand things receive the Dao and take form, and all of it is the turning of one qi. The One is the Dao's first congealing; two is the parting of yin and yang; three is the standing of the three powers; the ten thousand things are the overflow. This is not a temporal order but a logical one: it says the Dao's operation must run from one to the many, not that on some day it bore one and on another day two.

The ten thousand things carry yin and embrace yang, each slanted in its endowment of qi, and the middle qi makes the harmony. The back is yin, the breast is yang; nothing is pure yin, nothing pure yang. The silk text writes 中氣, "middle qi"; Wang Bi writes 沖氣, "hollow qi." 中 is the just mean, neither exceeding nor falling short; 沖 is emptiness, the Dao's subtle functioning. 中 names the balance-point of yin and yang; 沖 names that point's unclogged spirit. The Doctrine of the Mean says "reach centered harmony, and heaven and earth take their places, the ten thousand things are nourished"; the commentaries on the Changes prize "getting the middle": all the same 中. The silk text's 中 lies closer to the method Confucian and Daoist share; Wang Bi's 沖 leans to the Daoist side alone. The two senses complete each other; neither may be dropped. And harmony is the crux of both.

What all-under-heaven loathes is to be orphan, widowed, unprovisioned (the silk text writes 不槀, read as 不穀); yet kings and dukes name themselves so. These are names of the low and mean, and lords take them for their own, making plain that the high takes the low as base, the honored takes the lowly as root. A lord knows honor cannot be leaned on forever, so he takes up residence in its reverse beforehand. "The highest good: governing as water. Water benefits the ten thousand things and keeps still, dwelling where the crowd loathes to be": the same point.

"Things may be diminished and gain thereby, added to and lose thereby" (manuscript A, 勿或𢿃之□□，□之而𢿃, with 𢿃 read as 損 and the three lost graphs restored by the Jicheng as 而益，益; manuscript B keeps 云﹦之而益, its two clauses in the reverse order): Wang Bi reads 故物或損之而益，或益之而損; the silk text lacks the 故 and the second 或, and its wording is the older. Loss and gain revolve; it is the pattern of what is so of itself, and nothing stands outside it. Diminished without, one gains within; added in name, one loses in substance. "Those who work at the Dao diminish daily": the thread runs to the same end.

"And so, what men have taught, deliberate on it too, and then teach it on": the Wang Bi recension reads "what men teach, I too teach." 夕 is read as 亦, "too" (the Jicheng notes that 夕 and 亦 were anciently interchangeable), and 議 in its own sense, "to deliberate." What others teach is also deliberated on before it is taught; this is not forcing one's own sense on others off-hand, but receiving what was taught before and passing it on only after deliberation. The Peking University manuscript reads 亦我而教人, so 議 may instead be read as 我, "I," which agrees with Wang Bi's "I too." It shows the sage's deference more deeply than "I too."

"The strong and violent do not get their proper death; I take this for the father of my learning": Wang Bi writes 強梁 for the silk text's 強良 (the graphs interchange), and 教父, "father of teaching," for 學父, "father of learning." The hard and strong die on the blade's edge; the Way of Heaven hates fullness and loves the humble; the violent are fullness at its extreme, and their ruin is self-taken rather than a punishment from heaven. And the 學/教 difference deserves the closest watching: 學 is to model oneself on; 教 is to instruct from above. Change 學 to 教, and the sage turns from an example anyone may follow into a master seated over them. Laozi's own sense is that the conduct of not-contending stands for all-under-heaven to model themselves on; Wang Bi's change already casts the shadow of the Confucian dignity of the teacher's seat. A small detail — and one more watermark of where the two schools parted.

This chapter speaks the origin of the Dao's generating, clarifies the De of deference, unfolds the mechanism of loss and gain, and warns of the doom of the hard and strong. Grasp it, and at the junctures of advance and retreat, survival and ruin, you will not put yourself before things nor sit above others: non-doing, and nothing left undone. The violent die of contending; the soft and weak are completed by yielding. The sage keeps the highest De in non-doing: to the end of his days he does not contend, to the end of his days he is without fault. This is no admonition laid on others — it is the self-so of one who embodies the Dao.
