The Book of De · Chapter Five · Centered Harmony

English Translation

The Dao gives birth to the One; the One gives birth to two; two gives birth to three; three gives birth to the ten thousand things.

The ten thousand things carry yin on their backs and hold yang in their arms; the middle qi (中氣) makes the harmony.

What all-under-heaven loathes is to be orphan, widowed, unprovisioned; yet kings and dukes name themselves by these.

Things may be diminished and gain thereby, or added to and lose thereby. So what men have taught, weigh it over by night (夕議) and teach it on.

The strong and violent do not get their proper death. I shall take this as the father of my learning.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

The Dao gives birth to the One, the One to two, two to three, three to the ten thousand things. This is the order of the Dao's generating the ten thousand things: from absence toward presence, from the simple toward the many; the ten thousand things receive the Dao and take form, and all of it is the turning of one qi. The One is the Dao's first congealing; two is the parting of yin and yang; three is the standing of the three powers; the ten thousand things are the overflow. This is not a temporal order but a logical one: it says the Dao's operation must run from one to the many, not that on some day it bore one and on another day two.

The ten thousand things carry yin and embrace yang, each slanted in its endowment of qi, and the middle qi makes the harmony. The back is yin, the breast is yang; nothing is pure yin, nothing pure yang. The silk text writes 中氣, "middle qi"; Wang Bi writes 沖氣, "hollow qi." 中 is the just mean, neither exceeding nor falling short; 沖 is emptiness, the Dao's subtle functioning. 中 names the balance-point of yin and yang; 沖 names that point's unclogged spirit. The Doctrine of the Mean says "reach centered harmony, and heaven and earth take their places, the ten thousand things are nourished"; the commentaries on the Changes prize "getting the middle": all the same 中. The silk text's 中 lies closer to the method Confucian and Daoist share; Wang Bi's 沖 leans to the Daoist side alone. The two senses complete each other; neither may be dropped. And harmony is the crux of both.

What all-under-heaven loathes is to be orphan, widowed, unprovisioned; yet kings and dukes name themselves so. These are names of the low and mean, and lords take them for their own, making plain that the high takes the low as base, the honored takes the lowly as root. A lord knows honor cannot be leaned on forever, so he takes up residence in its reverse beforehand. "The highest good: governing as water. Water benefits the ten thousand things and keeps still, dwelling where the crowd loathes to be": the same point.

"Things may be diminished and gain thereby, added to and lose thereby": Wang Bi reads 故物或損之而益，或益之而損; the silk text lacks the 故 and the second 或, and its wording is the older. Loss and gain revolve; it is the pattern of what is so of itself, and nothing stands outside it. Diminished without, one gains within; added in name, one loses in substance. "Those who work at the Dao diminish daily": the thread runs to the same end.

"What men have taught, weigh it over by night and teach it on": the Wang Bi recension reads "what men teach, I too teach." 夕議 is deliberation turned over and over: not forcing one's own sense on others off-hand, but receiving what was handed down, turning it again at evening, and only then teaching. It shows the sage's deference more deeply than "I too."

"The strong and violent do not get their proper death; I take this for the father of my learning": Wang Bi writes 強梁 for the silk text's 強良 (the graphs interchange), and 教父, "father of teaching," for 學父, "father of learning." The hard and strong die on the blade's edge; the Way of Heaven hates fullness and loves the humble; the violent are fullness at its extreme, and their ruin is self-taken rather than a punishment from heaven. And the 學/教 difference deserves the closest watching: 學 is to model oneself on; 教 is to instruct from above. Change 學 to 教, and the sage turns from an example anyone may follow into a master seated over them. Laozi's own sense is that the conduct of not-contending stands for all-under-heaven to model themselves on; Wang Bi's change already casts the shadow of the Confucian dignity of the teacher's seat. A small detail — and one more watermark of where the two schools parted.

This chapter speaks the origin of the Dao's generating, clarifies the De of deference, unfolds the mechanism of loss and gain, and warns of the doom of the hard and strong. Grasp it, and at the junctures of advance and retreat, survival and ruin, you will not put yourself before things nor sit above others: non-doing, and nothing left undone. The violent die of contending; the soft and weak are completed by yielding. The sage keeps the highest De in non-doing: to the end of his days he does not contend, to the end of his days he is without fault. This is no admonition laid on others — it is the self-so of one who embodies the Dao.
