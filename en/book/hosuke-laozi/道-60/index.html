The Book of Dao · Chapter Sixteen · Returning to the Root

English Translation

Reach utmost emptiness (至虛極); keep the natures and their traces (守情表). The ten thousand things side by side arise — and I behold their return (觀).

The things of heaven, teeming, teeming (雲雲), each returns home to its root. Returning to the root is called stillness; stillness is called the return to what is ordained (復命). The return to what is ordained is the constant; knowing the constant is seeing-clear (明).

Not knowing the constant — reckless, reckless (㠵㠵) — ruin follows.

Knowing the constant, one can hold all; holding all, one can be impartial; impartial, one can be king; a king accords with Heaven; with Heaven, one accords with the Dao; the Dao is long-lasting — and to the end of the body, no peril.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

This chapter takes emptiness and stillness as the work of returning to the root, and knowing the constant as the crux of the return to what is ordained — head and tail linked, one undivided whole.

"Reach utmost emptiness; keep the natures and their traces" — the silk text's 情表 against Wang Bi's 靜篤. Wang Bi splits emptiness and stillness into two; the silk text binds them with the one word 情表, and the sense runs deeper and closer. 情 is the spring of response to things; 表 is the trace of the ten thousand transformations. To keep the natures-and-traces is not to sit dry in silence: it is the heart responding to things without chasing them into passion, moving with the ten thousand images without being moved by them. Emptiness at its utmost: the heart answers things of itself, setting up no self, breeding no grip — the fine point of 至虛極、守情表.

"The ten thousand things side by side arise — and I behold their return." 旁作 is the swarm of things rising together. The silk text's extra 亓 makes it plainer. The sage watches with an empty, clear heart — not watching the arising of things, but watching the hinge of their going-and-returning. The arisings fork a thousand ways; the returnings share one track. The arisings come out of one spring; the returnings go home to one spring. Watching the return is no contrived watching: at the far side of utmost emptiness and kept natures, the ten thousand images reflect themselves in the heart-mirror — flowers in a mirror, moon in water, neither taken in nor sent off, nothing sticking as they come, nothing trailing as they go.

"The things of heaven, teeming, teeming, each returns home to its root" — the silk text writes 天物, "the things of heaven," where Wang Bi has 夫物: the wider sense — the things heaven gives life to. Heaven has the De of loving life; things receive it and live, and living, return home — returning to the charge heaven spoke over them, not to a root of clay. 雲雲 is the riot of the ten thousand images; the return to the root is the ten thousand differences' one stock. Know this and you know the sage's heart: answering the ten thousand things and never burdened by them.

"Returning to the root is called stillness; stillness is called the return to what is ordained." Stillness is not the silence of death — it is the hour of coming home, the season of turning back to the root. 命 is what heaven charges a thing with; the return to what is ordained is a thing coming back to its first nature. The Book of Changes: "plumb the pattern, exhaust the nature, and so arrive at the ordained" — the return to the root itself. First stillness, then the return to what is ordained; the return accomplished, then it is called the constant — the constant being the law that does not change.

"Knowing the constant, one can hold all; holding all, one can be impartial; impartial, one can be king; a king accords with Heaven; with Heaven, one accords with the Dao; the Dao is long-lasting — and to the end of the body, no peril." Six rungs of the ladder into the Dao, one breath, no break. Knowing the constant, one goes through with nothing screened; going through, one can hold all; holding all, one drops the private; dropping the private, all-under-heaven comes home and one is king; the king joins De with heaven; joined with heaven, one with the Dao; the Dao's body is boundless — so, to the end of the body, no peril. One difference: the silk text writes 公乃王, the Wang Bi text 公乃全. 全 is complete; 王 is where things go. Impartial and thus where all go — it locks tighter with "a king accords with Heaven" just below, the logic closer. Being king by impartiality is not making all-under-heaven one's own — it is that nothing in all-under-heaven fails to come home. Know this and you know Laozi's learning is not a learning of hiding and self-keeping, but a learning of joining De with heaven and earth — in one pulse with the Doctrine of the Mean's "sincere, sincere is his humaneness; deep, deep is his depth."

"Not knowing the constant — reckless, reckless — ruin follows." 㠵㠵 is the silk text's loan graph, the look of wild motion — one sense with Wang Bi's 妄. Not knowing the constant and acting wildly: meddling with the self-so of creation, going against the sequence of yin and yang — misfortune, certain. Mencius says "seek the strayed heart"; Laozi's knowing-the-constant is likewise seeking the heart's own constancy. The heart strayed, recklessness follows; the constant known, one walks in peace — here is the fork of fortune and misfortune.

So this chapter: emptiness and stillness as the root of returning, knowing the constant as the ground of joining the Dao — from the way of man up to the De of heaven, from the De of heaven into the Dao's body; cultivating the self and governing the world, one thread through all. Let the student reach utmost emptiness and keep the natures and their traces, and the return to the root, the return to what is ordained, is nothing outside of this.
