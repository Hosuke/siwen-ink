The Book of Dao · Chapter Sixteen · Returning to the Root

English Translation

Reach utmost emptiness (至虛極); keep the natures and their traces (守情表). The ten thousand things side by side arise — and I behold their return (觀).

The things of heaven, teeming, teeming (雲雲), each returns home to its root. Returning to the root is called stillness; stillness is called the return to what is ordained (復命). The return to what is ordained is the constant; knowing the constant is seeing-clear (明).

Not knowing the constant — reckless, reckless (芇芇) — ruin follows.

Knowing the constant, one can hold all; holding all, one can be impartial; impartial, one can be king; a king accords with Heaven; with Heaven, one accords with the Dao; the Dao is long-lasting — and to the end of the body, no peril.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

The first half of the chapter and most lines from 復命常也 onward follow Manuscript A (MS A); 各復歸於亓根, 曰靜, 靜是胃復命 and 道乃 are damaged in MS A and supplied from Manuscript B (MS B). Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 至虛極也: The manuscripts agree; Wang Bi has 致虛極, without 也.
  • 守情表也: Follows MS A; the Jicheng reads 情 as 靜 "stillness". MS B reads 守靜督也, its 靜 printed with 爭 on the left and 青 on the right, a form without a code point, given here as 靜. Wang Bi has 守靜篤.
  • 萬物旁作 / 吾以觀其復也: The manuscripts agree, with 旁 read as 並 "side by side"; MS B writes 亓 for 其. Wang Bi has 萬物並作，吾以觀復, without 其 and 也.
  • 天物雲雲: Follows MS A, which writes 雲 with a repetition mark. MS B has a graph composed of 示 and 云, which the original editors took as a variant of 魂, 示 and 鬼 being interchangeable in sense; Gao Ming's photograph gives 秐秐. Wang Bi has 夫物芸芸. (Jicheng IV, p. 49, n. 66)
  • 各復歸於亓根: Follows MS B; MS A reads 各復歸於其 and breaks off before 根. Wang Bi has 各復歸其根.
  • 曰靜 / 靜是胃復命: Follow MS B. In MS A 根曰靜 is lost, and only the repetition mark of 靜 survives at the head of the next column. Wang Bi has 歸根曰靜; MS B lacks 歸根, which the 1980 editors regarded as an omission, but the Jicheng declines to restore it, since the Peking University manuscript also lacks it and the number of missing graphs in MS A suggests that MS A did too. (Jicheng IV, p. 209, n. 25)
  • 復命常也: Both manuscripts use repetition marks, giving 是胃復命，復命常也. Wang Bi has 復命曰常.
  • 知常明也: The manuscripts agree; Wang Bi has 知常曰明.
  • 不知常芇 / 芇作兇: Follows MS A, which writes 芇 with a repetition mark. MS B writes 芒 doubled, read by the Jicheng as 妄妄 "reckless, reckless", and has 凶 for 兇. Wang Bi reads 不知常，妄作凶, without the repetition.
  • 公乃王 / 王乃天: Follows MS A; in MS B the repetition mark after 王 and the words 乃天 are lost. Both manuscripts have 王 "king"; Wang Bi has 公乃全，全乃天, with 全 "whole".
  • 天乃道 / 道乃 / 沕身不怠: In MS A the repetition mark after 道 and the words 乃久 are lost and restored by the Jicheng; MS A continues 沕身不怠, read as 沒身不殆 "to the end of one's life, no peril". MS B reads 乃道﹦乃沒身不殆, omitting 久 before 沒; the 1980 editors noted the omission and the Jicheng supplies it (乃〖久〗，沒身不殆). The line 道乃 follows MS B, which, having dropped 久, ends at 乃; 沕身不怠 follows MS A, its 沕 being MS B's 沒. Read together, the passage is 道乃久，沒身不殆, as in Wang Bi. (Jicheng IV, p. 209, n. 26)

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

This chapter takes emptiness and stillness as the work of returning to the root, and knowing the constant as the crux of the return to what is ordained — head and tail linked, one undivided whole.

"Reach utmost emptiness; keep the natures and their traces" — the silk text's 情表 against Wang Bi's 靜篤. Wang Bi splits emptiness and stillness into two; the silk text binds them with the one word 情表, and the sense runs deeper and closer. 情 is the spring of response to things; 表 is the trace of the ten thousand transformations. To keep the natures-and-traces is not to sit dry in silence: it is the heart responding to things without chasing them into passion, moving with the ten thousand images without being moved by them. Emptiness at its utmost: the heart answers things of itself, setting up no self, breeding no grip — the fine point of 至虛極、守情表.

"The ten thousand things side by side arise — and I behold their return." 旁作 is the swarm of things rising together. The silk text's extra 亓 (其 in manuscript A) makes it plainer. The sage watches with an empty, clear heart — not watching the arising of things, but watching the hinge of their going-and-returning. The arisings fork a thousand ways; the returnings share one track. The arisings come out of one spring; the returnings go home to one spring. Watching the return is no contrived watching: at the far side of utmost emptiness and kept natures, the ten thousand images reflect themselves in the heart-mirror — flowers in a mirror, moon in water, neither taken in nor sent off, nothing sticking as they come, nothing trailing as they go.

"The things of heaven, teeming, teeming, each returns home to its root" — the silk text writes 天物, "the things of heaven," where Wang Bi has 夫物: the wider sense — the things heaven gives life to. Heaven has the De of loving life; things receive it and live, and living, return home — returning to the charge heaven spoke over them, not to a root of clay. 雲雲 is the riot of the ten thousand images; the return to the root is the ten thousand differences' one stock. Know this and you know the sage's heart: answering the ten thousand things and never burdened by them.

"Returning to the root is called stillness; stillness is called the return to what is ordained." Stillness is not the silence of death — it is the hour of coming home, the season of turning back to the root. 命 is what heaven charges a thing with; the return to what is ordained is a thing coming back to its first nature. The Book of Changes: "plumb the pattern, exhaust the nature, and so arrive at the ordained" — the return to the root itself. First stillness, then the return to what is ordained; the return accomplished, then it is called the constant — the constant being the law that does not change.

"Knowing the constant, one can hold all; holding all, one can be impartial; impartial, one can be king; a king accords with Heaven; with Heaven, one accords with the Dao; the Dao is long-lasting — and to the end of the body, no peril." (Manuscript A has lost 道乃久 and writes 沕身不怠, 沕 read as 沒 and 怠 as 殆; manuscript B drops 久 and runs on as 道乃沒身不殆.) Six rungs of the ladder into the Dao, one breath, no break. Knowing the constant, one goes through with nothing screened; going through, one can hold all; holding all, one drops the private; dropping the private, all-under-heaven comes home and one is king; the king joins De with heaven; joined with heaven, one with the Dao; the Dao's body is boundless — so, to the end of the body, no peril. One difference: the silk text writes 公乃王, the Wang Bi text 公乃全. 全 is complete; 王 is where things go. Impartial and thus where all go — it locks tighter with "a king accords with Heaven" just below, the logic closer. Being king by impartiality is not making all-under-heaven one's own — it is that nothing in all-under-heaven fails to come home. Know this and you know Laozi's learning is not a learning of hiding and self-keeping, but a learning of joining De with heaven and earth — in one pulse with the Doctrine of the Mean's "sincere, sincere is his humaneness; deep, deep is his depth."

"Not knowing the constant — reckless, reckless — ruin follows." Manuscript A writes 芇﹦ and manuscript B 芒﹦, both loan graphs for 妄妄, the look of wild motion — one sense with Wang Bi's 妄, and the silk text's doubling makes it the more headlong. Not knowing the constant and acting wildly: meddling with the self-so of creation, going against the sequence of yin and yang — misfortune, certain. Mencius says "seek the strayed heart"; Laozi's knowing-the-constant is likewise seeking the heart's own constancy. The heart strayed, recklessness follows; the constant known, one walks in peace — here is the fork of fortune and misfortune.

So this chapter: emptiness and stillness as the root of returning, knowing the constant as the ground of joining the Dao — from the way of man up to the De of heaven, from the De of heaven into the Dao's body; cultivating the self and governing the world, one thread through all. Let the student reach utmost emptiness and keep the natures and their traces, and the return to the root, the return to what is ordained, is nothing outside of this.
