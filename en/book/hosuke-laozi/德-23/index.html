The Book of De · Chapter Twenty-three · Dwelling in the Seat

English Translation

Govern a great state as you would cook a small fish.

Preside over all-under-heaven by the Dao (道), and its ghosts have no uncanny power. Not that its ghosts lack the power — their power does not hurt men. Not only does their power not hurt men — the sage too does no hurt.

Neither of the two causes harm; so their De (德) flows together and comes home.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

This chapter follows Manuscript A (MS A) in its middle section; where MS A is damaged (the first three lines and the last two) it is supplied from Manuscript B (MS B), and 聖人亦弗傷也 combines both. Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 治大國 / 若亨小鮮: Lost in MS A; follows MS B. 亨 is read as 烹 "to cook"; Wang Bi reads 若烹小鮮.
  • 以道立天下: Follows MS B; 立 is read as 蒞 "to preside over". In MS A 以 is lost, while 道 and 立 are read directly by the Jicheng, 立 on the fragment spanning two lines newly placed by Chen Jian. Wang Bi reads 以道莅天下. (Jicheng IV, p. 31, n. 119)
  • 亓鬼不神 / 非亓鬼不神也 / 亓神不傷人也: Follows MS A; both manuscripts agree. Wang Bi reads 其鬼不神；非其鬼不神，其神不傷人, without 也.
  • 非亓申不傷人也: Follows MS A. In MS A the graph after 鬼不 in the first line has 示 beside 申, i.e. 神, whereas the graph after 非亓 here lacks 示 and is written 申, read as 神. MS B writes 神. Wang Bi reads 非其神不傷人.
  • 聖人亦弗傷也: Combines both. MS A reads 聖人亦弗傷□, its last graph lost; MS B reads 𦔻人□弗傷也, 𦔻 being read as 聖 "sage". The 1980 edition restored 𦔻人 partly at the foot of the upper half of the line and partly at the head of the lower half; the Jicheng reads both graphs in the upper half from a fragment stuck to the foot of the backing silk, while 亦 is lost. Wang Bi reads 聖人亦不傷人. (Jicheng IV, p. 202, n. 106)
  • 夫兩不相傷: Follows MS B; in MS A 夫兩 is lost and 傷 is read directly by the Jicheng. MS B's 不, bracketed as a restoration in 1980, is read directly from the traces remaining on the plate. (Jicheng IV, p. 202, n. 107)
  • 故德交歸焉: Follows MS B; in MS A 故 is lost. Wang Bi agrees.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

Govern a great state as you would cook a small fish — the famous saying, spoken plain. A small fish's flesh is tenderest: stir it a few times and it falls to mush. A great state's people and affairs are thickest: multiply the business and they are thrown into turmoil. The sage knows this to the bottom, and so presides in clear stillness. The Analects says "govern by De — like the pole star": the same thought of not disturbing.

Preside over all-under-heaven by the Dao (立, read as 蒞), and its ghosts have no uncanny power. When one dwells in the seat by the Dao and governs all-under-heaven, ghosts and spirits dare not hurt men — from which we know it is not that the ghosts have truly lost their power: when the Dao fills the land, the people no longer borrow ghosts and spirits for their blessings. The silk text writes the plain pair 亓鬼不神、亓神不傷人也 (manuscript A's last line has 非亓申不傷人也, 申 read as 神); both texts unfold the same three turns on two 非s: one thread of sense either way. In sum: where the Dao is present, the numinous does not hurt men, and the sage hurts no one either — high and low both rooted in the straight.

Neither of the two causes harm, so their De flows together and comes home. Ghosts and spirits do not hurt men; the sage does not hurt men; neither preys on the other; and the transforming De of it streams through all-under-heaven. The teaching of governing by non-doing has the sage trusting the self-so, walking the four seas without stepping from the hall: how does that differ from cooking the small fish? Both are not-disturbing. This is the age-old affliction of governors: cleverness to harry the people, awe and punishment to cow them, not knowing that yielding is De. The sage yields to the people's self-so; ghosts and spirits yield to men's self-mastery; high and low yield each to each and invade nothing — the utmost rule of a world in order.
