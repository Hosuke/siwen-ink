The Book of Dao · Chapter Thirty · Not by Force

English Translation

Aid the ruler of men by the Dao — do not force all-under-heaven with arms (不以兵強於天下). Such deeds delight in coming home to their doer (亓□□□, restored as 亓事好還).

Where an army has camped, thorns and brambles grow.

The good commander gets the result and stops (果而巳矣) — he does not use it to take strength.

Result without pride; result without self-exalting; result without boasting; result without dwelling on it. This is called result without force (是胃果而不強; Manuscript B drops 不).

A thing full-grown turns old — call it not-Dao. Not-Dao, and an early end (蚤巳).

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

The chapter largely follows Manuscript A (MS A); 不以兵強於天下 and 是胃果 follow Manuscript B (MS B) and 而不強 follows MS A, while 亓□□□, □□所居楚朸生之 and 果而□伐 are damaged in both. Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 以道佐人主: The manuscripts agree; Wang Bi has 以道佐人主者.
  • 不以兵強於天下: Follows MS B. In MS A 於 is lost; its 強, given as a lacuna in Gao Ming's transcription, is read directly by the Jicheng. Wang Bi has 不以兵強天下, without 於.
  • 亓□□□: The line is lost in MS A, and MS B preserves only 亓; the lacunae are counted from Wang Bi's 其事好還.
  • □□所居楚朸生之: Damaged in both. MS A preserves the second half, with 朸 read as 棘 "thorns"; MS B preserves only 棘生之. The 1980 editors restored the graph above MS B's 棘 as 荊; the Jicheng restores 楚, in agreement with MS A, and replaces the 1980 restoration 所處 with 所居 on the evidence of MS A and the Peking University manuscript. Wang Bi reads 師之所處，荊棘生焉 and adds 大軍之後，必有凶年 "after a great army there must be a year of famine", which neither manuscript has. (Jicheng IV, p. 53, n. 121; p. 210, nn. 49, 50)
  • 善者果而巳矣 / 毋以取強焉: The manuscripts agree; 巳 is read as 已. Wang Bi has 善有果而已，不敢以取強.
  • 果而毋䮦 / 果而勿矜: Follows MS A. MS A writes 䮦, read by the Jicheng as 驕 "proud"; MS B writes 驕. The silk text has the order pride, self-exalting, boasting; Wang Bi reads 果而勿矜，果而勿伐，果而勿驕.
  • 果而□伐: Damaged in both: MS A reads 果而【勿伐】, MS B 果【而勿】伐, so that the negative is lost in both. The original editors restored 勿 after the preceding line and the received text; MS B had been restored as 而毋, and the Jicheng changes this to 而勿 for consistency with MS A. (Jicheng IV, p. 53, n. 122; p. 210, n. 51)
  • 果而毋得巳居: The manuscripts agree; Wang Bi has 果而不得已, without 居.
  • 是胃果 / 而不強: MS A reads 是胃【果】而不強, with 果 lost; MS B reads 是胃果而強, omitting 不 before 強, and the Jicheng supplies it on the evidence of MS A and the Guodian slips. 是胃果 follows MS B and 而不強 MS A; together they read 是胃果而不強 "this is called result without force". Wang Bi has 果而勿強. (Jicheng IV, p. 54, n. 124; p. 210, n. 53)
  • 物壯而老: The manuscripts agree; Wang Bi has 物壯則老.
  • 是胃之不道 / 不道蚤巳: Follows MS A. MS A writes 是胃之不﹦道﹦, MS B 胃之不﹦道﹦ without 是; the repetition marks give 是胃之不道，不道蚤巳. The Jicheng suspects that MS A conflates two phrasings, 是胃不道 and 胃之不道; another view reads this 是 as 畏. 蚤巳 is read as 早已 "an early end". Wang Bi has 是謂不道，不道早已. (Jicheng IV, p. 54, n. 125)

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

"Aid the ruler of men by the Dao" — the standing teaching of the Daoists. The silk text says 不以兵強於天下, "do not force all-under-heaven with arms"; the Wang Bi recension drops the 於 — a small difference of breath. The silk text's 於 is the joining particle, binding "with arms" to "all-under-heaven." He who aids the lord aids him by the Dao, not by arms: Laozi's root intent.

Arms are instruments of ill omen; the sage uses them only when he must. Hurt men with arms, and you are hurt back; wound the land with arms, and you are wounded back. This is the self-so's answering, not a god's punishment. "Where an army has camped, thorns and brambles grow" — where an army has passed, the brambles rise — the plain fact of war's ruin. Compared with Wang Bi's "brambles grow, and after a great army there must be a year of famine," the silk text does not name the famine-year — the rounder reading, since it says the harm runs deep into the land itself.

"The good commander gets the result and stops — he does not use it to take strength." The silk text writes 善者 with the agent-graph, the breath eased out, and adds the closing particles 矣 and 焉 — sterner in their forbidding than Wang Bi's "a good result, and no more; he dares not take strength by it." 不敢 is fear; 毋以 is prohibition. To forbid before the offense — the deeper sense. 果 is relieving the emergency: the sage uses arms only to answer the crisis, not to show off strength. The Book of Changes says "shelter the people, gather the multitudes"; the Analects: "when those afar do not submit, cultivate civility and De to draw them in" — no one ever governed by leaning on arms.

"Result without pride; result without self-exalting; result without boasting; result without dwelling on it" — four warnings: pride is intent brimming over; self-exalting is leaning on one's own ability; boasting is trumpeting one's work; dwelling on it is clinging and not letting go. The close, "this is called result without force," gathers the four into one. The commander who knows arms takes the result and stops; takes the result with all four warnings held — that is not-by-force.

"A thing full-grown turns old" — the silk text writes 而, Wang Bi writes 則: the one a turning word, showing the drift of things; the other a settling word, showing the law's necessity. The 蚤巳 distinction likewise: the silk text's 蚤 holds both "early" and "early death": "not-Dao, and an early end" says not only that it dies quickly, but that it is cast out of the Dao. The strong-armed are the companions of death; a thing full-grown is next old — full-grown is already decline's first hour: the constant of things. The Zhuangzi: "long life, much shame"; the Liezi: "life is to be pitied, and death is to be pitied" — the same thought.

The Dao is constantly nameless; when the sage wins, it is not what he wanted — he could not help it. The work done and not dwelt in: that is whole De. The Book of Documents, the Counsels of Great Yu: "because you do not presume, none in all-under-heaven contends with you in ability; because you do not boast, none contends with you in merit"; the modest, modest noble man — the myriad people submit to him: Confucian and Daoist need each other, one gauge. So the sage fights and wins through; won through, he withdraws at once; withdrawn, he forgets; forgotten, nothing shows; nothing showing, no aftermath. Those who show off strength perish — just this.
