The Book of De · Chapter Six · The Utmost Soft

English Translation

The softest in all-under-heaven gallops through the hardest in all-under-heaven.

The formless (无有) enters where there is no gap. By this I know the gain of non-doing (无為).

The teaching without words, the gain of non-doing: few in all-under-heaven ever come up to them.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

This chapter gathers up the gain of non-doing, borrowing the utmost soft to show the Dao's use.

Of all things in all-under-heaven the softest are water and qi (气); the hardest, metal, stone, and the unbending gut. Yet water's nature runs downward and wears away the heights; wind has no form and snaps great trees. The utmost soft gallops through the utmost hard, winning not by force but by following. The silk text says 馳騁於天下之致堅, with the particle 於: the galloping takes its bearings not from oneself but from the thing: softness sent against hardness, emptiness containing the solid. It answers, from the other side, the teaching that the soft and weak defeat the hard and strong.

"The formless enters where there is no gap" presses further. 无有 is no dead vacancy: it is the subtle presence that cannot be seen by looking, heard by listening, or caught by grasping; and just so it passes through the densest and most solid without obstruction. As the saying runs, the advantage of presence is the shape of things; the use of absence is the work of the hollow. The empty refuses nothing; the void enters everything; such is the proof of the softest galloping through the hardest.

"By this I know the gain of non-doing." Non-doing works by following the transformations, and what follows the transformations contends with nothing. The sage holds this non-doing, dwells in softness and keeps to weakness; without words the teaching carries itself, without doing the work completes itself. Of the teaching without words, Wenzi has it: "the sage sits above, cherishing the Dao and not speaking, and his bounty reaches the ten thousand people."

Compare the two texts at this chapter's crux: the silk text writes 馳騁於天下之致堅, a particle fuller, its breath unhurried, the weight falling on softness's power to enter; the Wang Bi text writes 馳騁天下之至堅, swift and taut, the weight falling on softness's having no match. Both come to conquering the hard by the soft and the solid by the empty; there is no second doctrine.

It is not that stone is weaker than water: water moves with time and stone does not; the victory of soft over hard is time's victory over the rigid. The sage takes this as his law: he holds non-doing, dwells soft, keeps weak: not doing nothing, but not doing what cannot be done. Go first in your own person, and the people follow, understanding without waiting on words. When Laozi says "few in all-under-heaven ever come up to it," the difficulty lies only in being without self: with a self one cannot be soft; with a self one cannot be formless (无有). Learn this, then: softness is not cowardice but the awareness that does not contend with things; non-doing is not stillness but the wisdom that does not move against the current. The world prizes contrived doing and moves against the current to display its ability; Laozi alone follows the current and hides his De. He who follows the current is the companion of the Dao.
