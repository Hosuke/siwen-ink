The Book of De · Chapter Six · The Utmost Soft

English Translation

The softest in all-under-heaven gallops through the hardest in all-under-heaven.

The formless (无有) enters where there is no gap. By this I know the gain of non-doing (无為).

The teaching without words, the gain of non-doing: few in all-under-heaven ever come up to them.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

The silk text of this chapter follows Manuscript A (MS A); the two manuscripts are damaged at different points, 馳 is supplied from Manuscript B (MS B), and lacunae common to both are kept. Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 天下之至柔: Both manuscripts agree. MS B's 柔 was bracketed as a restoration; traces survive on the plate, and the Jicheng reads it directly. (Jicheng IV, p. 199, n. 23)
  • 馳騁於天下之致堅: MS A's 馳 is lost and is supplied from MS B. MS A has 於 and 致 where MS B has 乎 and 至; MS B's 之至, bracketed as a restoration in the 1980 edition, is read directly in the Jicheng from the mirror-image offset on the backing silk, and the graph after it is lost. For MS A's 堅 the Jicheng prints a variant form with 臣 and 攴 above 土, glossed 堅. Wang Bi reads 馳騁天下之至堅, without a preposition. (Jicheng IV, p. 199, n. 24)
  • 无有人於无閒: Follows MS A. 人 is a scribal error for 入 "to enter", which the Jicheng corrects as 人〈入〉; 閒 is read as 間 "gap". The Peking University manuscript reads 无有入於无間. MS B keeps only the final 无閒; the original editors restored 出於无有，入於无間 after the Fu Yi text, and the Jicheng leaves this unchanged but doubtful, noting that line lengths in MS B vary by as much as ten graphs, so that 无有入於无間 cannot be ruled out. Wang Bi reads 無有入無間. (Jicheng IV, p. 12, n. 36)
  • 五是以知无為之□益也: Damaged in both. MS A writes 五, read as 吾 "I"; the Jicheng reads its 之 directly, and the graph after it, 有 in Wang Bi, is lost. MS B reads 吾是以 followed by five lost graphs; its 益 is read directly from traces on the plate. Wang Bi reads 吾是以知無為之有益, without 也. (Jicheng IV, p. 199, n. 25)
  • 不□之教: Follows MS A, whose 之 the Jicheng reads directly; the graph after 不 is lost. MS B keeps only 不. Wang Bi reads 不言之教 "the teaching without words".
  • 无為之益: Follows MS A; lost entirely in MS B.
  • □下希能及之矣: The first graph is lost in both. In MS B all five graphs 下希能及之 were bracketed as restorations; Chen Jian joined a small fragment keeping the left halves of 下希, and 能及之 is read from the offset on the backing silk. Wang Bi reads 天下希及之, without 能 and 矣. (Jicheng IV, p. 199, n. 26)

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

This chapter gathers up the gain of non-doing, borrowing the utmost soft to show the Dao's use.

Of all things in all-under-heaven the softest are water and qi (氣); the hardest, metal, stone, and the unbending gut. Yet water's nature runs downward and wears away the heights; wind has no form and snaps great trees. The utmost soft gallops through the utmost hard, winning not by force but by following. Silk manuscript A says □騁於天下之致堅 (its 馳 is lost; manuscript B has 馳騁乎天下, 乎 another preposition), with the particle 於: the galloping takes its bearings not from oneself but from the thing: softness sent against hardness, emptiness containing the solid. It answers, from the other side, the teaching that the soft and weak defeat the hard and strong.

"The formless enters where there is no gap" presses further. 无有 is no dead vacancy: it is the subtle presence that cannot be seen by looking, heard by listening, or caught by grasping; and just so it passes through the densest and most solid without obstruction. As the saying runs, the advantage of presence is the shape of things; the use of absence is the work of the hollow. The empty refuses nothing; the void enters everything; such is the proof of the softest galloping through the hardest.

"By this I know the gain of non-doing." Non-doing works by following the transformations, and what follows the transformations contends with nothing. The sage holds this non-doing, dwells in softness and keeps to weakness; without words the teaching carries itself, without doing the work completes itself. Of the teaching without words, Wenzi has it: "the sage sits above, cherishing the Dao and not speaking, and his bounty reaches the ten thousand people."

Compare the two texts at this chapter's crux: manuscript A writes □騁於天下之致堅, B 馳騁乎 — a particle fuller, its breath unhurried, the weight falling on softness's power to enter; the Wang Bi text writes 馳騁天下之至堅, swift and taut, the weight falling on softness's having no match. Both come to conquering the hard by the soft and the solid by the empty; there is no second doctrine.

It is not that stone is weaker than water: water moves with time and stone does not; the victory of soft over hard is time's victory over the rigid. The sage takes this as his law: he holds non-doing, dwells soft, keeps weak: not doing nothing, but not doing what cannot be done. Go first in your own person, and the people follow, understanding without waiting on words. When Laozi says "few in all-under-heaven ever come up to it," the difficulty lies only in being without self: with a self one cannot be soft; with a self one cannot be formless (无有). Learn this, then: softness is not cowardice but the awareness that does not contend with things; non-doing is not stillness but the wisdom that does not move against the current. The world prizes contrived doing and moves against the current to display its ability; Laozi alone follows the current and hides his De. He who follows the current is the companion of the Dao.
