The Book of De · Chapter Thirty-five · Jade in the Breast

English Translation

My words are very easy to know, very easy to practice — yet no one can know them, and no one can practice them.

Words have their lord (君); works have their ancestor (宗). Only because they know nothing do they not know me.

Those who know me are few — and thereby am I precious. Hence the sage wears coarse cloth, and harbors jade (被褐懷玉).

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

"My words are very easy to know, very easy to practice — yet no one can know them, no one can practice them." It is not that the Dao is far from men; men have gone far from the Dao. Laozi's sayings are simple to the utmost and near to the utmost: not contending, keeping to the soft, returning to the root, holding unhewn wood — plain on the page, plain to grasp. That no one can know or practice them is because men are dazzled by ornament and drowned in cleverness, and so take the easy for hard, the near for far.

"Words have their lord; works have their ancestor" — the order of silk manuscript A, where manuscript B and the Wang Bi recension reverse it: 言有宗，事有君. Yaomiao takes the silk text. Why? When words go forth, a lord presides over them — their master lies in the one speaker; when works come to completion, an ancestor gathers them home — their end lies in the one root. With Wang Bi's "words have their ancestor," the ancestor stands before the words ever were — a reading slightly contorted. Words exist to carry the meaning, and the master of the meaning is the lord; works exist to get things done, and what they come home to is the ancestor. The silk text carries more.

"Only because they know nothing do they not know me." The world has no one of true knowing, and so none can know me. Laozi's sigh is not self-exaltation. The world gallops after names and sayings, drowns in cleverness, takes the Dao for a far-off thing — not knowing the utmost Dao is not far: it is right in daily use. He who keeps the Dao prizes practice over words — the same sense. He who knows does not speak; he who speaks does not know: the lord of the Dao lies in doing, not in telling.

"Those who know me are few — and thereby am I prized." The pivot of the chapter is this one graph. The silk text reads 則我貴矣, "thereby am I prized"; the Wang Bi text reads 則我者貴, "he who patterns on me is prized." The word order shifts, and the sense turns with it. In the silk text: the fewer who know me, the plainer my worth — Laozi speaking of himself. In Wang Bi: the one who models himself on me is the prized one — the sentence bends downward to prize the student. This chapter is Laozi's own testament, and Yaomiao takes the silk text's sense as the stronger: Laozi says his Dao is prized regardless of whether men know it. That few know him is not the sage's misfortune — it is all-under-heaven's. Mencius said, "the sage is of my kind," and again, "seek it and you get it; let it go and you lose it" — the sage's way was never lofty or remote; it is only that men of themselves cannot know it, cannot practice it. Here Confucian and Daoist run together.

"Hence the sage wears coarse cloth, and harbors jade." The silk text adds one 而, showing cloth and jade as each other's need, not two severed things. The 褐 is coarse wear, mean; jade is De, precious. The sage's De fills him within; without, he wears the coarse cloth — thrifty, self-veiling, unornamented. All-under-heaven, dazzled by outer show, does not know the jade inside the cloth: this is why none can know, none can practice.

Ah — the Dao's easiness is exactly its difficulty. Easy on the page, hard in the doing. The world honors ornament, the Dao honors plainness; the world honors strength, the Dao keeps to the soft; the world honors getting, the Dao prizes yielding — walking against the world's grain: that is why what is so easy to know is so hard to practice. Not a divide of clever and dull — a divide of going with or against. Men in general love talking and slight doing; they know men, and do not know the Dao. So the sage holds the Dao and hides in the age, not holding his De as De — and his De is the more prized. My words are easy to know, my way easy to walk — pity that no one walks it. The sage wears serge and harbors jade: waiting, to the end, for the ears of a later age.
