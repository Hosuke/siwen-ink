The Book of De · Chapter Thirty-two · The Three Treasures

English Translation

All-under-heaven says I am great — and resemble nothing (不宵). Precisely because I am great, I resemble nothing. Did I resemble anything, I would long since have been small.

I constantly have three treasures. The first is nurturing love (茲); the second is thrift (檢); the third is not daring to stand first in all-under-heaven.

Nurturing love makes one brave; thrift makes one broad; not daring to stand first in all-under-heaven, one can become chief of completed works (成事長).

Now — abandon nurturing and be brave, [abandon thrift and be broad,] abandon the rear and take the front: that is death, certain.

For nurturing love: in attack it wins; in defense it holds firm. When Heaven would establish someone, it is as though it walls him round with nurturing love (女以茲垣之, 女 read 如).

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

This chapter largely follows Manuscript A (MS A); the opening, the run from 三曰不敢為天下先 to 檢敢能廣, and 夫茲以單則朕, all damaged in MS A, are supplied from Manuscript B (MS B). Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 天下□胃我大大而不宵: The opening of the chapter is lost in MS A; the original editors restored it from MS B and received texts, and the Jicheng replaces this with a mark of indeterminate lacuna. The text follows MS B, which has a repetition mark after 大, to be read 天下□胃我大，大而不宵; the graph after 下 is lost. 胃 is read as 謂 "to say", 宵 as 肖 "to resemble". Wang Bi reads 天下皆謂我道大，似不肖. (Jicheng IV, p. 34, n. 156)
  • 夫唯 / 故不宵: MS A reads 夫唯【大】，故不宵, with 大 lost; MS B reads 夫唯不宵，故能大, exchanging the two halves. The Peking University manuscript agrees with MS A; most received texts add 似 after 故, Wang Bi reading 夫唯大，故似不肖. (Jicheng IV, p. 34, n. 157; p. 203, n. 133)
  • 若宵細久矣: Follows MS A. MS B and the Peking University manuscript read 若宵，久矣亓細也夫; Wang Bi agrees with MS B (若肖，久矣其細也夫), and no received text has MS A's wording. (Jicheng IV, p. 34, n. 158)
  • 我恒有三葆之: Follows MS A. The original editors note that MS B reads 市（持）而寶之, as does the received text, and that MS A has dropped three graphs, 市而𤥯, which the Jicheng supplies as a restoration. MS B reads 我恆有三𤥯，市而𤥯之, with 市 read as 持 "to hold"; the Peking University manuscript writes 葆 for both treasure-graphs. For the graph before 之, Wang Bi and many texts have 保 "to keep", Heshang Gong, Fu Yi and others 寶 "to treasure", and the Jicheng follows the 1980 edition in reading it as 寶. Wang Bi reads 我有三寶，持而保之. (Jicheng IV, p. 34, n. 159)
  • 一曰茲 / 二曰檢: Both manuscripts agree; 茲 is read as 慈 "nurturing love", 檢 as 儉 "thrift".
  • 三曰不敢為天下先 / 夫茲 / 故能男 / 檢敢能廣: These lines are lost in MS A except for 故能廣; they follow MS B. MS B's 男 is read as 勇 "brave". For the 敢 of 檢敢能廣 MS A has 故; MS B's 敢 is presumably a slip induced by the surrounding 不敢. Wang Bi reads 慈故能勇，儉故能廣.
  • 不敢為天下先 / 故能為成事長: Follows MS A; MS B has 器 for 事, and the Peking University manuscript, like MS B, reads 故能為成器長. The original editors note that the Han Feizi ("Jie Lao") quotes the line as in MS A, while the received text has 故能成器長; Fan Yingyuan's and Sima Guang's texts have 為 after 能, agreeing with the silk and Peking University manuscripts. (Jicheng IV, p. 35, n. 160)
  • 今舍亓茲且勇 / 舍亓後且先: Follows MS A. All other texts have, after 舍亓茲且勇, the line 舍亓儉且廣 "abandon thrift and be broad", which MS A omits and the Jicheng supplies as a restoration; MS B reads 今舍亓茲且男，舍亓檢且廣，舍亓後且先. Since the silk text here follows MS A, the line is absent. (Jicheng IV, p. 35, n. 161)
  • 則必死矣: Follows MS A; MS B lacks 必, and Wang Bi has simply 死矣.
  • 夫茲以單則朕: In MS A 以單 is lost; follows MS B. MS A has 勝 where MS B has 朕. 茲 is read as 慈, 單 as 戰 "to do battle", 朕 as 勝 "to win". Received texts variously read 以戰則勝, 以陳則正 and 以陳則勝. (Jicheng IV, p. 35, n. 162)
  • 天將建之女以茲垣之: Follows MS A; MS B has 如 for 女, and 女 is read as 如 "as if". The original editors note that the received text has 救 "to rescue" for 建 and suggest that MS A's 建 is a copying error for the similar-looking 悈; the Jicheng considers this doubtful. The Peking University manuscript reads 天之救之. The original editors also note that the received text lacks 女 and has 衛 "to guard" for 垣, citing the Shiming: "垣 is 援, that on which men rely for protection"; the Peking University manuscript reads 若以茲衛之. Wang Bi reads 天將救之，以慈衛之. (Jicheng IV, p. 35, nn. 163, 164)

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

"All-under-heaven says I am great — and resemble nothing": the sense of "the great square has no corners," and in this chapter Laozi names his own three treasures, by which he keeps himself and orders the world.

That the Dao is great and resembles nothing: as a thing, it has in itself no form or name to be likened to. Whatever can be likened has an outline; whatever has an outline has lost its greatness. 肖 is resemblance: to resemble is to be bounded; the Dao, unbounded, is beyond likeness. The sage knows the great and keeps it, never chasing it in petty shapes and images.

"I constantly have three treasures": the first is nurturing love, the second thrift, the third not daring to stand first in all-under-heaven. Both silk manuscripts say 恆有, "constantly have," where Wang Bi has only 我有三寶, without 恆. Manuscript B goes on 市而𤥯之, 市 read as 持, matching Wang Bi's 持而保之, "hold and preserve"; manuscript A reads 我恒有三葆之, and the first editors judged that it has dropped the three graphs 市而𤥯. To constantly have is to be complete in oneself, nothing added from outside; to hold and preserve is to grip and not let go: near to having an attachment. The three treasures are not objects the sage clutches; they are what the Dao inherently has, coursing through the heart. This sense shows in the silk text's 恆; and manuscript A, though it may have dropped a phrase, speaks only of constantly having and not of holding, which brings the point out all the more.

Of the three, nurturing love comes first. The Great Learning says "as if protecting a naked babe"; Mencius says "the heart of pity is the sprout of humaneness" — the Confucian books on 仁. But Laozi's 慈 issues from the self-so: it does not wait on the ladder of kin-love, does not borrow the teaching of human bonds — it is the heart that takes the ten thousand things as one body. So "nurturing, one can be brave" (MS B; 男 is read as 勇): not the bravery of blood and temper, but the bravery of the unselfish. Nurturing love knows no dread — seeing another drowning as drowning oneself, it walks into peril without refusal. Husbandry lies in stillness and spending little; spend little, and the spirit stays whole — is not nurturing's gathering-in the very root of bravery?

Thrift, 檢 — that is 儉. "Thrifty, one can be broad": not mere penny-pinching, but the gathering-in of De until its reach goes everywhere. Water filling an empty valley — great, and nothing excluded: such is the breadth of thrift.

Not daring to stand first in all-under-heaven is the way of putting oneself behind — hence not contending, hence no one can contend. "Able to become chief of completed works": silk manuscript A says 成事長; manuscript B and Wang Bi, 成器長. 事 is the unfolding of the work; 器 is the finished object. The silk text stays near the power that presides; Wang Bi stays near the name of the thing made. Savor it, and the silk text carries more — he who dares not go first arrives at the front from behind: not standing over men, but leading men and works without taking the head.

Now abandon nurturing and be brave, abandon thrift and be broad, abandon the rear and take the front: death, certain. Remove one of the three and the root is gone: bravery without nurturing is a ruffian's rashness; breadth without thrift is rootless scattering; front without rear is the road to overturning.

"Nurturing love: in attack it wins, in defense it holds firm. When Heaven would establish someone, it is as though it walls him round with nurturing love." The silk text says 建, "establish," and 垣, "wall round"; Wang Bi says 救, "rescue," and 衛, "defend." To establish is active founding; to wall round is to make nurturing love the rampart around him: more active and more concrete than Wang Bi's rescue-and-defend. Heaven does not passively save a man: it actively founds him and, as it were, rings him with nurturing love. This is the utmost sense of "loving his own body for the sake of all-under-heaven" — the final word of the Daoist learning of 慈.
