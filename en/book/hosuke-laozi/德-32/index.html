The Book of De · Chapter Thirty-two · The Three Treasures

English Translation

All-under-heaven says I am great — and resemble nothing (不宵). Precisely because I am great, I resemble nothing. Did I resemble anything, I would long since have been small.

I constantly have three treasures. The first is nurturing love (茲); the second is thrift (檢); the third is not daring to stand first in all-under-heaven.

Nurturing love makes one brave; thrift makes one broad; not daring to stand first in all-under-heaven, one can become chief of completed works (成事長).

Now — abandon nurturing and be brave, abandon thrift and be broad, abandon the rear and take the front: that is death, certain.

For nurturing love: in attack it wins; in defense it holds firm. When Heaven would establish someone, it is as though it walls him round with nurturing love (如以茲垣之).

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

"All-under-heaven says I am great — and resemble nothing": the sense of "the great square has no corners," and in this chapter Laozi names his own three treasures, by which he keeps himself and orders the world.

That the Dao is great and resembles nothing: as a thing, it has in itself no form or name to be likened to. Whatever can be likened has an outline; whatever has an outline has lost its greatness. 肖 is resemblance: to resemble is to be bounded; the Dao, unbounded, is beyond likeness. The sage knows the great and keeps it, never chasing it in petty shapes and images.

"I constantly have three treasures": the first is nurturing love, the second thrift, the third not daring to stand first in all-under-heaven. Silk manuscript A says 恆有, "constantly have"; manuscript B and the Wang Bi text say 持而保之, "hold and preserve." To constantly have is to be complete in oneself, nothing added from outside; to hold and preserve is to grip and not let go: near to having an attachment. The three treasures are not objects the sage clutches; they are what the Dao inherently has, coursing through the heart. Here manuscript A has the better of it.

Of the three, nurturing love comes first. The Great Learning says "as if protecting a naked babe"; Mencius says "the heart of pity is the sprout of humaneness" — the Confucian books on 仁. But Laozi's 慈 issues from the self-so: it does not wait on the ladder of kin-love, does not borrow the teaching of human bonds — it is the heart that takes the ten thousand things as one body. So "nurturing, one can be brave": not the bravery of blood and temper, but the bravery of the unselfish. Nurturing love knows no dread — seeing another drowning as drowning oneself, it walks into peril without refusal. Husbandry lies in stillness and spending little; spend little, and the spirit stays whole — is not nurturing's gathering-in the very root of bravery?

Thrift, 檢 — that is 儉. "Thrifty, one can be broad": not mere penny-pinching, but the gathering-in of De until its reach goes everywhere. Water filling an empty valley — great, and nothing excluded: such is the breadth of thrift.

Not daring to stand first in all-under-heaven is the way of putting oneself behind — hence not contending, hence no one can contend. "Able to become chief of completed works": silk manuscript A says 成事長; manuscript B and Wang Bi, 成器長. 事 is the unfolding of the work; 器 is the finished object. The silk text stays near the power that presides; Wang Bi stays near the name of the thing made. Savor it, and the silk text carries more — he who dares not go first arrives at the front from behind: not standing over men, but leading men and works without taking the head.

Now abandon nurturing and be brave, abandon thrift and be broad, abandon the rear and take the front: death, certain. Remove one of the three and the root is gone: bravery without nurturing is a ruffian's rashness; breadth without thrift is rootless scattering; front without rear is the road to overturning.

"Nurturing love: in attack it wins, in defense it holds firm. When Heaven would establish someone, it is as though it walls him round with nurturing love." The silk text says 建, "establish," and 垣, "wall round"; Wang Bi says 救, "rescue," and 衛, "defend." To establish is active founding; to wall round is to make nurturing love the rampart around him: more active and more concrete than Wang Bi's rescue-and-defend. Heaven does not passively save a man: it actively founds him and, as it were, rings him with nurturing love. This is the utmost sense of "loving his own body for the sake of all-under-heaven" — the final word of the Daoist learning of 慈.
