The Book of Dao · Chapter Thirty-two · Knowing Where to Stop

English Translation

The Dao is constantly nameless (道恆无名).

Unhewn wood (楃), however small — nothing in all-under-heaven dares make it a subject.

If lords and kings can keep it, the ten thousand things will come as guests of themselves. Heaven and earth join in harmony and send down the sweet dew (甘洛): the people, with no one commanding them, even out of themselves.

When ordering begins, names arise (始制有名) — names, once there are names, one must also know where to stop (知止). Knowing where to stop is how one escapes peril.

The Dao's being in all-under-heaven is as little valley-streams are to the rivers and seas (猶小浴之與江海).

Commentary

Yaomiao says: this chapter takes the Dao's namelessness for its ancestor, shows the crux of lords and kings keeping it, and comes home to the warning of knowing where to stop — head and tail answering, the sense complete.

The Dao is constantly nameless. The silk text writes 恆, Wang Bi 常 — the old graphs pass for each other, no difference of sense. The Dao's substance is chaos undivided, with no name to name it, hence nameless.

"Unhewn wood, however small — nothing in all-under-heaven dares make it a subject." The silk text writes 楃, Wang Bi 樸 — old graphs passing for each other, both naming the Dao's plainness not yet split. Small means the Dao is hidden and minute, hard to see; "nothing dares make it a subject" means heaven, earth, and the ten thousand things cannot subdue it. Wang Bi's "unhewn wood, though small — nothing in all-under-heaven can make it a subject" is one sense with this. Small, yet it cannot be bent — exactly "weakness is the use of the Dao": the great breaks easily; the small keeps whole. This is turning-back, the Dao's movement. "If lords and kings can keep it, the ten thousand things will come as guests of themselves": keep the Dao and do not lose it, and the ten thousand things, receiving heaven's mechanism of transformation, each rest in their own nature and charge — none commanding them, and yet always self-so, echo answering shadow. This is what that means.

"Heaven and earth join in harmony and send down the sweet dew" — the silk text writes 俞甘洛, Wang Bi 降甘露: 俞 presumably borrows for 降 — both say heaven and earth in concord, the sweet moisture descending of itself. "The people, with no one commanding them, even out of themselves" — no instructions issued, and they level out of themselves: the proof of governing by non-doing.

"When ordering begins, names arise — names, once there are names, one must also know where to stop. Knowing where to stop is how one escapes peril." Once institutions are set and names and vessels established, what matters is knowing where to stop; not knowing it, one oversteps, and peril and ruin follow — the sage's warning. The Book of Changes: "he who knows advance and retreat, survival and ruin, and does not lose the straight" — the same thought; Confucian and Daoist light each other here.

"The Dao's being in all-under-heaven is as little valley-streams are to the rivers and seas." The silk text writes 小浴, Wang Bi 川谷, Heshang Gong also 川谷: 浴 presumably borrows for 谷 — valley, ravine, stream, one sense, all naming the small valleys. The valley is empty and so receives the hundred streams; the Dao is empty and so holds the ten thousand things. Each little stream runs its own course and comes home to the great sea; the Dao, nameless and whole, holds the ten thousand names in their differences — this is "heaven patterns on the Dao; the Dao patterns on the self-so." The sage patterns on it: good at lying low, and so all-under-heaven comes home to him. "The valley: empty, and so it can receive" — the same sense.

So then: know the unhewn namelessness, keep it and do not lose it; know where to stop, and no peril; the ten thousand things come as guests of themselves — and all-under-heaven can be transformed sitting down. Let the student turn these three over with care.
