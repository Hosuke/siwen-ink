The Book of Dao · Chapter Twenty-one · Following the Dao

English Translation

The bearing of great De (孔德之容): it follows the Dao alone.

The Dao as a thing: only indistinct, only far (唯朢唯忽).

Indistinct, ah, far, ah — within it there is image. Far, ah, indistinct, ah — within it there is thing. Dark, ah, murmuring, ah — within it there is inner truth (中有請). The truth is utterly real; within it there is faithfulness (信).

From today back to antiquity, its name has never departed — by it, one accords with the fathers of the many (以順眾父).

How do I know the fathers of the many are so? By this.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

The Dao cannot be had by form or name — yet the sage would open the way for later learners with words, and this is why the five thousand words of Laozi were written at all. This chapter of the silk text, the twenty-first of the Wang Bi recension, is the older in its wording; let the evidence be weighed before the doctrine.

"The bearing of great De: it follows the Dao alone." De is getting — the Dao lodged in a person's heart. The looks and movements of De, every one of them, issue from the Dao. 孔 is great; the man of utmost De, in every motion and rest, is one with the Dao. So De cannot make itself: it follows the Dao alone — the point of "the highest De does not hold to De."

"The Dao as a thing: only indistinct, only far" — the picture of the Dao as indistinct and far, deep, past all probing. The silk text writes 朢 and 忽, the Wang Bi recension 恍 and 惚 — near in sound, passing for each other. The Dao as a thing cannot be seen by looking or heard by listening; pressed for a name, we say "indistinct and far" and leave it there.

"Indistinct, ah, far, ah — within it there is image; far, ah, indistinct, ah — within it there is thing": the Dao is no sheer emptiness. When Laozi speaks of indistinct-and-far he does not mean vacant nothing — he means the emptiness that holds the ten thousand there-ares: not empty nothing, but the body they all come out of. Within the indistinct-and-far there are image and thing, so the Dao does not fall into annihilation.

"Dark, ah, murmuring, ah — within it there is inner truth; the truth is utterly real; within it there is faithfulness." 請 is the silk text's graph, borrowing for 情: the Dao's nature-as-it-is, true and unfeigned. The Dao is no poet's daydream — there is something real there to be verified. And here the deepest weighing: the Wang Bi text writes 精, "essence," for 請. 精 is the refined and subtle; 情 is the true nature. The silk text speaks of the Dao by 情 — showing the Dao has feeling and faithfulness, answering to the human heart; Wang Bi speaks by 精 — the subtle and marvelous. Both pass; the silk text is the more direct — feeling is the heart's issuing, and the Dao's feeling is the Dao's truth.

The last part: "from today back to antiquity, its name has never departed — by it, one accords with the fathers of the many." The silk text writes 順, "answer and accord," and 父, "fathers," where Wang Bi has 閱, "survey," and 甫, "beginnings." Surveying the many beginnings is looking at the start of things by the Dao; according with the many fathers is attending on the root of things by the Dao. The name shifts with the ages; what it points to has never changed. "According" runs deeper than "surveying": not merely watching, but according with it and coming home.

The last line: "how do I know the fathers of the many are so?" — Wang Bi has "the look of the many beginnings." 然, "so," and 狀, "look," are near in sense: both speak of how the root of things comes to be as it is.

In sum: the crux is that within the Dao's indistinct-and-far there is an inner truth — and this truth is the Dao's faithfulness. 信 is sincerity. The Doctrine of the Mean: "sincerity is the end and beginning of things; without sincerity there are no things" — one light with "within it there is faithfulness." Confucian and Daoist come home to one here: sincerity and faith for the root. The art of cultivating the Dao: empty the heart to behold the Dao's indistinct-and-far, and reach sincerity to trust the Dao's inner truth — the heart-to-heart teaching of the old sages.
