The Book of De · Chapter Eight · Clear Stillness (請靚)

English Translation

The great completion seems chipped: its use does not wear out. The great fullness seems hollow: its use does not run out. The great straightness seems bent. The great skill seems clumsy. The great surplus seems a guttering flame (㶧).

Bustle defeats cold; stillness defeats heat. Clear stillness (請靚) can set all-under-heaven right.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

This chapter lays out the five greatnesses that seem their opposites: the face of the utmost Dao, always contrary to common expectation. The silk text says the great completion seems chipped, its use does not wear out: one sense with Wang Bi's 不弊. The vessel most complete looks lacking from outside; complete yet seeming chipped, its use never wears. For what seems full must spill, and what seems whole must break. The great fullness seems hollow, its use never exhausted: full yet seeming hollow, so the use has no end; a vessel's usefulness lies in its hollow, not in its solid. This is the teaching that presence gives the advantage and absence gives the use. The great straightness seems bent (詘; Wang Bi writes 屈, the graphs interchanging): straightness at its utmost looks crooked. The great skill seems clumsy: skill at its utmost does not show its skill. The baldly straight get wounded; the visibly skillful get beaten. The sage is straight without display and skillful without show, not from guile but from following things to their end.

Then the silk text has 大贏如㶧, "the great surplus seems a guttering flame," where Wang Bi has 大辯若訥, "the great eloquence seems halting": the weightiest divergence between the two texts here. 㶧 is the faintness of a flame: the utmost abundance shows from outside as a low flicker, one pattern with "completion seems chipped" and "fullness seems hollow": the utmost of a thing wears the face of its reverse. Wang Bi's "eloquence seems halting" swerves into the realm of speech, out of kind with the three lines before it. Eloquence is a matter of words; abundance is a matter of things; the chapter's fabric tears exactly there. Follow the silk text.

As for "bustle defeats cold, stillness defeats heat": movement to drive off cold, stillness to master heat. The silk text writes 請靚: 請 borrows for 情, the genuine state; 靚 is the old graph of clear stillness. Joined, they say the Dao's genuine state lies in clear stillness: not a cultivated quality of quietness to be merely kept, but the Dao's own substance proving itself there. Wang Bi's "clear stillness sets all-under-heaven right" is plain and loses the resonance; the silk text makes it clear that stillness is not held in place by human effort; the Dao shows itself in it. That is its superiority. Heshang Gong, commenting on the highest-De chapter, says the sovereign of highest De governs by non-doing: the very point this chapter makes of bringing non-doing to the ordering of all-under-heaven.

Therefore keep the chipped as if it were whole; dwell in the hollow as if it were full; this is how the Dao is great. The sage seeks it all in himself: he does not display his completion, does not flaunt his fullness, does not flourish his straightness; and completion, fullness, and straightness are all there within. Watch this chapter and you see that Laozi's learning is not in the outward form, but in turning the gaze back and lighting the inside.
