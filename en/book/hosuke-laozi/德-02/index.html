The Book of De · Chapter Two · Getting the One

English Translation

Of old, these got the One (一 yī): heaven got the One and was clear; earth got the One and was steady; the spirits got the One and were numinous (霝); the valley-streams (浴) got the One and were full; lords and kings got the One and set all-under-heaven right.

Push it to the end: heaven, clear without cease, may crack; earth, steady without cease, may quake; the spirits, numinous without cease, may give out; the valleys, full without cease, may run dry; lords and kings, honored and high without cease, may topple.

Hence the honored must take the lowly as root; the high must take the low as base.

This is why lords and kings name themselves "the orphan," "the widowed," "the unprovisioned." Is this not the lowly serving as root? It is nothing less.

Hence to tally up honors is to have no honor. Therefore desire not to be lustrous like jade, but rough and hard like stone.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

The silk text of this chapter follows Manuscript A (MS A), with its lacunae supplied from Manuscript B (MS B); the lines 地毋巳寧 and 侯王毋巳貴 and the final line follow MS B. Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 昔之得一者: Follows MS A; MS B lacks 之.
  • 浴得一以盈: Both manuscripts write 浴; MS B lacks 以. Wang Bi has 谷 "valley".
  • 侯王得一以為天下正: Follows MS B. MS A reads 侯□□□而以為正, with 而 but without 天下, and the spacing leaves no room for those two graphs. Before this line Wang Bi has 萬物得一以生 ("the ten thousand things got the One and lived"), and below it 萬物無以生將恐滅; neither silk manuscript has either line. Wang Bi writes 貞 for 正.
  • 亓致之也: Follows MS A; MS B reads 亓至也. In MS A each of the five following lines opens with 胃 (read 謂); in MS B only the first does. (Jicheng IV, p. 8, n. 14)
  • 胃天毋巳清將恐連: The graph after 恐 is mostly lost in MS A; the original editors left it unread and restored 裂, but Guo Yongbing identified the surviving right side as 車 and read 連, which the Jicheng adopts. MS B writes 蓮; both are read as 裂 "to crack". The 1980 edition read 巳 throughout as 已; the Jicheng transcribes 巳, glossed 已, here and in the four lines that follow. (Jicheng IV, p. 8, n. 14)
  • 地毋巳寧將恐發: Follows MS B. MS A reads 胃地毋□□將恐發, opening with 胃; the Jicheng reads 發 directly.
  • 胃神毋巳霝將恐歇: Follows MS A. MS B lacks 胃; its 巳靈, bracketed as a restoration in the 1980 edition, is read directly in the Jicheng from the mirror-image offset on the backing silk. Here MS B writes 靈, unlike the 霝 of its earlier 神得一以霝; its 將 is lost. (Jicheng IV, p. 197, n. 4)
  • 胃谷毋巳盈將將恐渴: Follows MS A. This 谷 stands hard against a break in the silk, and the spacing leaves no room for a water radical; the 1980 edition read 浴（谷）, the Jicheng plain 谷. 將 is written twice, and the Jicheng marks the second as dittography. MS B reads 谷毋巳□□渴: the two graphs between 毋巳 and 渴 are too damaged to identify; the 1980 edition restored 盈恐, the Jicheng gives two lacunae. Both silk manuscripts and the Peking University manuscript have 渴; received texts have 竭. (Jicheng IV, p. 9, n. 16; p. 197, n. 5)
  • 侯王毋巳貴以高將恐欮: Follows MS B. MS A reads 胃侯王毋巳貴 and breaks off; its final graph keeps one horizontal stroke at the lower left, which the Jicheng judges not to belong to 欮 and transcribes as □（蹶）. Wang Bi reads 侯王無以貴高將恐蹶. (Jicheng IV, p. 9, n. 17)
  • 故必貴而以賤為本 / 必高矣而以下為𡉝: Follows MS A; MS B lacks 而 in the first line. 𡉝 is written with 亓 above 土, as in the Chu bamboo manuscripts and the ancient forms of the Hanjian; the 1980 edition read it directly as 基, while the Jicheng keeps the form and glosses it 基. MS B writes 土 and 亓 side by side. Wang Bi reads 故貴以賤為本，高以下為基. (Jicheng IV, p. 9, n. 18)
  • 夫是以侯王自胃曰孤寡不槀: Follows MS A. 曰 was bracketed as a restoration; the color photographs show the right half of its bottom stroke, and the Jicheng reads it directly. MS B has no 曰 after 胃. In both manuscripts 槀 is written 高 over 木 and is read as 穀; Wang Bi has 是以侯王自稱孤寡不穀. (Jicheng IV, p. 10, n. 19)
  • 此亓賤之本與 / 非也: Follows MS B. In MS A 之本與 is lost; the graph after 本 keeps only its right side, which may be 與 or 輿, and the Jicheng transcribes □（歟）. Wang Bi reads 此非以賤為本邪？非乎？ (Jicheng IV, p. 10, n. 20)
  • 故至數輿无輿是故不欲祿祿若玉硌硌若石: Follows MS B, whose 祿 and 硌 each carry a repetition mark, here written out. MS A reads 故致數與无與; the graph before 若玉 and the words 若石 are lost. The 1980 edition saw a repetition mark after MS A's 硌; the Jicheng finds it lost and corrects the earlier reading. 輿 and 與 are both read as 譽 "praise". Wang Bi reads 故致數輿無輿，不欲琭琭如玉，珞珞如石, without 是故 and with 如 for 若. (Jicheng IV, p. 10, n. 22)

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

Getting the One is the guiding thread of Laozi's whole book, and this chapter is its keenest statement. Of old, those that got the One (heaven, earth, spirits, valleys, lords and kings) all received the Dao to live, all leaned on the One to endure. The Dao is that by which the ten thousand things are so, that in which the ten thousand patterns are checked; the Dao disperses into the One, and the One is the ancestor of every transformation.

Now set the silk text against the Wang Bi recension, and the differences carry meaning at several points. 霝 against 靈: the silk text writes 霝 (rain above, falling drops below; the Shuowen: "rain coming down in drops"), a graph that still holds a picture of the sky at work. Wang Bi's 靈 hangs the shamans (巫) beneath it: the business of spirits crowds out the image of weather, a step further from nature. 浴 against 谷: the silk text's 浴 (in 浴得一以盈; the later line 胃谷毋巳盈 has 谷 in both manuscripts, as in Wang Bi) holds both bathing and hollow valley, a washing power; Wang Bi's 谷 is only a vessel that receives, and the sense has narrowed. And the line "the ten thousand things got the One and lived" is absent from the silk text. Heaven, earth, spirits, valleys, lords and kings are the great instances of heaven, earth and man; these five terms already contain the ten thousand things. Wang Bi's addition blunts the economy.

Weightiest of all is 正 against 貞. The silk text writes 正, to stand in one's proper place: lords and kings pattern themselves on the Dao, and the ten thousand things each stand right in their allotments. Wang Bi writes 貞, which shades toward the loyal minister's constancy and falls into political ethics. One graph changed, and a discourse on the Way of Heaven became a discourse on the way of kings. Here you can watch later exegesis drifting toward the teaching of names.

As for "the honored takes the lowly as root" and "orphan, widowed, unprovisioned" (both silk manuscripts write 不槀, read as 不穀): these are the sage's discipline of dwelling low. When lords and kings call themselves orphan, widowed, unprovisioned, it is not self-abasement; it is knowing that the lowly is the root of the honored, the low the base of the high. Reversal is the Dao's movement: in one person it is called humility; in one state it is called taking the lowly as root; the gauge is the same. To tally up honors is to have none: things arrive and are counted, the count peaks and the praise is spent; better the wholeness of no praise. So the sage keeps to substance, not to sheen: he does not wish to be lustrous like jade, but rough and hard like stone.
