The Book of De · Chapter Thirty-seven · Fearing What Is to Be Feared

English Translation

When the people do not fear what is to be feared (畏畏), the great fear is on its way.

Do not bar their dwellings (毋閘亓所居); do not oppress their livelihood (毋猒亓所生). Only by not oppressing them is one not loathed.

Hence the sage knows himself, but does not show himself; cherishes himself, but does not exalt himself. He leaves that and takes this.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

Chapter thirty-seven of the Book of De — this chapter is titled "Fearing What Is to Be Feared." Its point cuts deep: the utmost warning for those who govern.

Set against the Wang Bi recension, there are three differences; here is what matters in them.

First, 民之不畏畏: Wang Bi writes 民不畏威, "when the people do not fear the majesty." 畏畏 is to hold in awe what ought to be held in awe — the self-so order of the Way of Heaven, of rightness and pattern. 威 is external force: majesty, punishment. The silk text says: when the people lose their inward awe, the great fear arrives. Wang Bi says: when the people do not fear penal force, a greater force comes. The depths plainly part: the silk text is a doctrine of the moral self-so; Wang Bi, a calculus of rule. This fork in Laozi's root meaning must not be blurred.

Second, 毋閘亓所居: Wang Bi writes 無狎其所居. 閘 is to bar, to block up; 狎 is to take liberties with, to treat lightly. The silk text bids the ruler not encroach on the people's land, not block up their homes — let them have a settled place; Wang Bi bids him not slight their dwellings. Concrete government against abstract admonition — which is the weightier, anyone can see. The Heshang Gong text also has the line, as 無狹其所居, and comments on it — so the silk text's 閘, with its concrete sense of blocking up, stands alone among the recensions.

Third, "knows himself, but does not show himself": Wang Bi drops the linking 而. To show oneself (自見) is to flaunt outward; to know oneself is the light turned within. To exalt oneself (自貴) is to set oneself above others; to cherish oneself (自愛) is to hold oneself dear without pride. Four in file: the governor first drops the private itch to shine and dominate — only then can he reach the people.

The chapter's crux: "Only by not oppressing them is one not loathed." When those above do not oppress the people's life, the people do not loathe the rule above; high and low at peace with each other — the goodness of non-doing. Reverse it: press and harry the people with majesty and punishment until they have no settled dwelling and no work to love, and the people lose their awe — and the great fear arrives.

Seen from here, the sage's governing lies not in the sternness of majesty but in letting the people each be settled in their homes and fulfilled in their lives — governing his own self first, and only then reaching the people. This is Laozi's root intent, and it is the silk text alone that keeps it true.
