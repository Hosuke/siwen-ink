The Book of De · Chapter One · On De

English Translation

The highest De (德 dé) does not hold to De, and so it has De. The lower De never lets go of De, and so it is without De.

The highest De is non-doing (无為 wuwei: not doing nothing, but doing nothing contrived), without an end in view. The highest humaneness (仁 rén) acts, yet without an end in view. The highest rightness (義 yì) acts, and with an end in view. The highest rite (禮 lǐ) acts, and when none respond it bares an arm and hauls them in.

Hence: the Dao (道 dào) lost, then De; De lost, then humaneness; humaneness lost, then rightness; rightness lost, then rite. As for rite, it is loyalty and good faith run shallow, and the beginning of disorder.

Foreknowing is the flower of the Dao, and the beginning of folly.

Therefore the great man dwells in the thick, not in the shallows; dwells in the fruit, not in the flower. He leaves that and takes this.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

This first chapter of the Mawangdui silk text is Laozi's own statement of his theme. Most readers know the Daodejing through the Wang Bi recension, which begins with 道可道, "the Dao that can be spoken." But both silk manuscripts, text A and text B, place De (德) before the Dao (道). This is no copyist's error; it is Laozi's own intent. The Dao names the empty vastness (太虛); De is its trace as it takes form in things. One cannot enter the empty vastness except through the trace. So Laozi begins with De, and the four words he opens with land like a wake-up blow: 上德不德, the highest De does not hold to De.

De means getting (得): it is the Dao dispersed and received in the person. In the highest De, the Dao is whole and De complete, yet the person has no mind of De, and so "does not hold to De." It is not that there is no De; De is in him — a name he never names. For the highest De forgets De, and so completes De. The lower De, by contrast, grasps at it, afraid of losing it, deliberately seeking it; and at that grasping, De has already departed. This is the sage's learning of no-mind, wholly other than the common habit of parading one's De.

Then the chapter names four descending grades. The highest De: non-doing, without an end in view. The sovereign of highest De empties his heart, lets things be, and answers without fixed form; when the work is done and the matter is complete, no one knows whose doing it was. Hence "without an end in view." The highest humaneness acts, yet without an end in view. Humaneness is the aching fellow-feeling (惻怛) of a heart not yet free from feeling; it wishes to act for the world's nurture, but its heart is tranquil: 泊然, the same graph as this chapter's shallow water, here in its serene sense. There is no private urge to see its own effect. The highest rightness acts, and with an end in view. Rightness is the fit of judgment; it already has a mind toward giving and return, hence "with an end in view." Intention shows, and one departs further from the Dao. The highest rite acts, and when none respond, it bares an arm and hauls them in. Rite is the form of ceremonial fitness, compelling the people to conform. At that point no De remains to speak of.

These four are a descending sequence in Laozi's description, not his condemnation. Think of a mountain spring flowing down: at the source, clear; passing stones, turbulent; entering the brook, streaming; reaching the sea, it becomes the wave. The wave is still the spring, only farther from its source. The student traces upstream to the source and does not revile what lies below.

The silk text has 忠信之泊; Wang Bi has 忠信之薄. 泊 is water near exhaustion; 薄 is merely thin. Thinness can still be thickened; shallow water is about to run dry. Laozi's warning lands in that single character (see the collation niche above).

Foreknowing (前識) is anticipatory calculation: the flower of the Dao, the beginning of folly. 華 is the bloom: the Dao's outer ornament. To take the tip and abandon the root is where folly begins. The great man, one who has penetrated to the Dao, knows the flower cannot stand as the Dao, and so 去彼取此, "leaves that, takes this": he dwells in thickness and substance, not in shallowness and flower.

The Confucian teaching of subduing oneself and returning to rite (克己復禮) can be read alongside this. Subduing oneself is the practice of diminishing; illuminating the bright De (明明德) is the return to one's original brightness. Confucians take rite as the destination; Daoists take non-doing as the destination. Both are operations of subtraction, and in this their return is one; only the path differs. So this chapter does not teach one to discard rite, rightness, humaneness. It teaches one not to grasp rite as rite, not to grasp rightness as rightness, not to grasp humaneness as humaneness. To grasp is to thin; to transform is to thicken.

Writers on government, ancient and modern, have abandoned non-doing and prized broad giving; they cast off root and mother and use the child; they chase names in pursuit of substance, pursue the tips and forget the source. When names are established, substance is more lost; when flowers display, thickness is more hidden. Loyalty and good faith running shallow, and disorder beginning (忠信之泊，而亂之首): you see it exactly here. So the sage dwells in thickness and not in shallowness, in substance and not in flower: he leaves that and takes this, and with the highest De that does not hold to De contains all-under-heaven.
