The Book of De · Chapter Seven · Setting the Precept

English Translation

Name or body: which is nearer? Body or goods: which counts for more? Getting or losing: which does the greater harm?

Excess […]; much hoarding […] loss.

Hence: knowing what is enough, no disgrace; knowing where to stop, no peril; and one can last long.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

Manuscript B (MS B) is almost entirely lost in this chapter, so the silk text follows Manuscript A (MS A), keeping its lacunae where it too is damaged. Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 名與身孰親: Follows MS A; MS B keeps only 名與, and the rest of the chapter is lost in it.
  • 身與傎孰多: MS A writes 貨 "goods" as a graph of 人 beside 真, a miswriting of a similar form; the 1980 edition simply printed 貨, while the Jicheng transcribes the form as written and corrects it as 傎〈貨〉. Wang Bi reads 身與貨孰多. (Jicheng IV, p. 12, n. 39)
  • 得與亡孰病: Follows MS A, as does Wang Bi.
  • 甚□□□□ / 多臧□□亡: Damaged in both. MS A keeps 甚, 多臧 and 亡; the Jicheng reads 多臧 directly, 臧 being read as 藏 "to hoard". There is no room in MS A for 是故 before 甚. Wang Bi reads 是故甚愛必大費，多藏必厚亡. Both lines are lost in MS B; the Jicheng keeps the 1980 restoration for now, noting that the Peking University manuscript and many received texts have 是故 before them, that MS B probably had it too, and that the restored text may need to move further down the line. (Jicheng IV, p. 199, n. 27)
  • 故知足不辱: Follows MS A; Wang Bi lacks 故.
  • 知止不殆 / 可以長久: Follows MS A, as does Wang Bi; lost in MS B.

Commentary

This chapter is Laozi teaching the weighing of light and heavy. Load not the body with floating name and empty praise. Three questions, each biting deeper. Name or body, which is nearer: the division of spirit; a body cannot be bought back with a name. Body or goods, which is more: the division of inner and outer; a body cannot be traded for things. Getting or losing, which is the sickness: the division of survival and ruin. And the three close on a single question: to win the name and lose the body, gain or loss? To hoard much and die early: gain, or loss? The world, dazzled by rank, bewitched by wealth, blind in gain and loss, takes getting for fortune and losing for calamity. Laozi drives the question home in reverse — no rhetorical flourish but a precept pressed against the body.

"Deep love must cost dear; much hoarding must end in heavy loss" (manuscript A keeps only 甚, 多臧 and 亡, and manuscript B is wholly lost, so the line is read here after Wang Bi): eight characters, and the chapter's core. He who loves name spends his spirit dear; he who hoards goods suffers the heavier loss: his own body goes with them. "Gold and jade fill the hall, and no one can keep them": the proof of both. The fault is not in outer things; it is the loving, hoarding heart that wounds its own body.

Hence: knowing what is enough, one is not disgraced in the marketplace of profit and rank; knowing where to stop, one is not imperiled on the road of advancement, and so one can last long. Lasting long is not length of life but length of the Dao: while the Dao is in the body, the body endures; when the Dao leaves it, the body is already gone. "The enough of knowing-what-is-enough is constantly enough" was spoken precisely to heal this disease.

Here the silk text and the Wang Bi recension differ only in particles. Manuscript A lacks 是故 (by the column spacing there is no room for two graphs above 甚; manuscript B is damaged, and the Jicheng allows that it too may have had them), and the phrase is spare and steep; Wang Bi keeps it, and the prose runs smooth and plain. Cut, it is a knife-stroke; kept, it is flowing water. Laozi never fussed over diction; one particle more and the edge dulls; yet this is no matter for ranking better and worse. The silk text also carries no question-marks: 名與身孰親 may be read as flat statement. A question draws a man to think; a statement settles the principle; either way, the point is the waking.

The Changes says "the noble man strengthens himself without cease," and again, "the noble man receives men with emptiness." Self-strengthening is the Confucian's business; knowing what is enough is the Daoist's point; both are teachings the sages raised to the season, with no fixed formula. The world, unclear on the weighing of light and heavy, scrambles for rank and frets over wealth. Only he who knows what is enough and where to stop comes near the Dao.
