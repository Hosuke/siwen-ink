The Book of Dao · Chapter Twenty-two · Not Dwelling

English Translation

He who rushes the cooking (炊者) cannot stand firm. He who gazes at himself (自視) does not shine; he who displays himself does not see clear; he who boasts wins no merit; he who exalts himself does not endure.

To the Dao, these are called: leftover food, a redundant growth (餘食贅行) — things may well loathe them. Hence those who have desires (有欲者) do not dwell in them (弗居).

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

The sage holds the One as the measure of all-under-heaven — and never displays himself, never asserts himself, never boasts, never exalts himself. Set the silk collation against the Wang Bi recension: the words differ, the sense runs through, and a student must watch.

The silk text writes 炊者不立, "the fire-tending cannot stand firm," where Wang Bi has 企者不立，跨者不行, "the tiptoe cannot stand, the strider cannot walk." The fire-tender is the man in a frenzy for his meal; the tiptoe cranes to see; the strider strides ahead. Three images of hot haste, one fault. The silk text takes its image from the cooking fire — the frenzy; Wang Bi adds the tiptoe and the stride — the hurry. Frenzy and hurry are both against the self-so, so neither can stand, neither can walk. No mystery in it: they have lost the Dao.

And the silk text writes 自視者不彰, "the self-seeing are not shown," where Wang Bi has 自是者不彰, "the self-asserting are not shown." 視 is the blindness of looking into oneself; 是 is the grip of self-certain judgment — different names, one substance: both are being screened by the self and unlit by the Dao. "The self-seeing are not shown" and "the self-displaying are not bright" belong to Laozi's list of the five diseases; "things may well loathe them" — because they run against the self-so and are not of the Dao's straight.

At the close: the silk text has 故有欲者弗居, "hence those who have desires do not dwell in them," where Wang Bi has 故有道者不處, "hence the man of the Dao does not abide them." Not dwelling is not taking up residence in these matters of pride and boasting; not abiding is not standing outside the Dao. The breath differs slightly; the sense comes home to one: warn the student to keep himself with humility and withdrawal, never to preen his name or trumpet his merit. Not self-exalting, and so lasting — the sage endures precisely by not exalting his ability, not boasting his work. The reverse is not-of-the-Dao and an early end: a thing full-grown turns old, and its death is not far off.

The Dao prizes the soft and weak; the sage empties his heart and weakens his ambition, and so his work completes without his dwelling in it, and his De covers all-under-heaven. The man of the Dao does not abide in matters of pride and boasting — is it not this? Ah — man's disease is the love of seeing himself, asserting himself, boasting of himself, exalting himself — the fire-tender's frenzy to stand. Frenzy becomes restlessness; restlessness, the stumble; the stumble, the end. So the man of the Dao looks at these — leftover food, a redundant growth, things that may well be loathed — with a shudder, as at filth, not daring to dwell in them a single day. The point of "the bent are made whole" answers this chapter from the other side.
