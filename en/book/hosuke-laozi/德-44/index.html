The Book of De · Chapter Forty-four · The Right Half of the Tally

English Translation

Patch up a great grievance, and some grievance always remains — how can that count as good?

Hence the sage holds the right half of the tally (右介) — and does not dun anyone for it.

So: he who has De minds the tally (司介); he who lacks De minds the exacting (司徹).

The Way of Heaven has no favorites: it sides, constantly, with the good.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

The chapter follows Manuscript A (MS A), except that the first graph of 无德司徹 is lost in MS A and that line follows Manuscript B (MS B); much of MS B is damaged at both ends of the chapter. Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 私大怨: Follows MS A. MS A writes 私, a scribal error for 和 "to reconcile", which the Jicheng prints as 私〈和〉. MS B reads 禾大□, with 禾 read as 和. Wang Bi reads 和大怨.
  • 必有餘怨: Follows MS A; lost in MS B.
  • 焉可以為善: Follows MS A; MS B preserves only 為善. Wang Bi writes 安 for 焉.
  • 是以聖右介: Follows MS A. The original editors note that 人執 has dropped out here and that 介 is a loan for 契 "tally". MS B reads 是以𦔻人執左芥, with 芥 as a loan for 契 and 左 "left" for 右 "right". Gao Ming holds that 右契, as attested in early texts, is correct, and that the 左契 of MS B, the Peking University manuscript and the received texts continues a corrupt tradition. Wang Bi reads 是以聖人執左契. (Jicheng IV, p. 39, n. 203)
  • 而不以責於人: Both manuscripts agree; Wang Bi reads 而不責於人, without 以.
  • 故有德司介: Follows MS A; MS B reads 故又德司芥. Wang Bi reads 有德司契, without 故.
  • 无德司徹: Follows MS B; in MS A the first graph is lost, giving □德司勶. For this graph in both manuscripts the Jicheng prints a form of 彳 and 育 above 力 (close to 勶 but without 攵), read as 徹; it is recorded here as 勶 for MS A and 徹 for MS B. Wang Bi reads 無德司徹.
  • 夫天道无親: Follows MS A; in MS B these four graphs are lost. Wang Bi reads 天道無親, without 夫.
  • 恒與善人: Follows MS A; MS B preserves only 人. This 人 was a restoration in the 1980 edition; the Jicheng reads it directly from a fragment newly joined using the Hunan Museum's 2002 photographs. Wang Bi writes 常 for 恒. (Jicheng IV, p. 204, n. 150)
  • 德　三千卌一: At the end of the section, two graph-spaces after 人, MS B has the end-title 德　三千卌一 ("De, 3,041"), giving the graph count of the De section; it is not included in the silk text. The original editors note that the title is badly damaged: of 德 only two small dots of the left-hand 彳 remain, and the final 一 survives only as the two ends of a horizontal stroke, so it could also be 七 "seven". (Jicheng IV, p. 204, nn. 150–151)

Commentary

Yaomiao says: grievance is the great blight of human relations. Once a great grievance has formed, however you patch the peace, a remnant of bitterness stays behind. So the sage prizes the not-yet-grievance over the settled one, prizes no-contention over contention managed. To call the patching "good" is to miss where goodness lives: before the grievance forms, not after. Laozi has said it to the utmost — to forestall what has not yet taken shape, to never let the grievance form, is better than dissolving one already formed: the highest policy of governance.

Collating the texts: the silk text has 焉可以為善, Wang Bi 安可以為善 — different graphs, one sense: once a great grievance has formed, it is hard to call its settlement good.

The difference between left and right, and between 介 and 契, calls for care. Silk manuscript A, followed here, has 聖右介, with 人執 dropped out; manuscript B has 聖人執左芥, its left and right the reverse of A's; the Wang Bi recension has 聖人執左契. Wu Cheng explains the left tally as held by the owner of the goods, who waits for another to present the matching half and then gives what is due. Accounts of the two halves differ; left and right alone do not settle who gives and who receives. I read the chapter as a call to keep faith without demanding a return, never making what one gives a means of exacting payment. Water benefits the ten thousand things without contending; the sage gives without demanding recompense. To me, the two illuminate each other. The tally keeps faith; the exacting builds awe. He who has De minds the tally, binding men by trust; he who lacks De minds the exacting (so manuscript B; manuscript A reads □德司勶), taking from men by force. To give without asking return — the highest De; to press with steep law — the lowest De. One thought apart, order and ruin divide.

The Way of Heaven has no favorites: it sides, constantly, with the good. This is the answer of utmost impartiality — not that Heaven plays favorites: the good man's conduct of itself accords with Heaven's way, and so the Way of Heaven stands constantly at his side. The sage patterns on Heaven: keeping to the soft is called strength; doing without contending; governing by non-doing — long life and lasting sight, all-under-heaven gladly pushing him forward, never tiring.

Here the Book of De ends, forty-four chapters: from the practicable De of human relations it enters the dark distance of the Dao's own body — Laozi's book goes from the solid into the empty, in due order.
