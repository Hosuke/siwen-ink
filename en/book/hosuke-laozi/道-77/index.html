The Book of Dao · Chapter Thirty-three · Completing Oneself

English Translation

He who knows others is knowing (知); he who knows himself is clear-seeing (明).

He who defeats others has force; he who masters himself is strong.

He who knows what is enough is rich; he who presses on has will.

He who does not lose his place lasts long; he who dies and is not forgotten is long-lived (壽).

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

Eight clauses in this chapter, paired two by two, climbing step by step — the complete work of cultivating the self. First knowing others against knowing oneself; then defeating others against mastering oneself; then knowing-enough against pressing on; last, keeping one's place against dying unforgotten — from inside out, from self to De, the ladder clear, with self-knowledge and self-mastery at the root.

"He who knows others is knowing; he who knows himself is clear-seeing" — knowing and clear-seeing differ: knowing examines outward; clear-seeing lights inward. Heshang Gong's "Bian De" chapter says, "To discern the good and bad in others is knowing," and again, "To know whether one is worthy or unworthy is to turn one's hearing back to the soundless, one's gaze inward to the formless — hence clarity." The world knows only how to watch others, not how to turn the gaze back, so it has knowing to spare and clear-seeing too little. Laozi leads with knowing-oneself: the cultivator begins by looking within. This runs with the Great Learning's illuminating the bright De — both start from seeking it in oneself.

"He who defeats others has force; he who masters himself is strong" — force and strength differ likewise: force issues outward; strength holds within. The force to defeat others, anyone can muster; to master one's own desires is what men can hardly do. The Zhuangzi says "defeating things without being hurt"; Laozi's self-mastery means subduing the private self and ruling the stirrings of passion — that is true strength. The highest De abides in non-doing (无為): when De is full, private desire does not stir — the utmost of self-mastery.

"He who knows what is enough is rich; he who presses on has will." To stand at one's allotted share and crave no more — little, yet sufficient: this is why knowing-enough is wealth. Walking the Dao is not easy; holding the will is harder still — the will is the heart's direction, as the rudder is to the boat. The two complete each other, and the road of self-cultivation is whole.

"He who does not lose his place lasts long; he who dies and is not forgotten is long-lived" — these two lines reward the finest weighing. The silk text writes 死不忘者壽, "dies and is not forgotten"; the Wang Bi recension writes 死而不亡者壽, "dies and does not perish." Not-forgotten: one's De and bounty do not fade from men's hearts. Not-perished: the Dao's substance does not die out of heaven and earth. The Doctrine of the Mean: "sincere, sincere is his humaneness; deep, deep is his depth" — kindness reaching others, and the name does not sink: it answers the silk text's not-forgotten exactly. Wang Bi's not-perished pushes further, to the Dao's substance lasting forever. Both readings pass; the silk text lies closer to the De of human affairs: self-knowledge and self-mastery are the cultivation of the person; not losing one's place and dying unforgotten are the lasting of De. From cultivating the self to keeping De — the chapter's whole work.
