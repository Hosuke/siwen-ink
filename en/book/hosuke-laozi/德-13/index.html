The Book of De · Chapter Thirteen · Life and Death

English Translation

Out into life, in into death.

The companions of life are three in ten; the companions of death are three in ten; and the people who live for living — whose every move goes toward the ground of death — are also three in ten.

Why is that so? Because they live for living.

It is said: one good at holding to life walks the hills without shunning rhinoceros or tiger, enters an army without donning armor and blade. The rhinoceros finds nowhere to thrust its horn; the tiger finds nowhere to set its claws; the weapon finds nowhere to sink its edge.

Why is that so? Because there is no ground of death in him.

Commentary

Yaomiao says: life and death are the gathering and scattering of one qi (氣), not a gain and a loss but the ordinary course of transformation. In this chapter Laozi watches the world come out into life and go in into death, and looks for the way through; the intent runs deep. Of the people of the world: the companions of life are three in ten; the companions of death, three in ten; and those whose living-for-living moves them all toward the ground of death, three in ten again: all three arise from the count of what is so of itself, and together they make nine. Nine parts in ten walk the road to death; what remains is the one man good at holding to life. Hence "it is said" (蓋聞), marking how rarely it is heard.

The silk text writes 而民生生 where Wang Bi has 人之生: the turning particle 而 makes the sentence bite: precisely those who live for living are the ones whose every motion enters the ground of death. That is the root sense of "out into life, in into death." And 動皆之死地, "every move goes to the ground of death," is steeper than Wang Bi's wording. Again: the silk text has 陵行不辟兕虎, "walks the hills without shunning rhinoceros and tiger," where Wang Bi has 陸行不遇, "travels the land without meeting them." 陵 is hill-country; 辟 is to shun. One graph, and active parts from passive: the silk text's man walks near the beasts and does not shun them; because he has no ground of death in him, the tiger cannot touch him; Wang Bi's "does not meet" is a passive escape, mere luck. The one good at holding to life is actively without ground of death — he is not passively spared by fortune. The silk text's sense is the stronger; follow it.

As for 以亓生生也 against Wang Bi's 以其生生之厚: the silk text lacks 厚, "thickly." To live-for-living thickly is appetite without limit, savor without satiety. The Guanyinzi speaks of life and death as the transformations of qi; life and death are qi gathering and scattering: the same point. The man of fullest De is like a naked babe (bones soft, sinews weak, grip firm) because he is without desire. He who thickens his life wounds it: greedy for savor, he is cut down by appetite; over-tending the body, he drowns in the tending. To have no ground of death is not to be diamond-bodied; it is a heart without grasping, a life not thickened. Alas, you who would seize life and thicken it — that is ladling boiling water to stop the boil. Be plain as water instead: thicken nothing, and the length of life is yours.
