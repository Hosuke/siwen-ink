The Book of Dao · Chapter Thirty-seven · The Nameless

English Translation

The Dao is constantly nameless (道恒无名).

If lords and kings keep it, the ten thousand things will transform of themselves.

Transforming, and desire stirs (化而欲作) — I shall set them right (闐; the Guodian slips write 貞) with the unhewn wood of the nameless.

Set right with the unhewn wood of the nameless — and there will be no disgrace (不辱).

No disgrace — and stillness. Heaven and earth will set themselves right (天地將自正).

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

The chapter largely follows Manuscript A (MS A); 化而欲作 and the two lines with 吾將闐之以无名之樸, damaged in MS A, are supplied from Manuscript B (MS B). Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 道恒无名: The manuscripts agree. Wang Bi reads 道常無為而無不為 "the Dao is constantly non-doing, and nothing is left undone", quite unlike the silk text.
  • 侯王若守之: Follows MS A; MS B reads 侯王若能守之, with 能 "be able", as in Wang Bi.
  • 萬物將自𢡺: Follows MS A, which writes 𢡺 (爲 over 心), read by the Jicheng as 化 "to transform"; MS B writes 化. In both manuscripts 化 carries a repetition mark, giving 萬物將自化，化而欲作.
  • 化而欲作: Follows MS B; in MS A 作 is lost.
  • 吾將闐之以无名之樸 / 闐之以无名之樸: Follows MS B, with 闐 read as 鎮 "to press down, to steady". MS B writes 吾將闐﹦之﹦以﹦无﹦名﹦之﹦樸﹦, the repetition marks yielding two lines. The first half is lost in MS A, which preserves only ﹦名﹦之﹦楃﹦; the Jicheng restores 【作，吾將鎮﹦之﹦以﹦無】﹦名﹦之﹦楃﹦, supplying the repetition marks on 鎮之以 from MS B. The Peking University manuscript and the received texts repeat only the four graphs 無名之樸, and the Guodian slips repeat none of the seven; the Jicheng judges that the silk reading is probably correct. Gao Ming's transcription gives □名之楃□□□无名之楃, differing from the Jicheng. Wang Bi has 吾將鎮之以無名之樸。無名之樸. (Jicheng IV, p. 56, n. 149)
  • 夫將不辱 / 不辱以情: Follows MS A, with a repetition mark after 辱; 情 is read as 靜 "stillness", which MS B writes. Wang Bi has 夫亦將無欲。不欲以靜; the silk text has 不辱 "no disgrace" where Wang Bi has "no desire".
  • 天地將自正: The manuscripts agree; Wang Bi has 天下將自定.
  • End of the section: At the close of its Dao section MS B carries a tail title, 道　二千四百廿六, giving the section's name and its count of graphs (2,426); MS A has none.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

The Dao, toward things, has hidden and shown names; substance and function go by different roads. The silk text writes 道恒无名, "the Dao is constantly nameless," where the Wang Bi recension writes 道常無為而無不為, "the Dao is constantly non-doing, and nothing is left undone." One speaks the substance, one the function — one point. Constantly-nameless is the Dao's substance: still, unshaped, with no name to call it. Non-doing is the Dao's working: transforming and birthing the ten thousand things, the work done and not dwelt in. Substance and function one source, hidden and shown two names — and the Dao was never two. "If lords and kings keep this Dao, the ten thousand things will transform of themselves" — the very sense of that line.

Yet in the midst of their self-transforming, desire stirs and would act. 化而欲作: appetite sprouting, the first stir of contrivance. "I shall set them right with the unhewn wood of the nameless." Silk manuscript B writes 闐, the Guodian slips 貞, "set right," and Wang Bi 鎮, "press down." I venture 貞 is the longer sense: setting right what has gone off-true — not a clamp laid on from outside. That is: the sage governs all-under-heaven not by cleverness, not by force — he returns the hundred clans to the unhewn wood of desirelessness, letting them settle of themselves. The unhewn wood of the nameless is no technique: it is the Dao's own plainness, enough to set the ten thousand things' leanings right. At desire's first stirring, set it right with the nameless unhewn — and desire does not grow, the heart is not burdened: no disgrace, and still — the effect of the self-so.

"No disgrace — and stillness. Heaven and earth will set themselves right." The silk text writes 天地將自正, "heaven and earth will set themselves right"; Wang Bi writes 天下將自定, "all-under-heaven will settle of itself." As "clear stillness can set all-under-heaven right" has it: 正 is each thing getting its place, the ten thousand things ordering themselves; and "lords and kings got the One and set all-under-heaven right" — so 正 speaks through heaven, earth, and man, the three powers, while 定 leans toward the effect in governance. "Without striking, good at winning": the Way of Heaven abides in non-doing, and the ten thousand things go home to their rightness of themselves. The chapter's whole sense comes home to one word: stillness. At stillness's utmost, heaven and earth set themselves right — the rule of non-doing complete at last.

Let the student know: the nameless unhewn wood is nothing to seek outside — only to keep. Keep it, and stirring desire does not rise; no disgrace, and stillness; and heaven and earth set themselves right.
