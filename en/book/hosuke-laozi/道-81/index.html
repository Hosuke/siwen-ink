The Book of Dao · Chapter Thirty-seven · The Nameless

English Translation

The Dao is constantly nameless (道恆无名).

If lords and kings keep it, the ten thousand things will transform of themselves.

Transforming, and desire stirs (化而欲作) — I shall set them right (貞) with the unhewn wood of the nameless.

Set right with the unhewn wood of the nameless — and there will be no disgrace (不辱).

No disgrace — and stillness. Heaven and earth will set themselves right (天地將自正).

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

The Dao, toward things, has hidden and shown names; substance and function go by different roads. The silk text writes 道恆无名, "the Dao is constantly nameless," where the Wang Bi recension writes 道常無為而無不為, "the Dao is constantly non-doing, and nothing is left undone." One speaks the substance, one the function — one point. Constantly-nameless is the Dao's substance: still, unshaped, with no name to call it. Non-doing is the Dao's working: transforming and birthing the ten thousand things, the work done and not dwelt in. Substance and function one source, hidden and shown two names — and the Dao was never two. "If lords and kings keep this Dao, the ten thousand things will transform of themselves" — the very sense of that line.

Yet in the midst of their self-transforming, desire stirs and would act. 化而欲作: appetite sprouting, the first stir of contrivance. "I shall set them right with the unhewn wood of the nameless." Silk manuscript B writes 闐, the Guodian slips 貞, "set right," and Wang Bi 鎮, "press down." I venture 貞 is the longer sense: setting right what has gone off-true — not a clamp laid on from outside. That is: the sage governs all-under-heaven not by cleverness, not by force — he returns the hundred clans to the unhewn wood of desirelessness, letting them settle of themselves. The unhewn wood of the nameless is no technique: it is the Dao's own plainness, enough to set the ten thousand things' leanings right. At desire's first stirring, set it right with the nameless unhewn — and desire does not grow, the heart is not burdened: no disgrace, and still — the effect of the self-so.

"No disgrace — and stillness. Heaven and earth will set themselves right." The silk text writes 天地將自正, "heaven and earth will set themselves right"; Wang Bi writes 天下將自定, "all-under-heaven will settle of itself." As "clear stillness can set all-under-heaven right" has it: 正 is each thing getting its place, the ten thousand things ordering themselves; and "lords and kings got the One and set all-under-heaven right" — so 正 speaks through heaven, earth, and man, the three powers, while 定 leans toward the effect in governance. "Without striking, good at winning": the Way of Heaven abides in non-doing, and the ten thousand things go home to their rightness of themselves. The chapter's whole sense comes home to one word: stillness. At stillness's utmost, heaven and earth set themselves right — the rule of non-doing complete at last.

Here the Book of Dao ends. Let the student know: the nameless unhewn wood is nothing to seek outside — only to keep. Keep it, and stirring desire does not rise; no disgrace, and stillness; and heaven and earth set themselves right.
