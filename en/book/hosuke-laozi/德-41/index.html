The Book of De · Chapter Forty-one · The Soft and Weak

English Translation

A man in life is soft and weak; in death, constantly rigid, hard and strong (𬝖仞賢強). The ten thousand things, grass and tree, in life are supple and tender; in death, withered and dry.

Hence it is said: the hard and strong are the companions of death; the soft and weak, the minute and fine (柔弱微細) are the companions of life.

An army grown hard cannot win; a tree grown hard stands stiff as a dead thing (恒).

The hard and great dwell below; the soft, weak, and minute dwell above.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

Manuscript A (MS A) is complete in this chapter, and the silk text follows it throughout. Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 人之生也柔弱: Both manuscripts agree, and MS A opens with a chapter-division dot; Wang Bi agrees.
  • 亓死也𬝖仞賢強: Follows MS A; MS B reads 亓死也恆信堅強. MS A's 𬝖 is 艹 over 亙 (MS A always writes 恒 in the form 亙, which is conventionally transcribed as 恒); the Jicheng prints 艹 over 恆. For the corresponding graph in MS B, the Jicheng and the 1980 edition give 骨 beside 亙 (no code point); on the plate the right side looks closer to 亘; it is recorded here as 恆. Where MS A has 仞, MS B has 信, and where MS A has 賢, MS B has 堅; 賢 is read as 堅 "hard". Wang Bi reads only 其死也堅強, without the two graphs 𬝖仞.
  • 萬物草木之生也柔肥: Follows MS A; in MS B 物草 is lost and 肥 is written 椊. MS A's 肥 (月 with 巴) is read by the Jicheng as 脆 "tender"; earlier transcriptions sometimes print the reading 脆 directly. Wang Bi has 柔脆.
  • 亓死也𣒞𩫓: Follows MS A; MS B reads 亓死也𣒞槀. 𣒞 (木 beside 車) is common to both manuscripts and is read as 枯 "withered"; MS A's 𩫓 (高 over 死) and MS B's 槀 are both read as 槁 "dry". Gao Ming's typeset transcription prints 棹, a misprint for a similar-looking form. Wang Bi reads 其死也枯槁.
  • 故曰堅強者死之徒也: Follows MS A; MS B lacks 者. Wang Bi reads 故堅強者死之徒, without 曰 or 也.
  • 柔弱微細生之徒也: Follows MS A; MS B lacks 微細, reading 柔弱生之徒也. For MS A's 微 the Jicheng prints a form with 彳, something like 育 in the middle, and 攵 (close to 徹), read as 微; the same holds for 柔弱微細居上 below. Wang Bi reads 柔弱者生之徒.
  • 兵強則不勝: Follows MS A; MS B reads □以兵強則不朕, with two extra graphs at the start (the first lost), and 朕 for 勝. Wang Bi reads 是以兵強則不勝.
  • 木強則恒: Follows MS A, whose 恒 is written in the form 亙, the same element as in 𬝖 above. MS B reads 木強則兢, Wang Bi 木強則兵.
  • 強大居下: Follows MS A; MS B begins with 故. Wang Bi writes 處 for 居.
  • 柔弱微細居上: Follows MS A; MS B lacks 微細. Wang Bi reads 柔弱處上.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

This chapter takes the images of life and death to show the way of the soft and weak — spare words, deep sense; and set silk text against Wang Bi, the differences stand out plain.

"亓死也𬝖仞賢強" — "in death, long-standing stiff and hard": the Wang Bi text has only 堅強. 仞 is to stand erect — the look of the dead man's sinews and bones standing rigid; 𬝖 — in manuscript A, 艹 over 亙; in manuscript B, 骨 beside 亘, now recorded as 恆 — takes its sound from 亙 and may be read as 恆, lasting — the rigidity set, not melting away with time. This is the plain fact of the body, and Wang Bi's cut hides the image with the words. And manuscript A's "木強則恒" where Wang Bi has 木強則兵, "a tree grown hard [becomes] a weapon" — obscure. Read 恒 with 𬝖 above — in the manuscript both are built on 亙 — 恒 here is rigidity set long and never melting: the tree grown hard stands stiff as a dead thing — rigidity is death's posture arrived early. And what is too hard cannot last: an army grown hard draws vengeance home, a tree grown hard draws the carpenter's axe; neither meets its natural end. With 恒 answering 𬝖, the sense is whole. Follow the silk text.

As for 柔弱微細, the silk text's extra 微細, "minute," is the richer reading. Minute, and then fine; fine, and then soft; softness at its utmost is nearly formless — nearly the Dao. "The softest in all-under-heaven gallops through the hardest in all-under-heaven" — the utmost soft is exactly the utmost of soft-and-weak. "Keeping to the soft is called strength": not that softness turns into strength, but that through softness one can keep life whole, can defeat the strong, can join the true of the Dao.

"The soft and weak are the companions of life; the hard and strong, the companions of death" — no metaphor but the sign of one law. Water's highest good lies in lying low and not contending, so none can contend with it; the infant's soft weakness keeps its essence and qi within, so no creature can harm it. "The hard and great dwell below; the soft and weak dwell above" — not by anyone's arrangement: the stance of the self-so. The Way of Heaven loathes rigidity and aids the soft — the grain of things is so. The sage knows this: to the end of his days he does not contend; to the end of his days he is without fault.
