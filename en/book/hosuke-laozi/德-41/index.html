The Book of De · Chapter Forty-one · The Soft and Weak

English Translation

A man in life is soft and weak; in death, constantly rigid, hard and strong (恆仞堅強). The ten thousand things, grass and tree, in life are supple and tender; in death, withered and dry.

Hence it is said: the hard and strong are the companions of death; the soft and weak, the minute and fine (柔弱微細) are the companions of life.

An army grown hard cannot win; a tree grown hard stands stiff as a dead thing (恆).

The hard and great dwell below; the soft, weak, and minute dwell above.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

This chapter takes the images of life and death to show the way of the soft and weak — spare words, deep sense; and set silk text against Wang Bi, the differences stand out plain.

"亓死也恆仞堅強" — "in death, long-standing stiff and hard": the Wang Bi text has only 堅強. 仞 is to stand erect — the look of the dead man's sinews and bones standing rigid; 恆 is lasting — the rigidity set, not melting away with time. This is the plain fact of the body, and Wang Bi's cut hides the image with the words. And "木強則恆" where Wang Bi has 木強則兵, "a tree grown hard [becomes] a weapon" — obscure. Read 恆 with 恆仞 above — 恆 here is rigidity set long and never melting: the tree grown hard stands stiff as a dead thing — rigidity is death's posture arrived early. And what is too hard cannot last: an army grown hard draws vengeance home, a tree grown hard draws the carpenter's axe; neither meets its natural end. With 恆 answering 恆仞, the sense is whole. Follow the silk text.

As for 柔弱微細, the silk text's extra 微細, "minute," is the richer reading. Minute, and then fine; fine, and then soft; softness at its utmost is nearly formless — nearly the Dao. "The softest in all-under-heaven gallops through the hardest in all-under-heaven" — the utmost soft is exactly the utmost of soft-and-weak. "Keeping to the soft is called strength": not that softness turns into strength, but that through softness one can keep life whole, can defeat the strong, can join the true of the Dao.

"The soft and weak are the companions of life; the hard and strong, the companions of death" — no metaphor but the sign of one law. Water's highest good lies in lying low and not contending, so none can contend with it; the infant's soft weakness keeps its essence and qi within, so no creature can harm it. "The hard and great dwell below; the soft, weak, and minute dwell above" — not by anyone's arrangement: the stance of the self-so. The Way of Heaven loathes rigidity and aids the soft — the grain of things is so. The sage knows this: to the end of his days he does not contend; to the end of his days he is without fault.
