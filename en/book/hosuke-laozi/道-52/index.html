The Book of Dao · Chapter Eight · Governing as Water

English Translation

The highest good: governing as water (上善治水). Water is good at benefiting the ten thousand things — and keeps still (有靜). It dwells where the crowd loathes to be — and so comes near to the Dao.

In dwelling, good at the ground; in heart, the deep pool; in giving, heaven; in speech, trust; in governing, order; in works, ability; in moving, the season.

Only because it does not contend is it without fault.

Commentary

Yaomiao says: in this chapter of the Book of Dao the silk text and the Wang Bi recension differ at several points, each with its own depth — they must be weighed.

The silk text writes 上善治水, "the highest good: governing as water," where the Wang Bi recension writes 上善若水, "the highest good is like water." 治 is to follow a thing's nature and guide it — not to bend it against its own grain. One who has spoken of water's nature says: "where water's course meets the crooked, it must linger and fall back" — he has the point. 若 is only a figure of speech; 治 is a practice joined to the figure. "The highest good is like water" has flowed out over the world from Wang Bi's text, and students ever since have held the image and dropped the meaning — never knowing that the silk text's 治 is the sage's real work of self-cultivation, not a flourish of comparison.

Again: the silk text has 水善利萬物而有靜, "water benefits the ten thousand things and keeps still," where Wang Bi has 而不爭, "and does not contend." Stillness is water's substance; not-contending is its function. The deeper the substance, the farther the function reaches. The Book of Changes: "no thought, no doing — still and unmoving": what comes near the Dao is in water's stillness. Water flows and never stops, yet every point of it is still — the source of the clause "in moving, good at the season": it does not merely mean not contending. Putting oneself behind is the substance of not-contending — but know too that this substance comes of the heart's being still and unmoved.

"In giving, good at heaven": the silk text writes 予, where Wang Bi has 與善仁, "in association, good at humaneness." 予 is giving without asking return — heaven's bestowing and transforming, near the heart the Analects ascribes to Yao and Shun, who "still found it a strain": nothing privately covered, nothing privately given — hence "good at heaven." Humaneness, loving and bestowing, is one rung lower. Mencius's "he who loves men is constantly loved" is still humaneness not yet at its end. "In words, good at faith" and "in ruling, good at order" are both extensions of the De of not contending. 正 is governing: water's level can set shapes right; the sage's uprightness can set the world's inclinations right — one sense.

Of the seven goods, "in heart, good at the deep pool" is the crux. The Great Commentary: "plumb the spirit and know the transformations" — abyssal depth is how the heart comes near to the Dao. "Only because it does not contend is it without fault": fault is what blame grows from. The Analects: "the noble man contends in nothing" — one point. Contenders invite fault; the uncontending have none. Water moistens the hundred living things and takes no seat in the merit; the sage transforms and nurtures the multitudes and does not flaunt himself. The De of not contending — the very place where Confucian and Daoist roads come home to one end.
