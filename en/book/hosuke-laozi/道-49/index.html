The Book of Dao · Chapter Five · Keeping the Middle

English Translation

Heaven and earth are not humane (不仁): they treat the ten thousand things as straw dogs (芻狗). The sage is not humane: he treats the hundred clans as straw dogs.

The space between heaven and earth — is it not like a bellows (橐籥)? Empty, and never drained (不淈); move it, and ever more comes out.

Much hearing soon runs out. Better to keep to the middle (守於中).

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

The chapter follows Manuscript A (MS A) throughout, except that 天地之閒, damaged in MS A, follows Manuscript B (MS B), and 以百省為芻狗 combines the two. Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 聲人不仁: Follows MS A. MS A writes 聲人, MS B 𦔻人 (耳 beside 口); both are read as 聖人 "the sage".
  • 以百省為芻狗: The manuscripts complement each other. MS A reads 以百省□□狗, with 為芻 lost; in MS B 以 is lost and the text has 百姓. The Jicheng reads MS A's 省 as 姓 "clan". Wang Bi has 以百姓為芻狗.
  • 天地之閒: Follows MS B. In MS A 之 is lost; its 閒, given as a lacuna in Gao Ming's transcription, is read directly by the Jicheng. Both manuscripts write 閒, read as 間 "between".
  • 其猶橐籥輿: Follows MS A, whose 其 is read directly by the Jicheng. MS B reads 亓猷橐籥輿; Gao Ming's transcription gives 其猶橐籥與, but the plate shows 猷 and 輿. 猷 is read as 猶 "like", 輿 as the question particle 歟. Wang Bi has 其猶橐籥乎.
  • 虛而不淈: The manuscripts agree; 淈 is read as 屈 "to be exhausted". Wang Bi has 虛而不屈.
  • 蹱而俞出: Follows MS A, which writes 蹱 (足 with 童), read by the Jicheng as 動 "to move". A collation slip suggested that the Jicheng prints 踵 (足 with 重), but the print, enlarged, shows 蹱, as does Gao Ming's transcription. MS B writes 勭, also read as 動. 俞 is read as 愈 "the more". Wang Bi has 動而愈出.
  • 多聞數竆: Follows MS A. MS A writes 竆 and MS B a variant of 窮 (身 and 巳 under 穴); the Jicheng reads both as 窮 "to run out". Both manuscripts have 多聞 "much hearing"; Wang Bi has 多言 "much speaking".
  • 不若守於中: The manuscripts agree; Wang Bi has 不如守中.

Commentary

The crux of this chapter is empty use, and the crux of empty use is keeping the middle — three rings of one chain.

"Heaven and earth are not humane: they treat the ten thousand things as straw dogs." Heaven and earth have no heart, and let things transform of themselves. The straw dog is a grass effigy for the rite — set up and honored while the rite lasts, thrown aside when it ends. So heaven and earth treat the ten thousand things: no private fondness in the keeping, no grudge in the dropping — the impartiality of the Dao. The Analects: "the noble man, toward all-under-heaven, has nothing he is for and nothing he is against." The sage's "inhumanity" is the same sense — not the absence of a humane heart, but the great impartial humaneness made complete.

The bellows image makes the subtlety of empty use plain. The silk text writes 虛而不淈, "empty and never drained" (the 1980 editors read 淈 as 屈), where Wang Bi has 不屈, "unyielding." Read as written, 淈 is water running dry; 屈 is being bent within limits — the depths of the two graphs show themselves. The silk text's sense: the empty never runs out — the more you work it, the more wind issues — for the capacity of emptiness is limitless. The chapter "The Dao in Use" has it: "the Dao is hollow, yet in use it never fills" — one point. Heaven is called empty, earth is called still: emptiness is the root of the bellows, presence is born of absence — the pivot of all generating.

"Keep to the middle" is keeping to the empty. The silk text has 多聞數竆, "much hearing soon runs out," where Wang Bi has 多言, "much talk." 聞 is broad learning flaunted outward; 言 is racing speech that argues itself dry — both belong to contrivance, stirring the heart's emptiness so it drains the faster. The Great Learning says "know where to stop, and only then is there steadiness"; the Doctrine of the Mean, "before joy, anger, grief, and delight are stirred — that is called the middle." Both are the keeping of the middle and the empty — Confucian and Daoist running together, as they so often do.

So: keeping the middle is keeping the empty; the empty is the straw-dog image; the bellows is the instrument of empty use. Three senses, one thread — the sage's non-doing complete. Let the student gather this, and he will know: the inhumanity is the greatest humaneness of all.
