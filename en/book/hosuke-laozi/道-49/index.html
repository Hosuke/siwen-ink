The Book of Dao · Chapter Five · Keeping the Middle

English Translation

Heaven and earth are not humane (不仁): they treat the ten thousand things as straw dogs (芻狗). The sage is not humane: he treats the hundred clans as straw dogs.

The space between heaven and earth — is it not like a bellows (橐籥)? Empty, and never drained (不淈); move it, and ever more comes out.

Much hearing soon runs out. Better to keep to the middle (守於中).

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

The crux of this chapter is empty use, and the crux of empty use is keeping the middle — three rings of one chain.

"Heaven and earth are not humane: they treat the ten thousand things as straw dogs." Heaven and earth have no heart, and let things transform of themselves. The straw dog is a grass effigy for the rite — set up and honored while the rite lasts, thrown aside when it ends. So heaven and earth treat the ten thousand things: no private fondness in the keeping, no grudge in the dropping — the impartiality of the Dao. The Analects: "the noble man, toward all-under-heaven, has nothing he is for and nothing he is against." The sage's "inhumanity" is the same sense — not the absence of a humane heart, but the great impartial humaneness made complete.

The bellows image makes the subtlety of empty use plain. The silk text writes 虛而不淈, "empty and never drained," where Wang Bi has 不屈, "unyielding." 淈 is water running dry; 屈 is being bent within limits — the depths of the two graphs show themselves. The silk text's sense: the empty never runs out — the more you work it, the more wind issues — for the capacity of emptiness is limitless. The chapter "The Dao in Use" has it: "the Dao is hollow, and in its use there is always more" — one point. Heaven is called empty, earth is called still: emptiness is the root of the bellows, presence is born of absence — the pivot of all generating.

"Keep to the middle" is keeping to the empty. The silk text has 多聞數窮, "much hearing soon runs out," where Wang Bi has 多言, "much talk." 聞 is broad learning flaunted outward; 言 is racing speech that argues itself dry — both belong to contrivance, stirring the heart's emptiness so it drains the faster. The Great Learning says "know where to stop, and only then is there steadiness"; the Doctrine of the Mean, "before joy, anger, grief, and delight are stirred — that is called the middle." Both are the keeping of the middle and the empty — Confucian and Daoist running together, as they so often do.

So: keeping the middle is keeping the empty; the empty is the straw-dog image; the bellows is the instrument of empty use. Three senses, one thread — the sage's non-doing complete. Let the student gather this, and he will know: the inhumanity is the greatest humaneness of all.
