The Book of De · Chapter Thirty-three · Not Contending

English Translation

He who is good as an officer is not martial. He who is good at battle does not rage. He who is good at defeating the enemy does not engage (弗與). He who is good at using men places himself below them.

This is called the De (德) of not contending. This is called using men. This is called Heaven (是謂天) — the highest rule of old.

Commentary

Yaomiao says: in this chapter Laozi unfolds the De of not contending through four masteries, and roots it at last in Heaven.

He who is good as an officer is not martial: the good officer's trunk is heart and will, not a show of fists. Martial display is the dregs of blood and temper; the good officer knows it cannot win hearts for long, and so makes no show of it. He who is good at battle does not rage: rage is where the opening is lost — an army moved by rage seldom escapes defeat. The good fighter masters motion by stillness and waits on the hour; the sage takes the enemy as his resource and never acts under the whip of anger. He who is good at defeating the enemy does not engage: 與 is to close and lock blades; not-engaging is not a failure to win — it is winning by plan, by position, by De, so that the enemy yields of itself with no blade laid on. This is the subtlest form of the De of not contending, one taproot with Sunzi's "to break the enemy's resistance without fighting." Laozi says it with 弗與; Sunzi completes it with "the army kept whole" — one gauge. He who is good at using men places himself below them: men fear the one above them and draw close to the one below; the sage uses men by dwelling beneath them, and so men are glad to be used. These four masteries are the proofs of not contending.

But one thing must be watched, for the difference between the silk text and the Wang Bi recension touches the fine grain of the doctrine. Silk manuscript A says 是謂天, "this is called Heaven"; manuscript B writes 是謂肥天; Wang Bi writes 是謂配天, "this is called matching Heaven." In the silk text, the sage's De simply is Heaven — sage and Heaven are not two things waiting to be joined. Wang Bi's added 配 makes sage and heaven partners in a matching: the sage's De, however high, is not yet Heaven. Heshang Gong too comments "the sage's De matches heaven and earth." Yaomiao ventures that Laozi's intent is that the sage walks one Dao with Heaven: the good of "the highest good is like water" is the goodness of heaven's way — not a matching from outside. This is the extreme of doing without contending. Again: the silk text says 是謂用人, "this is called using men," where Wang Bi adds 之力, "the strength of men" — plainer, and poorer for it. "The way of heaven: not contending, and good at winning; not speaking, and good at answering" — Heaven's way does not contend; the sage patterns on it, and the four masteries are the fruits of not contending. And the chapter closes with 古之極也, "the highest rule of old": not Laozi's own coinage but the rule of the old sages retold — Laozi does not seat himself among the makers, and his modesty shows here. So the noble man dwells low as the earth, his heart deep as a pool; not contending, and no one in all-under-heaven can contend with him — this is why Heaven's way lasts long.
