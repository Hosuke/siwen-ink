The Book of De · Chapter Thirty-three · Not Contending

English Translation

He who is good as an officer is not martial. He who is good at battle does not rage. He who is good at defeating the enemy does not engage (弗與). He who is good at using men places himself below them.

This is called the De (德) of not contending. This is called using men. This is called Heaven (是胃天) — the highest rule of old.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

This chapter follows Manuscript A (MS A), except that the last graph of 善朕敵者弗與, lost in MS A, is supplied from Manuscript B (MS B), and 是胃不諍之德 is pieced together from both. Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 善為士者不武: Follows MS A; MS B begins with 故. The Jinglong stele, the Suizhou text, Fu Yi and several Dunhuang manuscripts begin with 古之 "of old", and the Jicheng takes MS B's 故 as a corruption of 古之. Wang Bi reads 善為士者不武. (Jicheng IV, p. 35, n. 165; p. 203, n. 134)
  • 善戰者不怒: Follows MS A; MS B writes 單 for 戰, read as 戰 "battle".
  • 善朕敵者弗與: MS A reads 善勝敵者弗□, its last graph lost; follows MS B. MS A has 勝 where MS B has 朕, read as 勝 "to defeat". Wang Bi reads 善勝敵者不與, with 不 for 弗.
  • 是胃不諍之德: The two manuscripts complement each other: MS A loses 是 and has 諍; MS B has 爭 and loses 之. 諍 is read as 爭 "to contend"; the left side of MS A's graph looks like 言. Wang Bi reads 是謂不爭之德.
  • 是胃用人: Both manuscripts agree. The original editors note that the received text reads 是謂用人之力; MS B also lacks 之力, and the Peking University manuscript agrees with the silk. (Jicheng IV, p. 35, n. 166)
  • 是胃天古之極也: Follows MS A. The original editors note that MS A has dropped 配 "to match", which the Jicheng supplies as a restoration; MS B reads 是胃肥天古之極也, and the Peking University manuscript likewise uses 肥 for 配, a loan that recurs in the Mawangdui silk texts and other excavated documents. In MS A a hook mark follows 天, dividing 是胃〔配〕天，古之極也. Wang Bi reads 是謂配天古之極, without 也. (Jicheng IV, p. 35, n. 166; p. 203, n. 135)

Commentary

Yaomiao says: in this chapter Laozi unfolds the De of not contending through four masteries, and roots it at last in Heaven.

He who is good as an officer is not martial: the good officer's trunk is heart and will, not a show of fists. Martial display is the dregs of blood and temper; the good officer knows it cannot win hearts for long, and so makes no show of it. He who is good at battle does not rage: rage is where the opening is lost — an army moved by rage seldom escapes defeat. The good fighter masters motion by stillness and waits on the hour; the sage takes the enemy as his resource and never acts under the whip of anger. He who is good at defeating the enemy does not engage: 與 is to close and lock blades; not-engaging is not a failure to win — it is winning by plan, by position, by De, so that the enemy yields of itself with no blade laid on. This is the subtlest form of the De of not contending, one taproot with Sunzi's "to break the enemy's resistance without fighting." Laozi says it with 弗與; Sunzi completes it with "the army kept whole" — one gauge. He who is good at using men places himself below them: men fear the one above them and draw close to the one below; the sage uses men by dwelling beneath them, and so men are glad to be used. These four masteries are the proofs of not contending.

But one thing must be watched, for the difference between the silk text and the Wang Bi recension touches the fine grain of the doctrine. Manuscript A says 是胃天，古之極也, "this is called Heaven, the highest rule of old"; manuscript B writes 是胃肥天古之極也; Wang Bi writes 是謂配天古之極, "this is called matching Heaven, the highest rule of old." Manuscript B's 肥 is shared by the Peking University manuscript, and the Mawangdui silk texts often borrow 肥 for 配 — so manuscript B in fact reads "matching Heaven"; manuscript A lacks the graph, and the original editors and the Jicheng alike take this as a copyist's omission and supply it. The silk and Wang Bi texts, then, do not differ: both say the sage's De matches Heaven. Heshang Gong too comments "the sage's De matches heaven and earth," speaking of a matching. Yaomiao ventures that Laozi's intent is this: to match is not to be likened from outside — the sage walks one Dao with Heaven, the good of "the highest good is like water" (上善治水) is the goodness of the Way of Heaven, and to match Heaven is to be one with Heaven. This is the extreme of doing without contending; and manuscript A's chance omission of 配, read as it stands, happens to bring this sense out. Again: the silk text says 是胃用人, "this is called using men," where Wang Bi adds 之力, "the strength of men" — plainer, and poorer for it. The silk text says, "The Way of Heaven: not doing battle (不單), and good at winning (善朕); not speaking, and good at answering" (manuscript B; 單 read as 戰, 朕 as 勝) — Heaven's way does not contend; the sage patterns on it, and the four masteries are the fruits of not contending. And the chapter closes with 古之極也, "the highest rule of old": not Laozi's own coinage but the rule of the old sages retold — Laozi does not seat himself among the makers, and his modesty shows here. So the noble man dwells low as the earth, his heart deep as a pool; not contending, and no one in all-under-heaven can contend with him — this is why Heaven's way lasts long.
