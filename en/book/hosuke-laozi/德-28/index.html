The Book of De · Chapter Twenty-eight · Dark De

English Translation

Hence it is said: he who works the Dao does not use it to make the people clever (明); he will use it to make them simple (愚). The people are hard to govern: because of what they know (知).

So to govern the state by knowing is the state's bandit; to govern the state by not-knowing is the state's De.

To know these two constantly is itself the rule (稽式). To know the rule constantly: this is called dark De (玄德).

Dark De is deep, is far; it runs counter to things — and then it reaches the great accord (大順).

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

The work of working the Dao is diminish and diminish again, down to non-doing. What this chapter speaks is exactly the learning of diminishing knowledge.

"Making them simple" does not mean making the people dull. The highest De has no name, and the sage's governing of the world is the same. Foreknowing is presumption and guesswork: taking one's own narrow view for heaven's vastness: the flower of the Dao and the beginning of folly. When the people are habituated to it, they are hard to govern. The sage wants them to return to unhewn wood, so he makes them simple: simple is unhewn, not stupid. The Great Learning's "rectifying the heart" and "sincerifying the intent": a heart right and an intent true, unscreened by appetite — come to the same point.

The silk text: 以知知邦，邦之賊；以不知知邦，邦之德. The Wang Bi recension: 以智治國，國之賊；不以智治國，國之福. One graph apart, and the senses stand a thousand miles off. 知 is the knowing that distinguishes: where there is knowing, there is right and wrong, profit and harm; each man brandishes his knowing, and contention rises. 智 tilts toward craft and cunning. And that is what makes a governor the bandit of the state. Again: the silk text says 邦, state in the wide sense that embraces heaven, earth, people, and things; Wang Bi's 國 narrows it to the polity.

The passage on the measuring-rule: he who can constantly know that governing by knowing is the bandit and governing by not-knowing is the De, holds the root law of rule. Heshang Gong: "玄 is heaven; this De is one with heaven": truly. And silk manuscript A opens with the blunt 故曰, "hence it is said"; manuscript B has 古之為道者, "of old, those who worked the Dao"; Wang Bi prefaces 古之善為道者, "of old, those good at working the Dao": the one direct and steep, the other winding and mild. That steepness is part of what the silk text is worth.

Dark De: deep, far, counter to things — and then the great accord. The world chases cleverness and runs with things; dark De turns against the current. At the end of that turning, it joins De with the Dao itself: close to what the Great Commentary calls "going before heaven, and heaven not opposing it." Turned, and then in accord with the ten thousand things: that is the great accord. That is dark De.
