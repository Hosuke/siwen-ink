The Book of Dao · Chapter Twenty-four · One with the Dao

English Translation

Few words — self-so (希言自然).

A whirlwind does not last the morning; a downpour does not last the day.

Who makes these? Heaven and earth — and even they cannot keep it up long, let alone a man.

Hence: he who works at the Dao is one with the Dao; he who works at De is one with De; he who works at loss is one with loss.

To one with De, the Dao likewise grants De (道亦德之). To one with loss, the Dao likewise loses him.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

The crux of this chapter is the law of being-one-with, pictured in wind and rain — a warning that much talk and contrived doing cannot last.

"Few words — self-so" — Laozi laying bare the root of governance. Sparse words are few words: without speaking, the people transform of themselves — the sage prizes sparse speech. Much talk makes the rule prying; a prying rule harries the people. The De of not contending and the point of sparse words complete each other, and both come home to the country of non-doing.

The wind-and-rain image: "a whirlwind does not last the morning; a downpour does not last the day." The silk text says 孰為此，天地而弗能久 — "who makes these? Heaven and earth, and even they cannot keep it up long." The Wang Bi text inserts a particle and reads "heaven and earth themselves cannot keep it up" — the breath a touch slower, the sense one. If heaven and earth, with all the force of the self-so, cannot make wind and rain persist — how long can a man's contrived doing last? So the sage follows the stance of the self-so and does not force on the people what they cannot bear.

The point of being-one-with: the silk text and Wang Bi differ a little in words and a great deal in sense. The silk text: "he who works at the Dao is one with the Dao; he who works at De is one with De; he who works at loss is one with loss" — spare words, full sense. Wang Bi adds 道者 — redundant in words, plainer in meaning. Below that, "to one with De, the Dao likewise grants De," the two texts part widely. Wang Bi runs: "one with the Dao — the Dao is glad to have him; one with De — De is glad to have him," three ends in one breath. The silk text says simply: "to one with De, the Dao likewise grants De; to one with loss, the Dao likewise loses him," lacking the first clause, words and sense both spare. I suspect the silk text has dropped a strip here — yet its "the Dao likewise grants De" points straight at the pairing: the Dao does not abandon De, and losing does not change either — the two senses facing each other also pass. Further: Wang Bi closes with the two lines "when good faith is not enough, there is mistrust" — which the silk text lacks. Those lines belong to the seventeenth chapter of the received ordering; they are a misplaced strip, slipped in somewhere in Wang Bi's transmission — their sense also passes, so let both stand.

The sense of being-one-with passes into the self-so of resonance. The Commentary on the Words: "same sounds answer each other; same qi seeks each other" — the necessity in the grain of things. Each kind goes to its own. The sage knows this, and so prizes sparse words and non-doing: coming near the Dao, he watches water's goodness at lying low and wakes to the point of not contending — not chasing the boundless with the bounded — and no one in all-under-heaven can contend with him. This is why the Dao lasts long.
