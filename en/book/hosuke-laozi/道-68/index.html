The Book of Dao · Chapter Twenty-four · One with the Dao

English Translation

Few words — self-so (希言自然).

A whirlwind does not last the morning; a downpour does not last the day.

Who makes these? Heaven and earth — and even they cannot keep it up long, let alone a man.

Hence: he who works at the Dao is one with the Dao; he who works at De is one with De; he who works at loss is one with loss.

To one with De, the Dao likewise grants De (道亦德之). To one with loss, the Dao likewise loses him.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

The text follows Manuscript A (MS A); the lines 天地而弗能久, 有兄於人乎 and 同於德者, damaged in MS A, are supplied from Manuscript B (MS B). Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 飄風不冬朝: Follows MS A, with 冬 read as 終 "to last out"; MS B writes 飄 as 𦾑 (艹 over 剽), read as 飄 "whirlwind". Wang Bi has 故飄風不終朝.
  • 暴雨不冬日: The manuscripts agree; Wang Bi has 驟雨不終日, with 驟 for 暴.
  • 孰為此 / 天地而弗能久: In MS A 而弗 after 天地 is lost; 能久 was bracketed as a restoration in the 1980 edition and is read directly by the Jicheng from a fragment newly joined by Chen Jian. The line follows MS B. Wang Bi has 孰為此者？天地。天地尚不能久. (MS A, col. 139; Jicheng IV, p. 52, n. 92)
  • 有兄於人乎: In MS A the two graphs for "how much more" are lost; the line follows MS B, whose 有 is read as 又 and 兄 as 況. Wang Bi has 而況於人乎.
  • 故從事而道者同於道: The manuscripts agree; Wang Bi has 故從事於道者，道者同於道, with 於 for 而 and 道者 repeated.
  • 德者同於德: The manuscripts agree. The Jicheng reads both instances of 德 as 得 "to get", as also the 德 of 道亦德之 below.
  • 失者同於失: Follows MS A. The scribe first miswrote the opening 失 as 者, struck it out in red and wrote 失 over it; the 1980 edition printed 者〈失〉. Examining the photographs, the Jicheng finds traces of the 失 written over the red and reads 失 directly. MS B has 失. (Jicheng IV, p. 52, n. 92)
  • 同於德者: MS A reads 同德□, without 於, its 者 lost; the line follows MS B. Wang Bi has 同於道者，道亦樂得之；同於德者，德亦樂得之: neither silk manuscript has the clause on "one with the Dao".
  • 同於失者道亦失之: The manuscripts agree; the Jicheng reads MS A's 失 here directly. Wang Bi has 同於失者，失亦樂得之, followed by two further lines, 信不足焉，有不信焉, which neither silk manuscript has.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

The crux of this chapter is the law of being-one-with, pictured in wind and rain — a warning that much talk and contrived doing cannot last.

"Few words — self-so" — Laozi laying bare the root of governance. Sparse words are few words: without speaking, the people transform of themselves — the sage prizes sparse speech. Much talk makes the rule prying; a prying rule harries the people. The De of not contending and the point of sparse words complete each other, and both come home to the country of non-doing.

The wind-and-rain image: "a whirlwind does not last the morning; a downpour does not last the day" (the silk text writes 冬 for 終 in both lines, and where it has 暴雨, "downpour," Wang Bi has 驟雨). The silk text says 孰為此，天地而弗能久 — "who makes these? Heaven and earth, and even they cannot keep it up long." The Wang Bi text inserts a particle and reads "heaven and earth themselves cannot keep it up" — the breath a touch slower, the sense one. If heaven and earth, with all the force of the self-so, cannot make wind and rain persist — how long can a man's contrived doing last? So the sage follows the stance of the self-so and does not force on the people what they cannot bear.

The point of being-one-with: the silk text and Wang Bi differ a little in words and a great deal in sense. The silk text: "he who works at the Dao is one with the Dao; he who works at De is one with De; he who works at loss is one with loss" — spare words, full sense. Wang Bi adds 道者 — redundant in words, plainer in meaning. Below that, "to one with De, the Dao likewise grants De," the two texts part widely. Wang Bi runs: "one with the Dao — the Dao is glad to have him; one with De — De is glad to have him," three ends in one breath. The silk text says simply: "to one with De, the Dao likewise grants De; to one with loss, the Dao likewise loses him," lacking the first clause, words and sense both spare. I suspect the silk text has dropped a strip here — yet its "the Dao likewise grants De" (the Jicheng reads each 德 here as 得, "to get," which brings it close to Wang Bi's "glad to have him"), read as written, points straight at the pairing: the Dao does not abandon De, and losing does not change either — the two senses facing each other also pass. Further: Wang Bi closes with the two lines "when good faith is not enough, there is mistrust" — which the silk text lacks. Those lines belong to the seventeenth chapter of the received ordering; they are a misplaced strip, slipped in somewhere in Wang Bi's transmission — their sense also passes, so let both stand.

The sense of being-one-with passes into the self-so of resonance. The Commentary on the Words: "same sounds answer each other; same qi seeks each other" — the necessity in the grain of things. Each kind goes to its own. The sage knows this, and so prizes sparse words and non-doing: coming near the Dao, he watches water's goodness at lying low and wakes to the point of not contending — not chasing the boundless with the bounded — and no one in all-under-heaven can contend with him. This is why the Dao lasts long.
