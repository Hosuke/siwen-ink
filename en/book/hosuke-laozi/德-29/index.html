The Book of De · Chapter Twenty-nine · Rivers and Seas

English Translation

That rivers and seas can be kings of the hundred valley-streams (浴) is because they are good at lying low, and so can be kings of the hundred streams.

Hence the sage, wishing to be above the people, must with his words place himself below them; wishing to be before the people, must with his person place himself behind them. So he stands in front, and the people come to no harm; he stands above, and the people feel no weight. All-under-heaven gladly pushes him forward and never tires. Is it not because he does not dispute (无諍), and so no one in all-under-heaven can dispute with him?

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

This chapter follows Manuscript A (MS A); 亓欲先民也 and the final line, damaged in MS A, are supplied from Manuscript B (MS B). Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • □海之所以能為百浴王者: Damaged in both. MS A loses its first graph, 江, and has 之; MS B reads 江海所以能為百浴王□, without 之 and with 者 lost; its 王, a restoration in the 1980 edition, is read directly by the Jicheng from surviving traces. 浴 is read as 谷 "valley". Wang Bi reads 江海所以能為百谷王者. (Jicheng IV, p. 203, n. 129)
  • 以亓善下之: Follows MS A. In MS B 以 is lost, 善, a restoration in the 1980 edition, is read directly by the Jicheng from a fragment stuck to the backing silk, and the line ends in 也. Wang Bi reads 以其善下之. (Jicheng IV, p. 203, n. 130)
  • 是以能為百浴王: Both manuscripts agree; Wang Bi reads 故能為百谷王.
  • 是以聖人之欲上民也: Follows MS A; MS B writes 𦔻 for 聖. Wang Bi reads 是以欲上民, without 聖人之 and 也.
  • 必以亓言下之 / 亓欲先民也 / 必以亓身後之: Both manuscripts agree, except that 民也 is lost in MS A and supplied from MS B. Wang Bi lacks all three instances of 亓: 必以言下之；欲先民，必以身後之.
  • 故居前而民弗害也 / 居上而民弗重也: Follows MS A; MS B reverses the two lines: 故居上而民弗重也，居前而民弗害也. The original editors note that the received text has MS B's order and suspect a transposition in MS A. Wang Bi reads 是以聖人處上而民不重，處前而民不害, in MS B's order, with 處 for 居 and 不 for 弗. (Jicheng IV, p. 34, n. 147)
  • 天下樂隼而弗猒也: Follows MS A; MS B reads 天下皆樂誰而弗猒也, adding 皆. 隼 and 誰 are read as 推 "to push forward", 猒 as 厭 "to tire of". Wang Bi reads 是以天下樂推而不厭.
  • 非以亓无諍與: Follows MS A; MS B reads 不以亓无爭與, its 以, a restoration in the 1980 edition, read directly by the Jicheng from surviving traces. In MS A the silk is split through 諍 and only the left half, somewhat like the 言 element, survives; Gao Ming records 靜, but the text follows the Jicheng in reading 諍, taken as 爭 "to contend". 與 is read as the final particle 歟. Wang Bi reads 以其不爭. (Jicheng IV, p. 203, n. 131)
  • 故天下莫能與爭: MS A preserves only the final 諍, which, the Jicheng notes, was formerly misread as 靜; follows MS B. The original editors restored MS A's lacuna from MS B; the Jicheng notes that all other witnesses, including the Guodian and Peking University manuscripts, have 之 after 與, so MS A's lacuna may hold one graph more. Wang Bi reads 故天下莫能與之爭. (Jicheng IV, p. 34, n. 148)

Commentary

That rivers and seas can be kings of the hundred valley-streams is because they are good at lying low. The silk text writes 浴: water at the left, the image of a bathing-fullness at the right — passing for 谷, the valley. Water's nature runs downward; the ravines and brooks race home to it. Rivers and seas sit below all the streams, so they can contain them and complete their greatness. "Water benefits the ten thousand things and keeps still": just this.

The sage embodies this Dao to steer all-under-heaven. Wishing to be above the people, he must with his words place himself below them; wishing to be before the people, he must with his person place himself behind them. Thus he stands in front and the people come to no harm; stands above and the people feel no weight; and all-under-heaven gladly pushes him forward and never tires. Manuscript A says 非以亓无諍與 (the silk is split down through 諍, and only its left half survives, apparently the speech radical; the 1980 editors read 諍, Gao Ming records 靜, and 諍 is provisionally followed here; the 諍 surviving in manuscript A at the chapter's close, which the Jicheng says was formerly misread as 靜, lends indirect support), where manuscript B and the Wang Bi recension write 爭. 諍 is straight speech that debates right and wrong; 爭 is the grappling and seizing of horns locked. What Laozi slights is not the forthright 諍 but the grappling 爭. Yet 无諍 runs finer than 不爭 — not only forgoing competition with things, but housing not even the thought of verbal dispute. "To take men by being good at lying low, never crossing things: the utmost of De" — is this not the very point? And "no one in all-under-heaven can dispute with him" — not that I leave no room for dispute; rather, there is simply no ground left under heaven from which to dispute him.

Heaven is long and earth lasting because they do not live for themselves; the sage puts his person behind and his person is in front, sets his person aside and his person endures. Water is the same: good at lying low, and the hundred streams come home; the sage good at lying low, and all-under-heaven comes home — one principle. This age, where the racing is fiercer than ever, runs the whole way backward — those who contend for the top end up below; those who contend to be first end up behind; those who contend for profit end up losing. Only he who is willing to dwell low, willing to put his person behind, can keep himself whole and receive the homecoming of all-under-heaven. This is Laozi's unchanging law.
