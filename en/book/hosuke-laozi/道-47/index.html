The Book of Dao · Chapter Three · Setting the People at Peace

English Translation

Exalt no worthies (不上賢), and the people will not contend. Prize no hard-to-get goods, and the people will not steal. Display nothing desirable, and the people will not be thrown into disorder.

Hence the sage's governing: he empties their hearts, fills their bellies, weakens their ambitions, strengthens their bones.

He keeps the people constantly without knowing and without desiring; he makes even the knowing dare not contrive — they merely refrain (弗為) — and there is nothing ungoverned.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

The first line, 使民不亂, 強其骨 and 恒使民无知无欲也 follow Manuscript A (MS A); the rest is damaged in MS A and is supplied from Manuscript B (MS B). Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 不上賢: The manuscripts agree; 上 is read as 尚 "to exalt". Wang Bi has 不尚賢.
  • 使民不爭 / 不貴難得之貨 / 使民不為盜 / 不見可欲: Badly damaged in MS A, which preserves only 民不為 and the 不 of 不見, the latter read directly by the Jicheng; all four lines are supplied from MS B.
  • 使民不亂: Follows MS A; MS B agrees. MS A's 使 is read directly by the Jicheng. In MS A the left half of 亂 is lost and only the hook 乚 remains; MS B writes the reduced form 乿; the Jicheng reads both as 亂 "disorder". Wang Bi has 使民心不亂 "keeps the people's hearts from disorder"; neither silk manuscript has 心.
  • 是以𦔻人之治也: MS A preserves only 是以聲人之 and is broken below; the line follows MS B. MS A writes 聲人, MS B 𦔻人 (耳 beside 口); both are read as 聖人 "the sage". Wang Bi has 是以聖人之治, without 也.
  • 虛亓心 / 實亓腹 / 弱亓志: Lost in MS A and supplied from MS B, whose 亓 is read as 其.
  • 強其骨: Follows MS A; MS B reads 強亓骨.
  • 恒使民无知无欲也: The manuscripts agree; Wang Bi has 常使民無知無欲, without 也.
  • 使夫知不敢弗為而巳: Follows MS B, with 巳 read as 已. MS A reads 使夫□不敢□為□□; its 不敢 and 為 lay within restored lacunae in the 1980 edition, but the Jicheng reads them directly on the strength of two small fragments newly joined. Wang Bi has 使夫智者不敢為也。為無為; neither silk manuscript has 者, both have 弗為而巳 "do not act, and that is all", and neither has 為無為 "do the not-doing". (Jicheng IV, p. 45, n. 17)
  • 則无不治矣: Lost in MS A and supplied from MS B. Wang Bi has 則無不治, without 矣.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

This chapter of the Book of Dao speaks the essentials of governing an age, and the crux is this: cut off what those above exalt, and give the people back to wholeness and simplicity. In their beginnings the people have no knowing and no desiring — clear, pure, unhewn, one with the Dao. Later comes: whatever those above are fond of, those below outdo. Exalt worthies and the people contend; prize goods and the people steal; display the desirable and the people's hearts shake. With the three aflame, all-under-heaven is in turmoil.

So the sage's governing begins by cutting the source. The heart is desire's storehouse — empty it, and appetites cannot enter. The belly is life's root — fill it, and hunger and cold cannot press. Ambition is craving's tip — weaken it, and there is no grasping. Bone is the body's frame — strengthen it, and there is vigor. Four measures together, and the people return of themselves to plain silk and unhewn wood.

Collating the silk text, three differences. First: the silk text has 不上賢, "exalt no worthies," where Wang Bi has 不尚賢 — 上 and 尚 alike mean to hold in honor. Once the name of "worthy" is set up, the people race to it like ducks, scrambling for rank and stipend — this is the sense of "exalt no worthies, and the people will not contend." Second: the silk text has "the people will not be thrown into disorder" (使民不亂), where Wang Bi adds the graph 心 — "the people's hearts not disordered." Either phrasing serves, and the sense runs through. With the desirable displayed, the desiring heart must stir; to keep it unshaken, the sage hides the lovely things and cuts off the outer lure — the crux of governance. Third: the closing lines differ most of all. The silk text has "makes even the knowing not dare — nothing but not-doing" (使夫知不敢，弗為而巳), where Wang Bi has "makes even the clever not dare to do. Do the not-doing, and nothing is ungoverned." As for "keeping the people without knowing and without desiring": this knowing is not witlessness but the knowing that calculates and distinguishes; this desire is not need but grabbing want. To keep the people in their native plainness is not to make them mindless. And 弗為 is not-doing the contrived — not "having doings," and not the formula "doing the not-doing" either. When nothing is contrived, the ten thousand things transform of themselves — this is called perfect order.

Those above do nothing, and the people transform of themselves: the crux of governance is not disturbing. The Confucians' "subdue yourself" and "without desire one is firm" truly run together with this. Ah — those who lose this Dao exalt worthies and inflame contention, prize goods and invite theft, display the desirable and lure disorder — the source fouled, and they ask the stream to run clear; the spring muddied, and they ask for order. Can it be had?
