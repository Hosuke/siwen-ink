The Book of De · Chapter Forty-three · The De of Water

English Translation

Nothing in all-under-heaven is softer or weaker than water — yet for wearing down the hard and strong, nothing can surpass it: nothing can take its place (无以易之).

That water defeats the hard, that the weak defeats the strong — no one in all-under-heaven fails to know it, and none can practice it.

Hence the sage's saying: He who takes upon himself the state's disgrace — he is called the lord of the altars. He who takes upon himself the state's misfortune — he is called the king of all-under-heaven.

Straight words seem their opposite (正言若反).

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

Nothing in all-under-heaven is softer or weaker than water, yet for wearing down the hard and strong nothing can surpass it — nothing can take its place. Set the silk text against the Wang Bi recension: the words are much the same, the flavor utterly apart.

"That water defeats the hard, that the weak defeats the strong — none fails to know it, and none can practice it." Laozi here makes plain water's constant softness, galloping through the hardest under heaven. The chapter on the highest good says "water benefits the ten thousand things and keeps still"; this chapter pushes it to the utmost — water's not-contending is no timidity: it knows contending profits nothing, and so no one in all-under-heaven can contend with it.

The point to savor longest: the silk text writes 水之勝剛, "water defeats the hard," where the Wang Bi recension puts 柔之勝剛, "the soft defeats the hard." The silk text leads with water — first the concrete thing, then the principle of weakness drawn from it. Wang Bi swaps water for softness, dropping the concrete for the abstract, and strays far from Laozi's way of making a point. Again: silk manuscript A has 受邦之詬 and 受邦之不祥 — "the state's disgrace," "the state's misfortune" — where manuscript B and Wang Bi write 國 for 邦. 邦 is the feudal lord's fief; 國, the king's capital. The silk text speaks of lords each keeping his own fief — the true speech of its time, keeping the older sense. And the silk text has the full line 故聖人之言云曰 with its extra 故, marking the carrying-forward of the previous chapter's point; Wang Bi's 是以 points straight at the doctrine, and the savor thins. These are the meddlings of later hands — not to be overlooked.

"Straight words seem their opposite" — four graphs shared by both texts, and the key of the whole chapter. Water flows downward yet wears down what is above; the sage abases himself and the people bear him up — seeming opposite, in fact straight; seeming loss, in fact gain. The Changes says of Qian ䷎ (Modesty), "the modest noble man shepherds himself by lowliness," and all six lines are auspicious — the very point. "Clear stillness can set all-under-heaven right": the sage takes his lesson from water, so he can lay himself below others in soft weakness and keep himself in humble retreat — and become in truth the king of all-under-heaven.

Ah — that everyone knows the soft masters the hard, and none can practice it: the difficulty is not in the practicing; few are those who know the soft for the treasure it is. The Doctrine of the Mean sighs, "few can hold to it long" — is it not said of exactly this?
