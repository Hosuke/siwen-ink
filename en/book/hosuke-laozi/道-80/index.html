The Book of Dao · Chapter Thirty-six · Subtle Clarity

English Translation

Would you close it (翕)? First stretch it wide (張). Would you weaken it? First make it strong. Would you cast it off? First give to it (與). Would you take from it? First bestow upon it (予). This is called subtle clarity (微明).

The soft and weak defeat the strong.

A fish cannot leave the deep. A state's sharp instruments cannot be shown to men.

Commentary

Yaomiao says: this chapter takes four opposed pairs to show that things at their extreme must turn back — and its crux is the two words "subtle clarity."

Stretching at its limit is closing's hinge; strength at its height is weakness's omen; long giving is taking's first move. To gather in, first let out; to weaken, first strengthen. This is not teaching men a technique for handling things: it shows the stance of the ten thousand things — fullness cannot last, stretching cannot hold its extreme: the self-so's law. The Great Commentary says, "moving, changing, never resting, coursing through the six empty places" — the same thought.

Clarity called "subtle": when things first stir in the fine edge, the man has already seen where they will go; before the matter shows, its crack of omen is out, and the sage watches it as one watches water flow, knowing it must reach the sea. As the Book of De's chapter on life and death has it — the companions of death are three in ten: those who go through to the Dao see clearly into ends and beginnings.

"The soft and weak defeat the strong" — one sentence gathering up all of the above. Water's highest good comes near to the Dao: not contending, hence without fault. The sage, wishing to be above the people, places himself below; puts himself behind, not daring to go first, and so can complete the great. This is no petty trick: it is seeing through to the ends and beginnings of things, going through the turns of human feeling, and holding the hinge of the ten thousand things.

A fish cannot leave the deep — out, and it dies. Sharp instruments cannot be shown to men — shown, and the handle is lost. Awe, punishment, and reward, by which the sage rules his ministers: hidden, nothing can spy them out; shown, villainy rides them in. The point of not contending and the sense of putting oneself behind are each other's outside and inside here.

Collated against the Wang Bi recension: the silk text writes 翕、張、去、奪, Wang Bi 歙、興、廢、與. 去 is straighter than 廢; 予 is more of one's own act than 與 — the Guiguzi too has the saying "to take, give in return": the silk text has it right. Four opposed pairs, spare in words, whole in sense; Wang Bi's extra pair （興、廢） may be a copyist's growth — not the silk text's age.

Grasp this, and you know why the sage takes the soft and yields: it is not yielding — it is following the self-so of the Dao and not daring to force. Yielding, and the state is at peace; the people transformed, and the sage's work completes itself. "Put your person behind — and your person is in front": this.
