The Book of Dao · Chapter Thirty-six · Subtle Clarity

English Translation

Would you close it (拾, read as 翕)? First stretch it wide (張). Would you weaken it? First make it strong. Would you cast it off? First give to it (與). Would you take from it? First bestow upon it (予). This is called subtle clarity (徵明, read as 微明).

The soft and weak defeat the strong.

A fish does not leave the deep. A state's sharp instruments cannot be shown to men.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

The chapter largely follows Manuscript A (MS A); 必古強之, damaged in MS A, is supplied from Manuscript B (MS B). Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 將欲拾之: Follows MS A. MS A writes 拾, MS B 㩉 (扌 beside 翕); the Jicheng reads both as 翕 "to close". Wang Bi has 歙.
  • 必古張之: The manuscripts agree; 古 is read as 固 "surely, first", here and in the three lines that follow.
  • 必古強之: In MS A 必古 is lost; the line follows MS B. In MS B a graph after 古 was blotted out by the scribe.
  • 將欲去之 / 必古與之: The manuscripts agree. The Jicheng reads 與 as 舉 "to raise". Wang Bi has 將欲廢之，必固興之, with 廢 for 去 and 興 for 與.
  • 必古予之: Follows MS A; in MS B 之 is lost. Wang Bi has 必固與之, with 與 for 予.
  • 是胃徵明: Follows MS A, which writes 徵, read by the Jicheng as 微 "subtle"; for MS B the Jicheng prints a variant form of 微 (彳, 亘 and 攵 side by side) that has no Unicode code point. Wang Bi has 是謂微明.
  • 柔弱勝強: Follows MS A. MS A's 柔 is written as two 屮 side by side above a horizontal stroke; the 1980 edition and Gao Ming's transcription both reproduce the form, and the 1980 editors took it for 友. The Jicheng notes that it differs from the ancient form of 友 in the Shuowen, traces the original shape, and reads it as 柔 "soft". Since the form has no code point, it is given here as 柔. MS B reads 柔弱朕強, with 朕 read as 勝 "to overcome". Wang Bi has 柔弱勝剛強, with an extra 剛. (Jicheng IV, p. 55, n. 145)
  • 魚不脫於潚: Follows MS A. MS A's 脫 has lost its left side, only 兌 remaining, and is read by the Jicheng as 脫 "to leave"; 潚 is read as 淵 "the deep". MS B reads 魚不可說於淵, with 說 read as 脫. Since MS B, the Peking University manuscript and the received texts all have 可 after 不, the Jicheng suspects that MS A has dropped it. Wang Bi has 魚不可脫於淵. (Jicheng IV, p. 55, n. 146)
  • 邦利器不可以視人: Follows MS A, with 視 read as 示 "to show". MS B reads 國利器不可以示人, avoiding the taboo graph 邦. Wang Bi has 國之利器不可以示人.

Commentary

Yaomiao says: this chapter takes four opposed pairs to show that things at their extreme must turn back — and its crux is the two words "subtle clarity."

Stretching at its limit is closing's hinge; strength at its height is weakness's omen; long giving is taking's first move. To gather in, first let out; to weaken, first strengthen. This is not teaching men a technique for handling things: it shows the stance of the ten thousand things — fullness cannot last, stretching cannot hold its extreme: the self-so's law. The Great Commentary says, "moving, changing, never resting, coursing through the six empty places" — the same thought.

Clarity called "subtle": when things first stir in the fine edge, the man has already seen where they will go; before the matter shows, its crack of omen is out, and the sage watches it as one watches water flow, knowing it must reach the sea. As the Book of De's chapter on life and death has it — the companions of death are three in ten: those who go through to the Dao see clearly into ends and beginnings.

"The soft and weak defeat the strong" — one sentence gathering up all of the above. Water's highest good comes near to the Dao: not contending, hence without fault. The sage, wishing to be above the people, places himself below; puts himself behind, not daring to go first, and so can complete the great. This is no petty trick: it is seeing through to the ends and beginnings of things, going through the turns of human feeling, and holding the hinge of the ten thousand things.

A fish cannot leave the deep — out, and it dies. Sharp instruments cannot be shown to men — shown, and the handle is lost. Awe, punishment, and reward, by which the sage rules his ministers: hidden, nothing can spy them out; shown, villainy rides them in. The point of not contending and the sense of putting oneself behind are each other's outside and inside here.

Collated against the Wang Bi recension: the silk text writes 翕、張、去、奪, Wang Bi 歙、興、廢、與. 去 is straighter than 廢; 予 is more of one's own act than 與 — the Guiguzi too has the saying "to take, give in return": the silk text has it right. Four opposed pairs, spare in words, whole in sense. Wang Bi puts 廢、興 in place of 去、與 and 與 in place of 予 — substitutions all, nothing added — and falls short of the silk text's archaic directness.

Grasp this, and you know why the sage takes the soft and yields: it is not yielding — it is following the self-so of the Dao and not daring to force. Yielding, and the state is at peace; the people transformed, and the sage's work completes itself. "Put your person behind — and your person is in front": this.
