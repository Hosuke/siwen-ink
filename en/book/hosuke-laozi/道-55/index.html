The Book of Dao · Chapter Eleven · The Dark Center

English Translation

Thirty spokes share one hub (轂): where it is empty (當亓无) — there is the cart's use.

Kneaded clay (埏埴) makes a vessel: where it is empty — there is the vessel's use.

A door and a window (戶牖) cut out: where they are empty — there is the room's use.

Hence: presence (有) gives the advantage; absence (无) gives the use.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

This chapter takes three vessels to show how presence and absence complete each other — and comes home to the true function of non-doing (无為).

A cart's thirty spokes gather at one hub: it is exactly at the empty place that the cart has its use. The spokes are the solid; the emptiness at the hub's center is the absence. The solid gives the shape; the absence gives the work — the power of turning lies not in the spokes but in the hub's hollow. Kneaded clay makes a vessel: again, exactly at the empty place the vessel has its use. The walls are the solid; the hollow belly is the absence. The solid forms the vessel; the absence holds the goods — the power of containing lies not in the wall but in the vessel's hollow. A door and a window cut out make a room: again, at the empty place the room has its use. The walls are the solid; the openings are the absence. The solid stands the room up; the absence lets the light through — the power of dwelling lies not in the wall but in the room's hollow.

Three vessels, one law: presence gives the shape, absence gives the use — neither can be dropped. But the world sees only the presence, never the absence, and so loses the use. The Zhuangzi's "In the Human World" says: "everyone knows the use of the useful; no one knows the use of the useless" — exactly this. Heshang Gong: "the vessel is hollow inside, and so it can receive and hold things." The world chases presence and drops absence, chases form and slights the empty — like galloping around the hunting field and losing the hollow one always had. The Kun hexagram ䷁ speaks of earth's thick De carrying all things — it carries them by an empty middle — answering the Daoist function of empty absence.

Collating the two texts: the silk text writes 卅輻同一轂 and 當亓无, Wang Bi 三十輻共一轂 and 當其無. 卅 is the short form of thirty; 亓 is the old graph for 其; 同一 and 共一 are one sense; and the silk text closes each clause with 也 where Wang Bi drops it — small variants, no difference of sense. On the whole, the silk text is nearer the ancient and plain; Wang Bi is tidier and smoother.

The crux of the chapter: absence is not mere emptiness — it is the root of what makes things work. Whatever has a function owes it to its empty part; lose that emptiness and the thing's use cannot stand. Laozi's lone seeing is exactly here: everyone knows to chase presence — and no one knows that absence's work is greater than presence's.
