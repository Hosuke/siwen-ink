The Book of De · Chapter Thirty-six · Knowing the Sickness

English Translation

To know that one does not know (知不知) — that is the highest. Not to know that one does not know (不知不知) — that is the disease.

Hence the sage is without disease: he takes disease as disease, and so is without disease.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

The first three lines follow Manuscript A (MS A); the last two, 以亓病病也 and 是以不病, are lost in MS A and are supplied from Manuscript B (MS B). Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 知不知: Both manuscripts agree. Wang Bi reads 知不知上 and has no 尚矣 after it.
  • 尚矣: Both manuscripts have 尚矣; Wang Bi has 上 without 矣, attached to the preceding phrase.
  • 不知不知病矣: MS A writes 不﹦知﹦病矣, with a repetition mark at the lower right of both 不 and 知 (the color plate shows two short strokes, not four written graphs), in the same pattern as 玄﹦德﹦; it is read here as 不知不知. Gao Ming expands the marks graph by graph as 不不知知; the manuscript itself cannot decide. The original editors' note says that, since all other texts read 不知知，病, the repetition mark on 不 should not be there and is a scribal slip; it also records that 病 was struck through in red. MS B writes 不知﹦病矣, read 不知知，病矣; Wang Bi has 不知知病. (Jicheng IV, p. 36, n. 176)
  • 是以聖人之不病: Follows MS A; MS B writes 𦔻 for 聖, has a lost graph after 不 and ends with 也. Before this line Wang Bi has two additional phrases, 夫唯病病，是以不病, and then reads 聖人不病, without 是以 or 之.
  • 以亓病病也: MS A preserves only 以亓; 病病也 is supplied from MS B, where 病 carries a repetition mark and is read 病病. Both manuscripts write 亓; Wang Bi has 其 and lacks 也.
  • 是以不病: Lost in MS A and taken from MS B; Wang Bi agrees.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

This chapter sets the levels of knowing-that-one-does-not-know, and by them divides sage from common man — spare words, deep sense, and students for ages have let it slip by. Set the silk text against the Wang Bi recension, and you see that a variant is never mere gain or loss of graphs: with them, the depth of meaning parts.

"To know that one does not know — that is the highest." The one who knows the Dao knows that there is what he does not know. The deeper the knowing, the plainer the not-knowing; the shallower the knowing, the more one takes oneself for omniscient. Socrates said, "all I know is that I know nothing" — sages East and West meeting on the same ground, and not by accident.

"Not to know that one does not know — that is the disease." Here manuscript A writes 不﹦知﹦ — each graph carries a repetition mark, as in 玄﹦德﹦ — and read by the rule that repeats the whole phrase, it doubles 不知 (Gao Ming spells the marks out graph by graph as 不不知知; the manuscript itself cannot decide, and Yaomiao provisionally follows the first reading), and the sentence falls with full weight. Manuscript B writes 不知﹦病矣, to be read 不知知，病矣, and the Wang Bi recension 不知知病 — both lack one of the pair. The original editors' note holds that the 不 in manuscript A should carry no repetition mark, a copyist's slip, so that manuscript A too should be read as manuscript B; but read by what manuscript A actually has, the doubled 不知 runs deeper than manuscript B. In 不知不知, the first not-knowing faces the world; the second faces oneself — the unawareness of one's own unawareness. The world, dazzled by its private view, does not know that it does not know, mistakes itself for knowing, and screens itself thereby — a harm deeper than ignorance. Two layers of not-knowing closing together: that man is under a blindness no medicine reaches.

"Hence the sage is without disease: he takes disease as disease." The sage is without disease not because he knows all — but because he knows he does not. 病病: to take one's own blindness for a disease and doctor oneself. The Great Learning's "investigating things and reaching knowledge" is not only plumbing the patterns of the ten thousand things — first it clears the self's screen and restores the original brightness. The silk text's sentence stands whole; Wang Bi splits it in two — full in words, but cracked. The sage escapes disease exactly by the heart that is never self-content: always taking his own blindness for the disease and doctoring it, he never falls gravely ill.

Ah — today's students gallop through recitation and call themselves broadly learned; they live out their days inside the disease and never know it. He whose knowing runs deep does not speak — not because he is mute, but because he knows words cannot carry the meaning whole, and so does not content himself with words. "He who knows does not speak; he who speaks does not know" — just this. Whoever reads this chapter should ask himself: what do I take myself to know, that I truly do not? What do I take myself to see, where I am truly blind? The chapter's whole force is in the one graph 病. The source of the ten thousand evils is not ignorance itself — it is ignorance screening itself, and never knowing.
