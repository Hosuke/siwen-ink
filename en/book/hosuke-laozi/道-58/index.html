The Book of Dao · Chapter Fourteen · The Thread of the Dao

English Translation

Looked at, and not seen — its name is the minute (微). Listened for, and not heard — its name is the rarefied (希). Reached for, and not grasped — its name is the level (夷). These three cannot be probed to their end, and so, blended, they are one.

As for the one: above, it is not bright; below, it is not dark. Unending, unending — ah (尋尋呵) — it cannot be named, and returns home to thinglessness.

Call it the shape of no shape, the image of no thing — call it the flickering and dim (惚恍). Follow it, and you do not see its back; meet it, and you do not see its head.

Hold the Dao of today (執今之道) to steer the presence of today, and so to know the beginning of old: this is called the thread of the Dao (道紀).

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

The Dao as a thing is beyond sight and hearing, beyond the grasping hand, and so Laozi, having no better way, gives it three names: the minute, the rarefied, the level. "Looked at and not seen, listened for and not heard": the Dao stands outside form and name, beyond what the private ear and eye can sound. The Zhuangzi has the voice called No-Beginning say: "the Dao cannot be heard — what is heard is not it; the Dao cannot be seen — what is seen is not it; the Dao cannot be spoken — what is spoken is not it." Just this.

The silk text writes 捪之而弗得, "reached for and not grasped," where Wang Bi has 搏之不得, "grappled and not gotten": both pass, but the silk text's 捪, with its hand-sign, means stroking and following, and so shows the Dao as not far from men at all. "These three cannot be probed to their end" — Wang Bi writes 致詰, "questioned to the limit": near in sense, but 至計, "reckoned to the end," better shows the Dao standing past all reckoning. Three names given, and none to be probed — so, blended, they are one. And "blended into one" is not three compounded into one: the Dao by root refuses names, standing above the distinctions of the three. What the senses divide is the difference of the ten thousand things; what the Dao holds whole is their oneness. Laozi neither dotes on the old nor flaunts the new — every time is the Dao's time, and the Dao, unsayable, cannot be interrogated by names.

"As for the one: above, not bright; below, not dark" — the sense of the flickering-and-dim is complete. 攸 borrows for 皦, "bright"; 忽 is the opposite of 昧, "dark." Above, it is not bright-clear; below, not dark-dim: past the pair of light and dark, one undivided whole. "Follow it and you do not see its back; meet it and you do not see its head" — the silk text's phrasing better shows the Dao without head or tail. The Great Commentary: "the spirit has no direction; the Change has no body" — one sense.

And then the distinction of holding-the-present against holding-the-past: the silk text reads 執今之道，以御今之有 — "hold the Dao of today to steer the presence of today" — where the Wang Bi recension reads 執古之道, "hold the ancient Dao." Each has its ground. The silk text's 无物 is the Daoists' older writing. "To know the beginning of old: this is called the thread of the Dao." The thread of the Dao is the Dao's guiding cord — not a record of history but the sense of the Dao threading through time. Knowing the beginning of old is how one passes through to today and holds today: the Dao stands beyond time, and stands inside time — and its use never ends.
