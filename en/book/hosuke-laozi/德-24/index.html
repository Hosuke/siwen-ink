The Book of De · Chapter Twenty-four · Dwelling Low

English Translation

A great state is the downstream — the female of all-under-heaven, the meeting-water (交) of all-under-heaven.

The female constantly defeats the male by stillness (靚); being still, she properly stays low.

A great state, lowering itself to a small state, takes the small state; a small state, lowering itself to a great state, is taken in by the great state. So the one lowers itself in order to take; the other lowers itself and is taken in.

The great state wants no more than to gather and feed men; the small state wants no more than to enter and serve.

Both get what they want — then it is the great that should stay low.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

In this chapter Laozi takes water as the figure for the dealings of states, and the whole way of great and small states together is one word: low. Water's nature runs down and does not contend; the rivers flow east to go home to the sea; the ravine-streams race to the hollow. Let a great state be the downstream of all-under-heaven, and the ten thousand states come home to it — is that chance?

The female of all-under-heaven, the meeting-water of all-under-heaven: 牝 is the female — still, and by stillness defeating the male. A great state like the female does not trample the small by force; it summons by stillness and takes by lying low. "The female constantly defeats the male by 靚": silk manuscript A writes 靚, read here for 靜, stillness; manuscript B and the Wang Bi text write 靜 outright — differing graphs, one sense. Stillness is no cowardice — still, and therefore able to receive; set below, it defeats what is above — the self-so of the Dao. When the great state humbles itself, the small states attach themselves of their own accord — just so.

A great state lowering itself takes the small; a small state lowering itself is taken in by the great; each gets its wish. The silk text says 或下以取，或下而取 — one lowers in order to take, one lowers and is taken. 以取 is the great state's initiative, gathering and feeding men; 而取 is the small state's reception, entering and serving. A distinction carried in the syntax itself — exactly where the old writers' precision shows.

The close: both get their wish; then the great should stay low. Both are satisfied, but the burden lies on the great. Why? The high, from on high, find going low hard; the low, low already, find it easy. A great state stands high — that it can lower itself is what makes it worthy; a small state is low already — its lowering is nothing remarkable. Hence "the great should stay low": no excess of modesty in the sage, but the deep self-knowledge of one who knows the Dao and knows his seat. Water benefits the ten thousand things and keeps still, dwelling where the crowd loathes to be — is not the key to a thousand ages of statecraft right here?
