The Book of De · Chapter Eighteen · Holding De Within

English Translation

He whose held De (德) is thick is like a naked babe (赤子). Wasp and scorpion, viper and snake do not sting him; snatching birds and beasts of prey do not strike him. Bones weak, sinews soft — and the grip firm. Knowing nothing yet of the joining of female and male, his member rises stiff: the utmost of seed-essence (精). Wailing all day without going hoarse: the utmost of harmony.

Harmony is called the constant; knowing harmony is called seeing-clear. Adding to life is called an ill omen (祥); the heart driving the qi is called forcing.

A thing full-grown is next old: call it not-Dao. Not-Dao, and an early end.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

For thickness of held De, nothing compares with the naked babe. Not that the babe's De is thick — the babe's De is whole. The babe has no knowing and no desiring; its pure qi is not yet thinned; the seed-essence is full and the spirit settled; and the creatures that harm do not cross it — the proof of De entire. Wasp and scorpion do not sting, beasts of prey do not strike, because it runs against nothing. It wails the whole day and the throat never cracks, because spirit and qi are whole and round. Bones weak, sinews soft, and the grip firm — the firm grip is the image of essence not leaking away. Not yet knowing the joining of female and male, and the member stirs — the stirring is the sign of essence full by nature, without desire. When essence is whole, qi is whole; when qi is whole, the voice does not crack. A whole day's wailing: harmony at its utmost.

Silk manuscript A says 和曰常，知和曰明 (manuscript B agrees with Wang Bi): harmony is called the constant; knowing harmony is called seeing-clear. The Wang Bi recension re-cuts it: 知和曰常，知常曰明, inserting one more step, "knowing the constant," and the turn grows busy. The silk text is more direct: harmony itself is the constant; to know harmony is itself to see clear — no intermediary, and the great clarity stands unscreened. A difference of graphs that must be weighed.

"Adding to life is called an ill omen": 祥 here is the omen of the uncanny. Adding to life means loading things on top of life: not a failure to nourish, but nourishing overdone, until it wounds life's own self-so. The tonics and fortifications the age is fond of are all the error of adding to life. "The heart driving the qi is called forcing": when the heart's intent whips the blood-breath along, that is contrived bravado, not the unforced harmony of non-doing. Forcing is not harmony; full-grown, a thing turns old; the man of not-Dao ends early.

So De entire lies in the unchiseled, and the unchiseled lies in knowing harmony. The sage's return to the infant does not regress a man into a child; it teaches him not to chisel his self-so nature with contrivance. Drop cleverness, cut off the appetites, reach emptiness and keep stillness, until the heart takes no stain and the qi no warp: long life and lasting sight — what worry then of age and death?
