The Book of De · Chapter Eighteen · Holding De Within

English Translation

He whose held De (德) is thick is like a naked babe (赤子). Wasp and scorpion, viper and snake do not sting him; snatching birds and beasts of prey do not strike him. Bones weak, sinews soft — and the grip firm. Knowing nothing yet of the joining of female and male, his member rises stiff: the utmost of seed-essence (精). Wailing all day without going hoarse: the utmost of harmony.

Harmony is called the constant; knowing harmony is called seeing-clear. Adding to life is called an ill omen (祥); the heart driving the qi is called forcing.

A thing full-grown is next old: call it not-Dao. Not-Dao, and an early end.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

This chapter follows Manuscript A (MS A); where MS A is damaged (含德之厚者, 未知牝牡之會而朘怒, 精之至也 and the last two lines) it is supplied from Manuscript B (MS B). Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 含德之厚者: In MS A 含德 is lost and is supplied from MS B; 者 is read directly by the Jicheng. Wang Bi reads 含德之厚, without 者.
  • 逢𢔯𧍥地弗螫: Follows MS A. 逢 is read as 蜂 "wasp", 𢔯 (彳 beside 剌) as 蠆 "scorpion", 𧍥 (虫 beside 畏) as 虺 "viper", and 地 as 蛇 "snake". MS B reads 蠭癘虫蛇弗赫; its 癘 is re-read by Chen Jian, followed by the Jicheng, as a graph built on 萬, the ancient form of 蠆. Wang Bi reads 蜂蠆虺蛇不螫. (Jicheng IV, p. 201, n. 79)
  • 擢鳥猛獸弗搏: Follows MS A. The 1980 edition normalized 擢 to 攫; following Gao Ming, the Jicheng reads 擢. MS B reads 據鳥孟獸弗捕, using 孟 for 猛 and 捕 for 搏. Wang Bi splits this into two lines: 猛獸不據，攫鳥不搏. (Jicheng IV, p. 26, n. 96)
  • 骨弱筋柔而握固: Follows MS A; MS B reads 骨筋弱柔而握固, with 筋 and 弱 transposed.
  • 未知牝牡之會而朘怒: Follows MS B. The original editors restored the two graphs after 牝牡 in MS A as 之會 from MS B and read the graph after 而 as 朘. Chen Jian has since joined a fragment to the ends of these lines: 之 is clear, but only the top of the next graph survives and it fits neither 會 nor 合, while the graph after 而 is also illegible, so the Jicheng gives □ for both, keeping the restored 怒 at the end. Wang Bi reads 未知牝牡之合而全作. (Jicheng IV, p. 26, n. 98)
  • 精之至也: Follows MS B; in MS A 之 is lost, and a check mark follows the line.
  • 終曰號而不嚘: Follows MS A. MS A's 曰 is a miswriting of 日 "day", printed 曰〈日〉 in the Jicheng; its 嚘 is written as a graph with 山 above 发, which the Jicheng reads as 嚘 (嗄 "hoarse"). MS B reads 冬日號而不□: the 1980 edition completed the last graph as 嚘 on the model of MS A, but the Jicheng, able to make out only the 口 radical, replaces it with □. Wang Bi reads 終日號而不嗄. (Jicheng IV, p. 201, n. 80)
  • 和之至也: Follows MS A. In MS B the 1980 edition restored 至 in the lower half of the line; the Jicheng reads it in the upper half from a fragment stuck to the backing silk, and reads 也 from the offset. (Jicheng IV, p. 201, n. 81)
  • 和曰常: Follows MS A. The original editors restored MS B as 知和曰常 after the received texts; Chen Jian, reading the offset on the backing silk, found room for only one graph between 曰 and the preceding 也, which can only be 和, so the Jicheng reads □（和）曰常, likewise without 知. Wang Bi reads 知和曰常. (Jicheng IV, p. 27, n. 100; p. 201, n. 82)
  • 知和曰明: Follows MS A; MS B reads 知常曰眀, and Wang Bi 知常曰明. The 1980 edition regarded MS A's 和 here as a miswriting of 常. (Jicheng IV, p. 27, n. 100)
  • 益生曰祥: Both manuscripts agree; MS B's 曰, bracketed as a restoration in 1980, is now read directly from the offset. (Jicheng IV, p. 201, n. 83)
  • 物壯即老: Follows MS A, whose 物壯, bracketed as a restoration in 1980, is now read directly from a newly joined fragment; MS B reads 物壯則老, its 壯 read from the offset. Wang Bi agrees with MS B. (Jicheng IV, p. 27, n. 101; p. 201, n. 84)
  • 胃之不道 / 不道蚤巳: Follows MS B, where both 不 and 道 carry repetition marks, giving 胃之不道，不道蚤巳; 蚤 is read as 早 "early" and 巳 as 已 "to end". In MS A the repetition mark after 道 and the graphs 早已 are lost and were bracketed as restorations in 1980; a fragment resembling 以 or 巳 occupies the position of 早, and the Jicheng provisionally keeps the 1980 reading. Wang Bi reads 謂之不道，不道早已. (Jicheng IV, p. 27, n. 101)

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

For thickness of held De, nothing compares with the naked babe. Not that the babe's De is thick — the babe's De is whole. The babe has no knowing and no desiring; its pure qi is not yet thinned; the seed-essence is full and the spirit settled; and the creatures that harm do not cross it — the proof of De entire. Wasp and scorpion do not sting, beasts of prey do not strike, because it runs against nothing. It wails the whole day and the throat never cracks, because spirit and qi are whole and round. Bones weak, sinews soft, and the grip firm — the firm grip is the image of essence not leaking away. Not yet knowing the joining of female and male, and the member stirs — the stirring is the sign of essence full by nature, without desire. When essence is whole, qi is whole; when qi is whole, the voice does not crack. A whole day's wailing: harmony at its utmost.

Silk manuscript A says 和曰常，知和曰明: harmony is called the constant; knowing harmony is called seeing-clear. The Wang Bi recension re-cuts it: 知和曰常，知常曰明, inserting one more step, "knowing the constant," and the turn grows busy. The silk text is more direct: harmony itself is the constant; to know harmony is itself to see clear — no intermediary, and the great clarity stands unscreened. Manuscript B also lacks 知 in the first clause, reading □曰常, where the lost graph can only be 和; only its second clause, 知常曰眀, agrees with Wang Bi. A difference of graphs that must be weighed.

"Adding to life is called an ill omen": 祥 here is the omen of the uncanny. Adding to life means loading things on top of life: not a failure to nourish, but nourishing overdone, until it wounds life's own self-so. The tonics and fortifications the age is fond of are all the error of adding to life. "The heart driving the qi is called forcing": when the heart's intent whips the blood-breath along, that is contrived bravado, not the unforced harmony of non-doing. Forcing is not harmony; full-grown, a thing turns old; the man of not-Dao ends early.

So De entire lies in the unchiseled, and the unchiseled lies in knowing harmony. The sage's return to the infant does not regress a man into a child; it teaches him not to chisel his self-so nature with contrivance. Drop cleverness, cut off the appetites, reach emptiness and keep stillness, until the heart takes no stain and the qi no warp: long life and lasting sight — what worry then of age and death?
