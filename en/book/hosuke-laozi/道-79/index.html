The Book of Dao · Chapter Thirty-five · The Great Image

English Translation

Hold the great image (執大象) — all-under-heaven comes to you. Comes to you and is not harmed: peace, evenness, greatness.

Music and dainties (樂與餌) — past their limit, they cease (過格止).

Hence the Dao, when it issues in words, says: Bland, ah (談呵) — it has no flavor. Look for it, and it will not meet the eye. Listen for it, and it will not meet the ear. Use it — and it cannot be used up (不可既).

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

Hold the great image, and all-under-heaven comes to you. The great image is the Dao's whole body: no form, no name, and the ten thousand things take it for their ancestor. "Hold the great image — all-under-heaven comes to you": the sage cradles the One and keeps the unhewn, not troubling the people with cleverness, not cutting things up with punishments and names, and the people of all-under-heaven come home of themselves, unharmed. The Dao patterns on the self-so (自然) and abides in non-doing (无為). Music and dainties are what human appetite delights in: they hold only while they last — past the appetite, they cannot stay. So "music and dainties — past their limit, they cease": pleasure has its end. The sage's rule does not lean on rewards and coaxings to please the people's hearts, and the people fulfill their own natures — the effect of peace, evenness, greatness: how it differs from rule by decrees.

The Dao, when it issues in words, is bland and flavorless; looked for, it will not meet the eye; listened for, it will not meet the ear; used, it cannot be used up. Why is the Dao flavorless? Because its substance is the self-so and non-doing, borrowing no ornament.

Now the differences: the silk text writes 亓 for Wang Bi's 其 — old graph against new; and 淡呵 for Wang Bi's 淡乎 — 呵 and 乎 are both breath-particles, but 呵 is the older breath, the silk text's native color. Weightiest: the silk text writes 過格止, where Wang Bi has 過客止, "the passing guest stops." Read as 客: the pleasures of sound and color are a passing guest's one-night stay, never rivaling the Dao's bland permanence. Read as 格: music and dainties run out in their season, while the flavorless Dao cannot be used up. And it answers "great fashioning makes no cuts": the sage's rule does not fence all-under-heaven in with decrees — and each thing fulfills its nature.

The Dao's substance is emptiest, and its use never fails. Let the ruler hold this Dao and face all-under-heaven: the ten thousand things stand right in their nature-and-charge, and the rule of non-doing is complete. The Confucians' "without desire one is firm" and the Daoists' prizing of flavorlessness — one gauge. Bland is not scant flavor: it is needing nothing from outside. When the ten thousand things are sufficient in it, all-under-heaven comes to you and is not harmed.
