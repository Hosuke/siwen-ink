The Book of Dao · Chapter Thirty-five · The Great Image

English Translation

Hold the great image (執大象) — all-under-heaven comes to you. Comes to you and is not harmed: peace, evenness, greatness.

Music and dainties (樂與餌) — past their limit, they cease (過格止).

Hence the Dao, when it issues in words, says: Bland, ah (談呵) — it has no flavor. Look for it, and it will not meet the eye. Listen for it, and it will not meet the ear. Use it — and it cannot be used up (不可既).

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

The chapter largely follows Manuscript A (MS A); 天下往 and 視之不足見也, damaged in MS A, are supplied from Manuscript B (MS B). Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 執大象: The manuscripts agree. MS A's 執 is faint in ink, and in the photographs of Gao Ming's transcription it resembles 埶; but on the plates its left element is 幸, with the vertical stroke running through, not 坴, and both the 1980 edition and Gao Ming's transcription read 執 "to hold". 執 is correct.
  • 天下往: Follows MS B; in MS A 天下 is lost. In both manuscripts 往 carries a repetition mark, giving 天下往，往而不害, as in Wang Bi.
  • 安平大: Both manuscripts write 大, which the Jicheng reads as 泰 "great peace"; Gao Ming's transcription gives 太. Wang Bi has 安平太.
  • 樂與餌: Follows MS A; in MS B 餌 is lost.
  • 過格止: The manuscripts agree; 格 is read as 客 "guest". Wang Bi has 過客止.
  • 故道之出言也: The manuscripts agree; Wang Bi has 道之出口.
  • 曰談呵其无味也: Follows MS A, which writes 談, read as 淡 "bland" (so also Gao Ming's transcription); MS B reads 淡呵亓无味也. Wang Bi has 淡乎其無味, without 曰 and with 乎 for 呵.
  • 視之不足見也 / 聽之不足聞也: The first line follows MS B, MS A having lost 視之; in the second the manuscripts agree. Wang Bi lacks 也 in both.
  • 用之不可既也: The manuscripts agree; Wang Bi has 用之不足既.

Commentary

Hold the great image, and all-under-heaven comes to you. The great image is the whole substance of the Dao: no form, no name, and the ten thousand things take it for their ancestor. "Hold the great image — all-under-heaven comes to you": the sage cradles the One and keeps the unhewn, not troubling the people with cleverness, not cutting things up with punishments and names, and the people of all-under-heaven come home of themselves, unharmed. The Dao patterns on the self-so (自然) and abides in non-doing (无為). Music and dainties are what human appetite delights in: they hold only while they last — past the appetite, they cannot stay. So "music and dainties — past their limit, they cease": pleasure has its end. The sage's rule does not lean on rewards and coaxings to please the people's hearts, and the people fulfill their own natures — the effect of peace, evenness, greatness: how it differs from rule by decrees.

The Dao, when it issues in words, is bland and flavorless; looked for, it will not meet the eye; listened for, it will not meet the ear; used, it cannot be used up. Why is the Dao flavorless? Because its substance is the self-so and non-doing, borrowing no ornament.

Now the differences: manuscript B writes 亓 (manuscript A, 其) for Wang Bi's 其 — old graph against new; and manuscript A writes 談呵 (manuscript B, 淡呵) for Wang Bi's 淡乎 — 呵 and 乎 are both breath-particles, but 呵 is the older breath, the silk text's native color. Weightiest: the silk text writes 過格止, where Wang Bi has 過客止, "the passing guest stops." The 1980 editors read 格 as 客, and then the silk text and Wang Bi agree; I venture that 格 may also be read as written, and then each reading has its own home. Read as 客: the pleasures of sound and color are a passing guest's one-night stay, never rivaling the Dao's bland permanence. Read as 格: music and dainties run out in their season, while the flavorless Dao cannot be used up. And it answers "great fashioning makes no cuts": the sage's rule does not fence all-under-heaven in with decrees — and each thing fulfills its nature.

The Dao's substance is emptiest, and its use never fails. Let the ruler hold this Dao and face all-under-heaven: the ten thousand things stand right in their nature-and-charge, and the rule of non-doing is complete. The Confucians' "without desire one is firm" and the Daoists' prizing of flavorlessness — one gauge. Bland is not scant flavor: it is needing nothing from outside. When the ten thousand things are sufficient in it, all-under-heaven comes to you and is not harmed.
