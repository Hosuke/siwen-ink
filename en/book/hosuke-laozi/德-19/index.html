The Book of De · Chapter Nineteen · The Dark Sameness

English Translation

He who knows does not speak (弗言); he who speaks does not know.

Stop the openings, shut the doors; soften the shining, join the dust; blunt the sharp, untie the tangle — this is called the dark sameness (玄同).

Hence you cannot get him close, nor get him distant; cannot profit him, nor harm him; cannot ennoble him, nor debase him. Therefore he is the prized of all-under-heaven.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

"He who knows does not speak; he who speaks does not know" — Laozi's testimony about himself, and the five thousand words' own footnote. The Dao is gotten by words and lost by words: gotten beyond them, lost inside them. The silk text's 弗 is steeper than Wang Bi's 不: the knower does not merely refrain from speaking — he refuses to; the speaker does not merely fail to know — his speaking screens his knowing. A confusion a thousand years old, settled the day the silk text came out of the ground.

Stop the openings and shut the doors; soften the shining and join the dust; blunt the sharp and untie the tangle — these six are not six items on a curriculum: they are six faces of the dark sameness. Stop and shut, to end the outward chase; soften and join, to blur self and things; blunt and untie, to still the inner contest. None of the three plays favorites, and together they are 玄同. To soften the shining and join the dust: gather in the blaze and keep the pattern sheathed, dazzling no one; walk among the common crowd, differing from no one. With the light banked inward, none can sound your depths; with your dust mixed into the road's, none can find your edges. It answers "the highest good: governing as water — dwelling where the crowd loathes to be."

"Blunt the sharp, untie the tangle" — the silk text writes 紛 where the Wang Bi recension writes 分. 分 is the first fork of distinction-making; 紛 is the tangled aftermath. To untie the fork is to work at the source; to untie the tangle is already to be sweeping up results: Wang Bi's 分 is the reading that goes to the root. But the graphs passed for each other in the old script, and each text has its gain; let both stand.

"Cannot be gotten close nor distanced, profited nor harmed, ennobled nor debased" — not that the sage repels these; he gives them nothing to land on. Like a mirror meeting things, they pass and nothing stays. It meets all and detains none, reflects all and answers all: what closeness or distance, what profit or harm, what honor or disgrace could apply? This is the point of "the highest De does not hold to De." The sage is the prized of all-under-heaven exactly because he does not accept the world's prizing. Accept nobility, and the split of noble-and-base exists; accept nothing, and there is no split: the crowning paradox of the dark sameness.

Ah — the world hunts the Dao in words, not knowing that words cannot carry it across. What cannot be told in the telling: the "dregs of the ancients" that wheelwright Bian spoke of, chisel in hand — Zhuangzi's sigh. Mencius says "seek the strayed heart"; the Great Learning says "the reaching of knowledge lies in the investigation of things" — where Confucian and Daoist differ is the method used; where they are one is the ground attained. Let a student once truly grasp "does not speak," and the dark sameness is not far.
