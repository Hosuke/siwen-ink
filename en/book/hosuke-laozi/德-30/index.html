The Book of De · Chapter Thirty · At Home in Peace

English Translation

A small state, few people.

Let implements by the tens and hundreds go unused. Make the people take death gravely and shun distant migration. Though there are carts everywhere (周), nowhere to ride them; though there are armor and blades, nowhere to array them. Bring the people back to knotted cords (結繩) for their reckoning.

They savor their food, delight in their dress, rejoice in their customs, and rest in their homes.

Neighbor states within sight of each other, the cries of chickens and dogs within earshot, and the people, to old age and death, never come and go.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

This chapter describes the crowning work of perfect order, and its crux is three words: small, few, and enough. "A small state, few people" is no contempt: it is taking smallness for the work and fewness for the De. A great state breeds a swarm of affairs; a numerous people breeds a swarm of desires. Many affairs, and craft and fraud are born; many desires, and contention rises. Only the small can easily hold to unhewn wood; only the few can easily know what is enough, and so the rule of non-doing moves among them.

Set the silk text beside the Wang Bi recension, and there are fine points worth saying. The silk text writes 邦 where Wang Bi has 國: 邦 is the old name from the house of Zhou; the silk text, nearer the ancient, keeps it as it was. Again: the silk text says 使十百人之器毋用, "implements by the tens and hundreds, gone unused," where Wang Bi has "though there be tools of the tens and hundreds, they go unused." The silk text's 毋用 is a clean, flat never: more decisive than Wang Bi's turning phrasing, and the plainer for a teaching that prizes simplicity. And the graph 周 in "carts and boats everywhere, nowhere to ride them," which Wang Bi lacks: the 周 makes it universal: not an occasional disuse but a whole world with no need. Presence gives the advantage, absence gives the use: tools possessed and never ridden are the plainest proof that the usefulness of having loses to the usefulness of not-having. Armor and blades are the same: weapons exist not for show but against the unforeseen; when the people's nature is honest, contending with none, there is nothing to array: the sense of "governing a great state as you would cook a small fish": stir it often and you spoil its sheen; harry the people and it is the same.

"Make the people take death as weighty and stay far from moving": the silk text drops the linking particle and the sentence runs on one breath. Taking death as weighty, they do not rush at peril; staying far from moving, they are settled in their soil and glad in their homes. Heshang Gong reads it as "weighty toward death, long toward life": he who values life does not die lightly; he who is glad in his home does not wander far: the grain of the self-so. "Sweet their food, fine their dress, content their customs, settled their homes": all the effects of a people sufficient in themselves. Sweet food needs no rare dishes; fine dress needs no brocade: knowing enough, everything is sweet; at peace in their lot, everything is joy. Is this not the same arrival as the Great Learning's illuminating the bright De — by another road? The Confucian illuminates the bright De and renews the people; the Daoist lets the people suffice in themselves: the crux in both is that each stands right in its own nature and lot.

"Bring the people back to knotted cords for their reckoning": a return to the plainness of high antiquity. Quit ornament, honor substance; trim the crowded, take the simple. This is not making the people stupid: it is the Dao's flower dropped, and the beginning of folly made whole. "To old age and death, never coming and going": within sight, within earshot: that is how near they are; never in a lifetime crossing the neighbor's gate — that is how sufficient they are. Near but never joined, aged and never in touch: not that the ruler forbids the coming and going; the people have no need of it. Sima Qian quotes this passage for the image of an age well governed, and adds, "were one to make this the task of the late world, sealing up the people's ears and eyes, it could hardly be done": not that the saying is wrong, but that times have moved, and a sage's words cannot be sung with the pillar glued.

Yet Yaomiao ventures a further seeing: this chapter is not a political blueprint alone: it shows the truth of the Dao. The Dao's truth is for tending the body; its surplus, for governing state and house; its husks, for all-under-heaven. The body cultivated, then the house evened; the house evened, then the state ordered: Confucian and Daoist, two roads, one arrival. Every heart can be the plain world of a small state with few people: not an outer layout but an inner sufficiency; not a retreat in history but a keeping whole of nature and lot. Only the sage dwells in the work of non-doing and walks the teaching without words, letting the people each fulfill their nature and rest in their lot, and so the image of perfect order can be seen again today.
