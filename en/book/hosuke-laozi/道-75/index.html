The Book of Dao · Chapter Thirty-one · Honoring the Left

English Translation

Arms are instruments of ill omen (不祥之器): things may well loathe them, and so those who have desires do not dwell with them.

The noble man in peace honors the left; in arms he honors the right.

So arms are not the noble man's instruments. Arms are instruments of ill omen: he uses them only when he must, prizing sharpness and speed (銛龐為上).

He does not find them beautiful. To find them beautiful is to delight in killing men.

He who delights in killing men can never have his way in all-under-heaven.

Hence in affairs of good fortune the left is the seat of honor; in affairs of mourning, the right.

Hence the deputy general sits at the left, the commanding general at the right — meaning: they take their places by the rites of mourning.

When many are slain, stand with sorrow and stay (以悲依立之). A victory won — receive it with the rites of mourning.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

Arms are instruments of ill omen — the utmost contrary of heaven and earth. This chapter makes plain the sage's using arms only when he must, and its crux is meeting them with grief and reverence — not preening on martial work.

The silk text opens 夫兵者不祥之器也, where the Wang Bi text reads 夫佳兵者 — an extra 佳, "fine." "Fine arms" would prettify the weapon; drop the 佳 and keep 兵, and the ill omen of arms stands all the plainer. Arms are the instrument of ruin; war is the dangerous affair; the sage uses them only when he must — just so.

Then the way of using them. The silk text writes 銛龐為上, "prizing sharpness and speed," where Wang Bi has 恬淡為上, "prizing calm detachment" — the great variant of the chapter. Yaomiao ventures: the silk text's 銛龐 takes sharpness and swiftness, and it foots what follows — "he does not find them beautiful": the business of arms is already past all calm detachment, and if one then takes killing for beauty, the delight-in-killing can never have its way. Wang Bi's "calm detachment" runs in one thread with the previous chapter: detachment is the sage's heart's posture — winning and not finding it beautiful is the heart's detachment. The two readings join; there is no choosing between them.

As for the left and right of fortune and mourning: in the old military ordering, fortunate affairs honored the left and mournful ones the right. Hence the deputy general sits at the left, the commanding general at the right: they take their places by the rites of mourning. The silk text says 以悲依立之, "stand attending them in grief," where Wang Bi has 以哀悲泣之, "greet them with weeping grief": the silk text carries the deeper sense — 悲依 is attending on the slain with a grieving heart, not merely the weeping. To mourn the many slain: grief is what keeps the humane heart whole; rites are what fit heaven's pattern. The Book of Changes: "the army must march by its law, or there is ruin" — when an army rises, if it is not measured by rites, the ruin is the greatest. Laozi's point answers the Changes.

And one more word: arms are ill-omened, the man of the Dao does not dwell with them — yet the sage does not abolish them either: it is only when he must. Not contending is the hinge of the whole book. The highest good: governing as water — water benefits the ten thousand things and keeps still — and so the sage puts himself behind, not first; dwells low, not refusing. Mencius: "he who practices humaneness with De is king" — Tang with seventy li, King Wen with a hundred li, and all-under-heaven came home to them, because they did not contend. Laozi's "do not force all-under-heaven with arms" answers Mencius's winning men by De. The sage sits above and the people feel no weight; stands in front and the people come to no harm; all-under-heaven gladly pushes him forward and never tires — for no reason but that he does not contend. He who delights in killing men can never have his way in all-under-heaven — Laozi's deepest warning. Which lord of later ages has ever truly tasted it?
