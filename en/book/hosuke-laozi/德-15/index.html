The Book of De · Chapter Fifteen · Keeping to the Mother

English Translation

All-under-heaven has a beginning: take it for the mother of all-under-heaven.

Having got the mother, know the children by her; then return and keep to the mother, and to the end of the body there is no peril.

Stop the openings (悶, read as 兌), shut the doors, and to the end of your days you will not be worn. Open the openings, pursue the affairs, and to the end of your days you will be past saving.

To see the small is called seeing-clear (眀); to keep to the soft is called strength.

Use the shining (光), then return home to the clear (明), and leave no calamity on the body: this is called inheriting the constant (襲常).

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

This chapter follows Manuscript A (MS A); where MS A is damaged (以知亓子, 見小曰眀) it is supplied from Manuscript B (MS B), and 終身不棘 combines the two. Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 天下有始: Both manuscripts agree. MS A opens the chapter with a chapter-division dot. (Jicheng IV, p. 21, n. 76)
  • 𢟪得亓母: Follows MS A, which writes 既 in a variant form, read as 既; MS B writes 既, after which the scribe first miscopied 得 and then circled it out in black ink. (Jicheng IV, p. 201, n. 61)
  • 以知亓子: 子 is lost in MS A and supplied from MS B. Wang Bi follows with the line 既知其子, and MS B too has 既知亓子, but MS A never had it; the original editors took it as an omission, and the Jicheng supplies it with the sign for omitted text. (Jicheng IV, p. 21, n. 79)
  • 沒身不殆: Follows MS A; MS B writes 佁 for 殆.
  • 塞亓悶: Follows MS A, which places a chapter-division dot before this line, thus dividing the received chapter 52 into two. MS B writes 㙂 for 悶; Wang Bi has 兌 "openings". (Jicheng IV, p. 21, n. 76)
  • 終身不堇: Follows MS A; MS B writes 冬 for 終. Wang Bi reads 終身不勤.
  • 啟亓悶 / 濟亓事: Follows MS A; MS B writes 㙂 for 悶 and 齊 for 濟. MS B's 事 was bracketed as a restoration in the 1980 edition; the Jicheng reads it directly from the tip of its surviving vertical stroke. Wang Bi reads 開其兌，濟其事. (Jicheng IV, p. 201, n. 64)
  • 終身不棘: In MS A the two graphs after 終身 are lost; the original editors restored 不棘 from MS B, but the Jicheng, noting that MS A has 濟亓事 just above, expects a word of the 不救 kind rhyming with 事 and replaces the restored 棘 with □. MS B reads 冬□不棘, 冬 being read as 終; the 1980 edition restored 終身 at the head of the lower half of the line, whereas the Jicheng reads 冬 at the foot of the upper half from a fragment stuck to the backing silk. Wang Bi reads 終身不救. (Jicheng IV, p. 22, n. 82; p. 201, n. 65)
  • 見小曰眀: Follows MS B, which writes 眀 with the eye radical; MS A keeps only 小曰, and with 見 lost it cannot be known whether a chapter-division dot stood before it. Wang Bi has 明. (Jicheng IV, p. 21, n. 76)
  • 守柔曰強: Follows MS A; in MS B 柔 is lost, and 曰, bracketed as a restoration in the 1980 edition, is now read directly from the offset on the backing silk. (Jicheng IV, p. 201, n. 66)
  • 用亓光 / 復歸亓明: Both manuscripts agree, MS B writing 眀 for 明. The 1980 edition restored these six graphs in MS B as 其光 and 復歸其明; the Jicheng reads them from the offset on the backing silk. (Jicheng IV, p. 201, nn. 67, 68)
  • 毋道身央: Follows MS A, whose 道 is a miswriting of 遺, printed 道〈遺〉 in the Jicheng. MS B reads 毋遺身央; its 毋, restored as 无 in the 1980 edition, is read as 毋 from the offset, and the graph after it is, in Chen Jian's view, a false start on 央 anticipated from below, left unfinished and circled out. 央 is read as 殃 "calamity"; Wang Bi reads 無遺身殃. (Jicheng IV, p. 201, n. 69)
  • 是胃襲常: Both manuscripts agree; MS B's 襲, bracketed as a restoration in the 1980 edition, is now read directly from the offset. Wang Bi reads 是為習常. (Jicheng IV, p. 201, n. 70)

Commentary

The Dao as a thing gives birth to heaven and to earth; it is what the ten thousand things come out from. "All-under-heaven has a beginning: take it for the mother of all-under-heaven" — 始 is the root in time, 母 the root in kinship, and this makes plain that the Dao is the wellspring of the ten thousand things, which receive it to be born and follow it to their end.

"Having got the mother, know the children by her; then return and keep to the mother — and to the end of the body, no peril." This is the way of knowing the root and keeping the root. Getting the mother, one knows the children; but getting the children, one must not forget the mother. To know the children without keeping the mother is to chase the tips and lose the root; to keep the mother without knowing the children is to stay confined to one side. Only the double movement makes the whole. The Changes says "trace the beginning, return to the end," which sheds light on, and takes light from, the teaching of returning to the root and to what is ordained. Worth fine attention: manuscript A runs 𢟪得亓母，以知亓子，復守亓母, three clauses in file, the sense already complete; manuscript B and the Wang Bi recension have a fourth, 既知亓子 (Wang Bi: 既知其子), saying it over again. The 1980 editors and the Jicheng both take manuscript A to have dropped the clause in copying, and restore it with an omission mark; yet read on its own terms, manuscript A's three clauses follow one another and the sense lacks nothing. For clarity, manuscript B is the clearer; for cleanness, manuscript A is the cleaner — perhaps manuscript A dropped the clause by chance, and its flow stands complete regardless.

"Stop the openings, shut the doors, and to the end of your days you will not be worn." The openings (兌 — manuscript A writes 悶, manuscript B 㙂, both read as 兌) are mouth, ears, eyes; the door is where the heart goes out and in. Stopping and shutting is not cutting off the outer world; it is not being conscripted by it. Laozi does not teach deafness and blindness; he teaches making the heart the master and not the ears and eyes. The silk text's 不堇 is 不勤: a heart not galloping outward, a body not worn down. And the reverse: "open the openings, pursue the affairs, and to the end of your days you will be past saving" (after 終身 manuscript A is lost, and the text follows manuscript B, whose 棘 is read as 救). Fling open the appetites, chase the business, until nothing can save you. Let the student take the warning.

"To see the small is called seeing-clear; to keep to the soft is called strength" (見小曰眀 follows manuscript B; manuscript A keeps only 小曰). Seeing the small is not working down from the large; it is seeing the Dao right in the small. The world slights the minute and worships the conspicuous; the sage sees the Dao's operation in one leaf, one bead of dew. Keeping to the soft is no weakness; it is the body that receives ten thousand changes and does not snap — as water's nature runs downward yet bores through the hard.

"Use the shining, then return home to the clear." 光 is the outward-shining use; 明 is the inward-stored substance. To shine without returning to the clear is mere dispersal; to use the shine and come home to the clear is motion that keeps its stillness — the subtlety of use returning to substance. And the close: "leave no calamity on the body — this is called 襲常" (manuscript B, 毋遺身央，是胃襲常, with 央 read as 殃 and 胃 as 謂; manuscript A miswrites 遺 as 道). Mark the graph: the silk text writes 襲, to inherit, where Wang Bi writes 習, to practice. 襲 is transmission: the Dao handed down generation to generation; 習 is rehearsal — one man's drill at a constant rule. 襲 runs deeper and farther, for the Dao is no one man's and no one age's private thing: it is what awaits a sage a hundred generations hence and is not confounded. Keeping to the mother: that is this chapter's crux. To keep the mother is to know the root; knowing the root, no peril: the way of tending the self and ordering the world is all in this.
