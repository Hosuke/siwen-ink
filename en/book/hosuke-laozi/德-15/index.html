The Book of De · Chapter Fifteen · Keeping to the Mother

English Translation

All-under-heaven has a beginning: take it for the mother of all-under-heaven.

Having got the mother, know the children by her; then return and keep to the mother, and to the end of the body there is no peril.

Stop the openings (兌), shut the doors, and to the end of your days you will not be worn. Open the openings, pursue the affairs, and to the end of your days you will be past saving.

To see the small is called seeing-clear (明); to keep to the soft is called strength.

Use the shining (光), then return home to the clear (明), and leave no calamity on the body: this is called inheriting the constant (襲常).

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

The Dao as a thing gives birth to heaven and to earth; it is what the ten thousand things come out from. "All-under-heaven has a beginning: take it for the mother of all-under-heaven": 始 is the root in time, 母 the root in kinship: the Dao is the wellspring of the ten thousand things, which receive it to be born and follow it to their end.

"Having got the mother, know the children by her; then return and keep to the mother — and to the end of the body, no peril." This is the way of knowing the root and keeping the root. Getting the mother, one knows the children; but getting the children, one must not forget the mother. To know the children without keeping the mother is to chase the tips and lose the root; to keep the mother without knowing the children is to stay confined to one side. Only the double movement makes the whole. The Changes says "trace the beginning, return to the end": one light with the teaching of returning to the root and to what is ordained. Worth fine attention: silk manuscript A runs 既得亓母，以知亓子，復守亓母, three clauses in file, the sense already complete; manuscript B and the Wang Bi recension insert a fourth, 既知其子, saying it over again. Clearer, yes — but not the silk text's cleanness, and the chisel-mark shows.

"Stop the openings, shut the doors, and to the end of your days you will not be worn." The openings (兌) are mouth, ears, eyes; the door is where the heart goes out and in. Stopping and shutting is not cutting off the outer world; it is not being conscripted by it. Laozi does not teach deafness and blindness; he teaches making the heart the master and not the ears and eyes. The silk text's 不堇 is 不勤: a heart not galloping outward, a body not worn down. And the reverse: "open the openings, pursue the affairs, and to the end of your days you will be past saving": 逨 passes for 救. Fling open the appetites, chase the business, until nothing can save you. Let the student take the warning.

"To see the small is called seeing-clear; to keep to the soft is called strength." Seeing the small is not working down from the large; it is seeing the Dao right in the small. The world slights the minute and worships the conspicuous; the sage sees the Dao's operation in one leaf, one bead of dew. Keeping to the soft is no weakness; it is the body that receives ten thousand changes and does not snap — as water's nature runs downward yet bores through the hard.

"Use the shining, then return home to the clear." 光 is the outward-shining use; 明 is the inward-stored substance. To shine without returning to the clear is mere dispersal; to use the shine and come home to the clear is motion that keeps its stillness — the subtlety of use returning to substance. And the close: "leave no calamity on the body — this is called 襲常." Mark the graph: the silk text writes 襲, to inherit, where Wang Bi writes 習, to practice. 襲 is transmission: the Dao handed down generation to generation; 習 is rehearsal — one man's drill at a constant rule. 襲 runs deeper and farther, for the Dao is no one man's and no one age's private thing: it is what awaits a sage a hundred generations hence and is not confounded. Keeping to the mother: that is this chapter's crux. To keep the mother is to know the root; knowing the root, no peril: the way of tending the self and ordering the world is all in this.
