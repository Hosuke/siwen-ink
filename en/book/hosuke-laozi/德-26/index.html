The Book of De · Chapter Twenty-six · No Difficulty

English Translation

Do the not-doing (為无為); attend to the no-affairs; taste the tasteless (味无未, 未 read 味).

Great as small, much as little: answer grievance with De (德).

Plan for the hard at its […]; […] at its small. The hardest things in all-under-heaven begin from what was easy; the greatest things in all-under-heaven begin from what was small.

Hence the sage never works at the great — and so can […].

A promise lightly given […] trust; much taken easy breeds much difficulty.

Hence the sage treats even the easy as hard — and so in the end meets no difficulty.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

This chapter largely follows Manuscript A (MS A); the lines 夫輕若□□信 and 多易必多難, lost in MS A, are supplied from Manuscript B (MS B), and the lines on planning for the hard and working at the great, damaged in both, are pieced together from the two. Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 為无為 / 事无事: Follows MS A, which marks the start of the chapter with a dot; in MS B the two graphs after 事 are lost, and 事 itself, a restoration in the 1980 edition, is read directly by the Jicheng from surviving traces. (Jicheng IV, p. 203, n. 120)
  • 味无未: Follows MS A; lost in MS B. 未 is read as 味 "taste"; Wang Bi has 味無味.
  • 大小多少 / 報怨以德: Follows MS A; lost in MS B. The Peking University manuscript reads 大小（少）之 and lacks everything the received text has from here up to 多易必多難. Guodian also has this chapter, but with many gaps, which some take as deliberate abridgement and others as the original state of the text. (Jicheng IV, p. 32, nn. 129, 130)
  • 圖難乎亓□□ / □□乎亓細也: Damaged in both. MS A preserves 圖難乎亓 (its 亓 read directly by the Jicheng), with the next two graphs lost; MS B loses the first line entirely and preserves 乎亓細也 of the second. Wang Bi reads 圖難於其易，為大於其細; the silk text has 乎 for 於 and ends with 也.
  • 天下之難作於易 / 天下之大作於細: Follows MS A. In MS B the first 於 and the 於細 of the second line are lost; its 難作 and 作, restorations in the 1980 edition, are read directly by the Jicheng from surviving traces. MS B agrees with MS A; the Peking University manuscript and the received texts have 事 after 難 and 大, and Guodian lacks these two lines. Wang Bi reads 天下難事必作於易，天下大事必作於細, adding 事 and 必. (Jicheng IV, p. 32, n. 131; p. 203, n. 121)
  • 是以聖人冬不為大 / 故能□□: Follows MS A; lost in MS B. 冬 is read as 終 "in the end, ever". The two graphs after 故能 are lost; Wang Bi has 故能成其大.
  • 夫輕若□□信: Lost in MS A; follows MS B. 若 is read as 諾 "promise"; the two graphs before 信 are lost, Wang Bi having 夫輕諾必寡信. The 1980 edition restored MS A here as 【夫輕諾者必寡信，多易】必多難; the Jicheng replaces this with a mark of indeterminate lacuna, since the Peking University manuscript places 者 after 多易, and MS A, like Yan Zun's text, may have lacked the opening 夫 and had two instances of 者. (Jicheng IV, p. 32, n. 132)
  • 多易必多難: Follows MS B; MS A preserves only 必多難.
  • 是以𦔻人猷難之: The two manuscripts complement each other: MS A loses 以聖, MS B loses 難. MS B writes 𦔻 for 聖. MS B's 猷 was restored as 猶 in the 1980 edition; the Jicheng, seeing from the traces that the graph was 猷, corrects it. MS A also writes 猷. 猷 is read as 猶 "still, even". (Jicheng IV, p. 203, n. 122)
  • 故終於无難: Follows MS A; MS B preserves only 故, after which the silk is lost, and the Jicheng marks a lacuna of indeterminate length. Wang Bi reads 故終無難矣, with 矣 and without 於.

Commentary

"Do the not-doing; attend to the no-affairs; taste the tasteless" (味无未, 未 read as 味) — six characters, three pairs: the whole of non-doing is in them. Doing-the-not-doing is not doing nothing: it is doing without the heart of doing. Attending to no-affairs is not shunning affairs: it is attending without the heart of having affairs. Tasting the tasteless is not tasting nothing: it is finding the utmost taste in the tasteless. One principle through the three: walk the deeds of doing with the heart of not-doing. This is the true sense of Laozi's non-doing, and it is far from the world's idea that leaving everything undone is non-doing. Follow the self-so of things, and entrust yourself to it: just this.

"Great as small, much as little; answer grievance with De": those four characters are the names of every distinction. The sage dissolves the distinctions, and so sees the great as small, the much as little. Answering grievance with De is no soft indulgence; it is refusing to step into the trap of grievance. Grievance answered with grievance never ends; answered with De, it melts of itself: an outflow of "water is good at benefiting the ten thousand things, and keeps still."

"Plan for the hard where it is still easy; work at the great where it is still small. The hardest things in all-under-heaven begin from what was easy; the greatest things in all-under-heaven begin from what was small" — this is the sage's learning: plan for the hard in easy times, work at the great in small ones. The world waits for difficulty before planning — too late; waits for greatness before working — too slow. Read the same law in reverse: every difficulty now is a heap of former eases; every greatness now is a heap of former smalls. Small heaps into great; easy heaps into hard: the law of time.

"The sage never works at the great, and so can complete his greatness" (manuscript A; 冬 is read as 終, and the two lost graphs 成大 are supplied from the Dao section's 故能成大): not working at the great means not contending with the world for greatness. He who does not seek it follows the Dao, and greatness comes of itself. He who seeks it contrives; contriving, he drives it off. As Laozi says: "the Way of Heaven — not contending, and good at winning." This is the fruit of not contending.

"A promise lightly given is little trusted; much taken easy breeds much difficulty" (manuscript B; 若 is read as 諾, the two graphs above 信 are damaged, and Wang Bi has 夫輕諾必寡信) — the light promiser's word runs short of the deed; he who takes everything easy meets difficulty head-on. Men habitually slight what looks easy. Time turns the slighted into the hard.

"The sage treats even the easy as hard — and so in the end meets no difficulty": he looks at ease as at hardness, and so is careful in everything. The teaching of husbandry answers this: he who can husband holds his spirit still, and only then does the plan come home; the plan come home, and he can steer the ten thousand things, and every road is level.

Collation notes: the silk text writes 天下之難作於易 where Wang Bi has 天下難事必作於易 — the silk text lacks 事 and 必, and the sense is one. At the close, the silk text drops the final particle: 故終於无難, cut off clean, with no tail of breath. The chapter's great point runs through either way: what matters is not at the tips of the words.
