The Book of De · Chapter Twenty-six · No Difficulty

English Translation

Do the not-doing (為无為); attend to the no-affairs; taste the tasteless (味无味).

Great as small, much as little: answer grievance with De (德).

Plan for the hard where it is still easy; work at the great where it is still small. The hardest things in all-under-heaven begin from what was easy; the greatest things in all-under-heaven begin from what was small.

Hence the sage never works at the great — and so can complete his greatness.

A promise lightly given is little trusted; much taken easy breeds much difficulty.

Hence the sage treats even the easy as hard — and so in the end meets no difficulty.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

"Do the not-doing; attend to the no-affairs; taste the tasteless": six characters, three pairs: the whole of non-doing is in them. Doing-the-not-doing is not doing nothing: it is doing without the heart of doing. Attending to no-affairs is not shunning affairs: it is attending without the heart of having affairs. Tasting the tasteless is not tasting nothing: it is finding the utmost taste in the tasteless. One principle through the three: walk the deeds of doing with the heart of not-doing. This is the true sense of Laozi's non-doing, and it is far from the world's idea that leaving everything undone is non-doing. Follow the self-so of things, and entrust yourself to it: just this.

"Great as small, much as little; answer grievance with De": those four characters are the names of every distinction. The sage dissolves the distinctions, and so sees the great as small, the much as little. Answering grievance with De is no soft indulgence; it is refusing to step into the trap of grievance. Grievance answered with grievance never ends; answered with De, it melts of itself: the downstream of "the highest good is like water."

"Plan for the hard where it is still easy; work at the great where it is still small. The hardest things begin from the easy; the greatest things begin from the small": this is the sage's learning: plan for the hard in easy times, work at the great in small ones. The world waits for difficulty before planning: too late; waits for greatness before working: too slow. Read the same law in reverse: every difficulty now is a heap of former eases; every greatness now is a heap of former smalls. Small heaps into great; easy heaps into hard: the law of time.

"The sage never works at the great, and so can complete his greatness": not working at the great means not contending with the world for greatness. He who does not seek it follows the Dao, and greatness comes of itself. He who seeks it contrives; contriving, he drives it off. The Changes: "the way of heaven — not contending, and good at winning."

"A promise lightly given is little trusted; many eases make many difficulties": the light promiser's word runs short of the deed; he who takes everything easy meets difficulty head-on. Men habitually slight what looks easy. Time turns the slighted into the hard.

"The sage treats even the easy as hard: so in the end no difficulty": he looks at ease as at hardness, and so is careful in everything. The teaching of husbandry answers this: he who can husband holds his spirit still, and only then does the plan come home; the plan come home, and he can steer the ten thousand things, and every road is level.

Collation notes: the silk text writes 天下之難作於易 where Wang Bi inserts 事: the graphs differ, the sense is one. At the close, the silk text drops the final particle: 故終於无難, cut off clean, with no tail of breath. The chapter's great point runs through either way: what matters is not at the tips of the words.
