The Book of Dao · Chapter Thirteen · Favor and Disgrace

English Translation

Favor and disgrace both startle (龍辱若驚, 龍 for 寵). Prize the great calamity as your own body (貴大患若身).

What is "favor and disgrace both startle"? Favor is a thing of the low: getting it startles, losing it startles — this is "favor and disgrace both startle."

What is "prize the great calamity as your own body"? The reason I have great calamity is that I have a body. When I have no body — what calamity could there be?

Hence: he who prizes the work of self above the work of all-under-heaven — he can be entrusted with all-under-heaven. He who loves, with the self, all-under-heaven — all-under-heaven can be lodged with him.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

The crux of this chapter is the distinction between the self and all-under-heaven. Who in the world does not want favor and freedom from disgrace? Who does not want to stay far from calamity and keep the body safe? Yet favor, when it comes, startles; disgrace, when it arrives, startles; gain and loss trade places, and the startling never ends. The reason is simple: all of it comes of having a body.

The manuscripts write 龍 (and text B has 弄) for 寵 — the graphs passed for each other of old. 龍, built on the dragon-graph, is the image of change beyond fathoming: favor and disgrace come and go inconstantly, exactly like that — a better graph than 寵. "Getting it startles, losing it startles": between favor and disgrace, men never let go of the reckoning of gain and loss. The world takes favor for glory; Laozi alone sees favor as a thing of the low — to be favored is to lean on one above: the beginning of dependence. Getting startles, losing startles — favor and disgrace weigh the same: Laozi's meaning, turned against the world's.

"The reason I have great calamity is that I have a body; when I have no body, the calamity is gone too." The Huainanzi says of Yao's rule: "Yao held all-under-heaven not out of greed for the wealth of the myriad people, nor for the ease of the ruler's seat — it was because the hundred clans were struggling by force, the strong oppressing the weak, the many preying on the few." From which we know: the sage forgets his body for all-under-heaven exactly because he does not make a private thing of this one body. This is no teaching to destroy the body or cut off life: it makes plain that the burden of trouble is born of body-view; drop the view of having-a-body, and calamity has nothing left to cling to.

The silk text's 有何患, "what calamity could there be," runs smoother than Wang Bi's 吾有何患 — the final 有 answering the earlier 為吾有身也, head and tail linked in one vein. Heshang Gong puts prizing before loving, matching the silk text's order: prizing is the heart's direction, loving is feeling put to work — first prizing, then loving, heart before feeling, a progression. He who loves his own body more than all-under-heaven can be entrusted with all-under-heaven precisely because he does not make all-under-heaven his own resource, and so all-under-heaven trusts him: the law that seeking the common good fulfills the private, and keeping to the private serves the common.

Here Confucian and Daoist meet. The Doctrine of the Mean: "following nature is called the Dao." Husbandry as an art is born of the Dao's pattern — cherishing one's essence and spirit, husbanding one's wits. And "learning not-learning, repairing the crowd's oversights" is likewise the work of returning to the root. To make light of the body against all-under-heaven is to lose the root; the body is the root of all-under-heaven; to forget the body is the utmost for all-under-heaven. The sage prizes his body yet has no body, loves his body yet does not own it — with the heart of no-body-no-calamity he carries the substance of all-under-heaven lodged and entrusted: the hinge of the Dao and De.

Calamity is born of having a body; calamity dies of having no heart. Forget the body, and there is no calamity — and without calamity, all-under-heaven can be lodged with you.
