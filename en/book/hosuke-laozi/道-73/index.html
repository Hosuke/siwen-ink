The Book of Dao · Chapter Twenty-nine · The Self-so

English Translation

Would you take all-under-heaven and do at it (取天下而為之)? I see it: it cannot be forced (亓弗得已).

All-under-heaven is a spirit-vessel (神器): not a thing to be done at.

Who does at it undoes it; who grips it loses it.

Things: some go ahead, some follow; some blaze (炅), some blow; some stand strong, some crumble (䂳); some are built up, some fall.

Hence the sage drops the extreme, drops the inflated, drops the lavish (去甚，去大，去楮).

Commentary

Yaomiao says: the vessel called all-under-heaven is a spirit-vessel — nothing more. A spirit-vessel runs by its own law: not to be held by craft, not to be steered by contrivance. The silk text says 將欲取天下而為之，吾見亓弗得已 — "would you take all-under-heaven and do at it? I see it: it cannot be forced" — steeper than Wang Bi's 吾見其不得已, "I see it cannot be helped." 不得已 is the passive: circumstances do not allow it. 弗得已 is the active verdict: knowing it cannot be done and doing at it anyway — the wording of self-undoing. One graph between them, and the weight hangs far apart; the reader must not miss it. "Who does at it undoes it; who grips it loses it" — these eight words are the guideline of the whole Laozi, and they answer, from the other side, the twenty-seventh chapter's "assisting the self-so of the ten thousand things." All-under-heaven never needed to be governed by human doing; to govern it by doing is to fish in the deep and to pick a tree's growth with your hands.

So the sage, knowing this, drops the extreme, drops the inflated, drops the lavish. The silk text's 楮 borrows for 奢, lavish — one sense with Wang Bi's 去奢, different graph. The extreme is going past; the inflated is puffing up; the lavish is over-ornament. The three droppings drop the private contrivance and give back the root of the self-so. The Book of Changes: "no thought, no doing — still and unmoving; stirred, it goes through all-under-heaven's every turn" — just this. He who governs all-under-heaven cannot but pattern on heaven. The Way of Heaven: not contending, and good at winning; not speaking, and good at answering — its work is simply the self-so. The sage embodies heaven and walks: drop the private self, and all-under-heaven sets itself straight with nothing needing to be done in between — the fine point of the Dao and De, and a mirror for ten thousand ages.
