The Book of Dao · Chapter Twenty-six · The Baggage Wagon

English Translation

The heavy is the light's root; the clear is the restless one's lord (清為趮君).

Hence the noble man, traveling all day, does not part from his baggage wagon (甾重).

Even amid the encircling guard, he rests at ease and shines clear (燕處則昭).

How then could the king of ten thousand chariots conduct himself lightly in all-under-heaven (以身巠於天下)?

Lightness loses the root; restlessness loses the lord.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

Chapter twenty-six of the Book of Dao: silk texts A and B and the Wang Bi recension differ here and there, and the depth of the doctrine turns on the differences. Let me lay out the point for later students.

"The heavy is the light's root; the clear is the restless one's lord" — eight words gathering up the whole chapter. The silk text writes 清, "clear," where Wang Bi has 靜, "still" — both mean settled clarity; no great divide. The root is the root-stock; the lord is the master. Heavy and clear belong to the root; light and restless belong to the tip. The heavy is the root of the light; the settled and clear is the lord of the restless. The two stand by each other and cannot be parted for an instant.

"The noble man, traveling all day, does not part from his baggage wagon" — Wang Bi writes "the sage." Yaomiao ventures that 君子 is the rounder word: the sage is spoken of as ruler of all-under-heaven; the noble man belongs to the student of the Dao — the ranges differ. With 君子 the chapter covers lord and minister, high and low — not for the ruler alone. The baggage wagon is the army's load-carrier: the image of holding oneself heavily. This is the sense of traveling without parting from the wagon.

"He rests at ease — and is luminous": Wang Bi writes 燕處超然, "rests at ease, above it all." The silk text's 昭, with its sun-sign, is the unceasing bright clarity, shining without strain; Wang Bi's "above it all" is the use of standing untangled. One speaks the substance, one the function — they need each other. Resting quiet in his chamber, luminous of himself — nothing sought from outside. This answers "in heart, the deep pool": a heart deep and still as a pool rests at ease and is luminous of itself, with no need of splendid views.

Then the sentence "making his body light against all-under-heaven": silk manuscripts A and B both read 而以身輕於天下 — a warning cast as a rhetorical question. How could the king of ten thousand chariots treat his body as a light thing, moving against all-under-heaven? It locks on to "the heavy is the light's root" above, breath unbroken.

The close: "light, he loses the root; restless, he loses the lordship." The Book of Changes praises Kun ䷁: "the way of Kun is compliant — it receives heaven and moves with the season." Laozi says "the heavy is the light's root": Kun receives heaven by stillness, and man bears all-under-heaven by stillness too — where Confucian and Daoist light each other. The lord of ten thousand chariots holds all-under-heaven as the heaviest of vessels and his own body as the lightest of things; precisely when he cannot keep himself clear and still does lightness lose the root and restlessness lose the lord — and a trifling body throws away a towering realm. Look back over the lords of old: none who failed to honor clear stillness long kept his state. This is heaven and earth's constant way, the shared creed of sages.
