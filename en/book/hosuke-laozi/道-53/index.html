The Book of Dao · Chapter Nine · Holding to Fullness

English Translation

To hold and fill (持盈) — better to stop short of it.

To hammer and sharpen — the edge cannot be kept for long.

Gold and jade fill the room — and no one can keep them.

Honored, wealthy, and proud — you invite your own blame.

The work done, the body withdrawn like a bending shoot (功述身芮): this is the Way of Heaven.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

This chapter of the Book of Dao takes up the harms of holding fullness and hammering edges, and shows the law of not dwelling in completed work — the most daily-use truth in the whole book. Set silk text against Wang Bi: the graphs differ, the senses part, and there is much that must be weighed.

"Holding and filling" is what men most desire — and Laozi warns against it. Not desiring fullness is one of the Dao's great ends. Water brims and spills; the moon fills and wanes; things at their crest must turn — the grain of things, certain. Within, not to seek fullness; without, not to sit in completeness — that has the point. And "better to stop short of it" is not merely stopping: it is stopping before the brim — by not filling full, never spilling. This is the sage's learning of knowing where to stop.

"To hammer and sharpen — the edge cannot be kept for long": Wang Bi writes 梲, to pare down. Paring an edge ever finer brings it to the break. Heshang Gong reads 揣 as "to work": work it sharp, and the sharp is soon blunted — it is not that things cannot last; it is that men do not know where to stop. One root with the Book of De's Chapter Forty-one: "the soft and weak are the companions of life; the hard and strong, the companions of death."

"Gold and jade fill the room": the silk text's 盈室 and Wang Bi's 滿堂 are near in sense, and 莫之守 against 莫能守 differ in words, not in point. Hard-to-get goods make men stumble in their conduct; the harder you guard them, the faster they go — not for want of guarding skill: fullness itself is the calamity.

Then 功述身芮 — the eye of the whole chapter, and the finest weighing of graphs. In the silk text, 述 passes for 遂, "accomplished," and 芮 for 退, "withdraw" — but the flavors differ in depth. 退 is plain and obvious. 芮, per the Shuowen, is young grass small and new — and it also carries the sense of gathering inward and drawing closed. The work done, one gathers the body back and withdraws, storing one's De to keep whole — not merely stepping back, but folding the work itself away. The Kun hexagram ䷁ says, "tie the sack: no blame" — Laozi's "the work done, the body withdrawn" is the same Dao in different words. The Way of Heaven completes the ten thousand things without a word and never dwells in the work; the sage patterns on it, completes his work and gathers it away — so the work cannot turn and bite. "Work and do not lean on it; complete the work and do not dwell in it" — all of it is not desiring fullness.

Today's world chases profit hotter than ever — seeking fullness, seeking edges, seeking wealth, seeking honor, not knowing where to stop and greedy for more: exactly what Laozi calls "adding to life is an ill omen." The Way of Heaven takes from the surplus to give to the wanting; the way of man takes from the wanting to offer to the surplus. The sage knows that fullness must spill and edges must break, and so he keeps the empty hollow always, does not dwell in completed work, gathers and stores to keep his person whole — not from timidity, but in accord with the Way of Heaven.
