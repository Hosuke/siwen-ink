The Book of Dao · Chapter Nine · Holding to Fullness

English Translation

To grasp it and fill it (㨁, read as 持) — better to stop short of it.

To hammer and sharpen — the edge cannot be kept for long.

Gold and jade fill the room — and no one can keep them.

Honored, wealthy, and proud — you invite your own blame.

The work done, the body withdrawn like a bending shoot (功述身芮): this is the Way of Heaven.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

The first line and the run from 金玉盈室 to 功述身芮 follow Manuscript A (MS A); MS A is damaged from 不若亓巳 to 不可長葆也, and these lines, with the last line, are supplied from Manuscript B (MS B). Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 㨁而盈之: Follows MS A; MS B agrees. The graph is 㨁 (扌 beside 直), which the Jicheng reads as 持 "to hold". Older transcriptions sometimes give 植, but the hand radical 扌 is clear on the plates; it is not 木. Wang Bi has 持而盈之. (MS A, col. 106; Jicheng IV, pp. 40, 205)
  • 不若亓巳: MS A preserves only 不若, the two graphs for "its stopping" being lost; the line follows MS B, whose 亓 and 巳 the Jicheng reads as 其 and 已. Wang Bi has 不如其已.
  • 㨕而允之: This line is broken in MS A, which the Jicheng prints as 【揣而】□之□之; the text follows MS B. MS B's 㨕 (扌 beside 盈) is read as 揣 "to hammer, to temper", and 允 as 銳 "sharp". The graph 允 has a small 厶 above a long hook; Gao Ming's transcription gives 兑, but the 1980 edition and the plates show 允. Wang Bi has 揣而梲之.
  • MS A's □之□之: The 1980 editors took the damaged graph above the first 之 to have 㕣 on the right, possibly 鉛, and regarded the extra □之 as a scribal slip. The Jicheng finds the 㕣 reading unconvincing, judges the second damaged graph illegible, and, the situation being unclear, does not mark the words as an interpolation; Gao Ming's view that □之 is the remnant of 兌 it rejects as still less credible. (Jicheng IV, p. 46, n. 33)
  • 不可長葆也: Follows MS B. MS A reads □可長葆之, ending in 之. The 1980 editors thought the graph above 可 resembled 惡; the Jicheng identifies it as a botched 可, struck out in red, above which traces of a rewritten 不 can just be made out. Wang Bi has 不可長保. (Jicheng IV, p. 46, n. 34)
  • 金玉盈室: Follows MS A; MS B agrees, and its 盈, given as a lacuna in Gao Ming's transcription, is read directly by the Jicheng. Wang Bi has 金玉滿堂.
  • 莫之守也: Follows MS A; MS B reads 莫之能守也, with 能 "be able" as in Wang Bi.
  • 貴富而䮦: Follows MS A. For 䮦 the Jicheng prints 馬 beside 高, read as 驕 "proud"; MS B writes 驕. Both manuscripts have the order 貴富 "honored and wealthy"; Wang Bi reverses it to 富貴.
  • 自遺咎也: The manuscripts agree; Wang Bi has 自遺其咎.
  • 功述身芮: Follows MS A; the Jicheng reads 述 as 遂 "accomplished" and 芮 as 退 "withdraw". MS B reads 功遂身退; its 退 is printed in the Jicheng as 𢓴, an archaic form of 退, which the data records as 退. Wang Bi agrees with MS B. (MS A, col. 107; Jicheng IV, p. 46, n. 28)
  • 天之道也: In MS A 道也 is lost and is supplied from MS B. Wang Bi has 天之道, without 也.

Commentary

This chapter of the Book of Dao takes up the harms of holding fullness and hammering edges, and shows the law of not dwelling in completed work — the most daily-use truth in the whole book. Set silk text against Wang Bi: the graphs differ, the senses part, and there is much that must be weighed.

"To grasp it and fill it" (㨁, read as 持) is what men most desire — and Laozi warns against it. Not desiring fullness is one of the Dao's great ends. Water brims and spills; the moon fills and wanes; things at their crest must turn — the grain of things, certain. Within, not to seek fullness; without, not to sit in completeness — that has the point. And "better to stop short of it" is not merely stopping: it is stopping before the brim — by not filling full, never spilling. This is the sage's learning of knowing where to stop.

"To hammer and sharpen — the edge cannot be kept for long" (manuscript B, 㨕而允之，不可長葆也, with 㨕 read as 揣 and 允 as 銳; manuscript A is damaged here and ends 長葆之) — Wang Bi writes 揣而梲之, and 梲 is to pare down. Paring an edge ever finer brings it to the break. Heshang Gong reads 揣 as "to work": work it sharp, and the sharp is soon blunted — it is not that things cannot last; it is that men do not know where to stop. One root with the Book of De's Chapter Forty-one: "the soft and weak are the companions of life; the hard and strong, the companions of death."

"Gold and jade fill the room": the silk text's 盈室 and Wang Bi's 滿堂 are near in sense, and 莫之守 against 莫能守 differ in words, not in point. Hard-to-get goods make men stumble in their conduct; the harder you guard them, the faster they go — not for want of guarding skill: fullness itself is the calamity.

Then 功述身芮 — the eye of the whole chapter, and the finest weighing of graphs. In the silk text, 述 passes for 遂, "accomplished," and 芮 for 退, "withdraw" — but the flavors differ in depth. 退 is plain and obvious. 芮, per the Shuowen, is young grass small and new — and it also carries the sense of gathering inward and drawing closed. The work done, one gathers the body back and withdraws, storing one's De to keep whole — not merely stepping back, but folding the work itself away. The Kun hexagram ䷁ says, "tie the sack: no blame" — Laozi's "the work done, the body withdrawn" is the same Dao in different words. The Way of Heaven completes the ten thousand things without a word and never dwells in the work; the sage patterns on it, completes his work and gathers it away — so the work cannot turn and bite. The Book of De says, "he works and does not possess; completes the work and does not dwell in it" (Chapter Forty-two, manuscript B: 為而弗又，成功而弗居也, 又 read as 有) — all of it is not desiring fullness.

Today's world chases profit hotter than ever — seeking fullness, seeking edges, seeking wealth, seeking honor, not knowing where to stop and greedy for more: exactly what Laozi calls "adding to life is an ill omen." The Way of Heaven takes from the surplus to give to the wanting; the way of man takes from the wanting to offer to the surplus. The sage knows that fullness must spill and edges must break, and so he keeps the empty hollow always, does not dwell in completed work, gathers and stores to keep his person whole — not from timidity, but in accord with the Way of Heaven.
