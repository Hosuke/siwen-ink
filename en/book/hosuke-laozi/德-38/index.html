The Book of De · Chapter Thirty-eight · Heaven's Net

English Translation

He who is brave in daring gets killed; he who is brave in not-daring stays whole (栝). Of these two, one profits, one harms.

What Heaven hates — who knows the reason?

The Way of Heaven: it strikes not (不彈), yet wins well; speaks not, yet answers well; uncalled, comes of itself; at ease (彈 — read here as 繟然), yet plans well.

Heaven's net is vast (恢恢); its meshes are wide — and nothing slips through.

Commentary

Yaomiao says: courage, for men, divides by its use. He who is brave in daring is resolute in contrivance, a strong-arm walking into peril — that is what gets him killed. He who is brave in not-daring has courage and reins it in, reading the hour and keeping his lot — that is what keeps him alive. It is not that Laozi holds courage cheap; he holds dear using it aright. As the saying runs, "without speaking, good at answering": heaven does not speak, and the four seasons proceed; a man who does not speak may yet answer all-under-heaven. The way of using courage is the same.

"Of these two, one profits, one harms" — the same courage, profit or harm by how it is used. No fixed rule: it shifts with time and circumstance. This is Laozi's dialectic.

"What Heaven hates — who knows the reason?" Heaven's likings cannot be sounded by human wit. Even the sage keeps his awe and does not presume to guess Heaven's heart. Hence the four images of the Way of Heaven that follow. Without striking, good at winning: the silk text's 彈 means to strike — the Way of Heaven does not contend, and things submit of themselves; it answers Wang Bi's 不爭 in sense, on an older graph. Without speaking, good at answering: no injunction given, and the answer arrives of itself. Unsummoned, it comes of itself: the Way of Heaven transforms by drawing, with no need of calling — things come submitting of themselves. And "unhurried, good at planning": Heaven dwells at ease and plans things well. Wang Bi writes 繟然而善謀 — 繟 is slack and slow — close in sense.

"Heaven's net is vast; its meshes wide, and nothing slips through": 恢恢 is Heaven's measure — its span is the widest; 疏 is the wideness of its meshes — and yet the requital of good and evil follows like a shadow, and no one walks free by luck. The world is dazzled by what daring can do and blind to what not-daring profits, not knowing that the Way of Heaven lies not in fineness but in certainty: it does not set men free for want of watching, and none escapes for want of form. As the saying runs, "only by not contending is one without fault" — he who knows this can keep his life whole, can pattern on Heaven. The differences between the two texts come to two points: Wang Bi adds the line "hence even the sage treats it as hard," showing the sage's heart keeps its awe always; and 不彈 against 不爭, 彈而 against 繟然而 — the graphs differ in age, the senses run together. Set side by side, the silk text is spare of words and whole in meaning — not something later hands could have padded.
