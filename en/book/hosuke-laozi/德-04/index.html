The Book of De · Chapter Four · Turning Back and Returning

English Translation

Turning back (反): this is the movement of the Dao (道). Weakness: this is the use of the Dao.

The things of all-under-heaven are born of presence (有 yǒu); presence is born of absence (无 wú).

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

Nowhere in Laozi's five thousand words is there a chapter as spare as this. Twenty-five characters, and the Dao's substance and function, presence and absence, movement and stillness, strength and weakness, are exhausted in them.

"Turning back is the movement of the Dao": the single graph 反 bears the most weight. 反 is not opposition; it is return. The infant at birth turns back toward the parents' qi; the ten thousand things in their aging turn back into the soil; sun and moon on their courses turn back to the degree where they began. Such is the way of heaven and earth: stripping reaches its limit, and return must come. The Book of Changes says of the hexagram Fu ䷗, "Return: prevailing": the image of yang's first sprouting, the right way coming back. Laozi watched this and knew that in all-under-heaven nothing goes out that does not come back: the strong are weakened, the weak strengthened; the full are emptied, the emptied filled. Those on high think none can match them, not knowing the toppled will be replaced; those below think themselves unable to prevail, not knowing the weak defeats the strong. So "turning back is the Dao's movement" means: the Dao adds nothing to things from outside; things return to it of themselves. Read this alongside Mencius's "the ten thousand things are complete in me" and the Changes' "on the seventh day, return": Confucian and Daoist seal together here, and not by accident.

"Weakness" is not cowardice; it is the Dao's use, and such use cannot be forced. The soft and weak defeat the hard and strong: water benefits the ten thousand things without contending, not because it could contend and refrains, but because it does not know there is such a thing as contending. This is the point of "the highest good: governing as water." The sage knows hardness cannot be gripped, so he keeps the De of not contending, and no one in all-under-heaven can contend with him. It is not that weakness is resolute in doing nothing; it is non-doing that leaves nothing undone.

"The things of all-under-heaven are born of presence; presence is born of absence": these two lines are the spine of Laozi's ontology. Presence is the mother of the ten thousand things; absence is what presence itself is born from. The common take presence for the real and absence for the void. Laozi turns it over: absence is the substance and the real; presence is the trace and the use. So "illuminating the bright De" is the Confucian entering from the side of presence; "those who work at the Dao diminish daily" is the Daoist returning from the side of absence: two gates, one arrival.

Collate the silk text with Wang Bi, and there are three differences of voice and sense. The silk text writes 反也者，道之動也, with two extra particles: the cadence unhurried, an elder talking at his ease. Wang Bi trims them, and the voice turns clipped. That is one. The silk text has 天下之物, "the things of all-under-heaven"; Wang Bi has 天下萬物, "the ten thousand things of all-under-heaven." 物 is the class-name, wide in its embrace; 萬物 is the exhaustive name, leaving nothing out. The silk text wants inclusion; Wang Bi wants explicitness. That is two.

As for 无 against 無, the difference runs to the very bottom. 无 is the old graph, and it keeps a kinship with presence: it is what 有 issues from. 無 is the later graph, drifted toward sheer negation, close to an absolute not-being. The silk text's 无 still breathes with 有, each generating the other; Wang Bi's 無 stands cut off from 有, confronting it. One graph moved, and the depth of Laozi's ontology is decided; so read Laozi in the silk text's 无, and you touch his root sense. This is a distinction earlier commentators never weighed finely. Heshang Gong's text also writes 無: by the Six Dynasties the border of 无 and 無 had already blurred, and the dimming of Laozi's original sense begins there.

Most who read this chapter mistake "turning back is the Dao's movement" for a tactic of living: the weak defeats the strong, therefore feign weakness to seize strength. This is the grossest of errors. Laozi is showing that the Dao moves thus; he is not teaching stratagem. He shows that it is so; he does not teach the counterfeiting of its being so. Turning back is not opposition but homecoming; weakness is not cowardice but use. He who grips strength defeats himself; he who keeps to weakness stands — not because weakness wins, but because the weak walk always with the Dao. Only one who can hold this distinction is truly reading Laozi.
