The Book of De · Chapter Thirty-one · No Hoarding

English Translation

True words are not fine; fine words are not true. He who knows is not broad; the broad one does not know. The good are not excessive (多); the excessive are not good.

The sage hoards nothing. The more he works for others, the more he has; the more he gives to others, the more abundant he becomes.

Thus the Way of Heaven: benefits and does not harm. The way of man: acts and does not contend (為而弗爭).

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

This chapter stands thirty-first in the silk text's Book of De: and last, the eighty-first, in the Wang Bi recension: the closing of the whole five thousand words. The silk collation text and Wang Bi differ in small ways a student must watch.

"True words are not fine; fine words are not true": words that are real need no ornament; words that are ornamented need not be real. Not that fine words are all false — but he who seeks the fine in words mostly loses the true. The first office of words is truth; fineness is extra.

"He who knows is not broad; the broad one does not know": knowing here is not broad learning. The knower holds the one thread that runs through; the broad are scattered across the swarm of tasks. Confucius said, "one thread stringing it all": Confucian and Daoist agree here. The one-threaded steers the many by the simple; the scattered can only heap, and cannot go through.

"The good are not much; the much are not good": Wang Bi writes 善者不辯，辯者不善, "the good do not argue; the argumentative are not good." The silk text's 多, "much," is the wider word, covering much of speech, much of doing, much of taking, much of hoarding. The good are simply not-much: the truly good gather themselves in, lessen themselves, quiet themselves. Wang Bi's 辯 is only one corner of speech: the sense narrows. Here the silk text wins.

"The sage hoards nothing" — Wang Bi writes 不積, and the silk text 无積. The two graphs pass for each other, but press them and they part: 无積 is a thorough no-store, nothing laid up at all; 不積 is a not-hoarding that could still hoard unawares. One graph's difference: the whole Daoist distinction of presence and absence — and the sage's unhoarded completeness follows the good's not-much in one file.

"The more he works for others, the more he has; the more he gives, the more abundant he becomes": that giving returns as having is no arithmetic of profit; it is the Dao's turning-back. He who gives empties his store; the store emptied, the Dao flows; the Dao flowing is not penned in one body: hence the more given, the more had, the more abundant. Not a counted more: a more of the country of De.

Wang Bi writes 聖人之道, "the way of the sage"; the silk text has 人之道, "the way of man." The silk text carries more: the way of man is rooted in the Way of Heaven, and the sage is simply the man who walks the human way to its end. Say "the way of the sage," and man's way and heaven's way split into two; say "the way of man," and every man can reach for it: Heshang Gong's "the sage puts himself behind." Laozi entrusts the Dao to the people, not to sages alone.

The silk text's 故, "thus," opens the close, which Wang Bi lacks: with 故, above and below run through: the Way of Heaven and the way of man come down in one breath. The way of heaven bestows and transforms, never wounding the root: just so.

The chapter's crux is not contending. The De of not contending is no shrinking timidity: it is standing above without strutting, standing in front without racing; the ten thousand things come home and meet no refusal. Working for others and having more, giving to others and growing more abundant: all of it rests on no-hoarding and no-contending. The world guards its private store, and the more it stores the narrower it grows; the sage's breast is empty and swept clean, and the more he gives the fuller he becomes. Is this not why Laozi prizes the soft and keeps to the female, returning to the root and to what is ordained?
