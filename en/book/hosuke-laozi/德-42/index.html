The Book of De · Chapter Forty-two · The Way of Heaven

English Translation

The way of all-under-heaven is like the drawing of a bow: what is high it presses down; what is low it raises up; what has surplus it takes from; what falls short it makes good (補).

Thus the Way of Heaven takes from the surplus to give (益) to the wanting; the way of man takes from the wanting to offer to the surplus.

Who can have surplus, and take it to offer up to Heaven? Only one who has the Dao.

Hence the sage works and does not possess; completes the work and does not dwell in it — he has no wish to be seen as worthy.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

The way of all-under-heaven is like the drawing of a bow. The bow's way is to set crooked and straight aright; the Dao's way in the world is to even high and low. Watch the bow drawn: the limb an inch too high is pressed down; the limb an inch too low is raised; the surplus is taken from; the wanting is made good — the image of the Way of Heaven. The Changes says of the hexagram Qian ䷎ (Modesty), "the noble man takes from the much to give to the little," and of Yi ䷩ (Increase), wind and thunder aiding each other. The sage watches the images and takes his law from them — the crux of ordering the world lies here.

The Way of Heaven takes from the surplus to give to the wanting: this is the sense of evening out, not merely of patching. The Shuowen: "益 is to brim over"; "補 is to make a garment whole." 益 adds until there is bounty and breadth; 補 fills the gap to just-enough. Heaven's heart is broad like the brimming gift, so the ten thousand things each rest in their allotments and nothing withers at the edge. The way of man is otherwise: taking from the wanting to offer to the surplus — tilting toward the lopsided, ever farther from Heaven.

Collating the silk text against Wang Bi, three differences. First, silk manuscript A has 天下之道, "the way of all-under-heaven," where manuscript B, like Wang Bi, has 天之道: embracing heaven, earth, and man — the wider sense. Second, the silk text has 益不足, "giving to the wanting," for Wang Bi's 補不足, "patching the wanting" — 益 and 補 differ in depth, as the niche above shows. Third, the silk text has 取奉於天者, "taking to offer up to Heaven," for Wang Bi's 奉天下, "offering to all-under-heaven": the sage gathers in his surplus and returns it to Heaven, never daring to hold it privately.

The sage works and does not possess; completes the work and does not dwell in it; has no wish to be seen as worthy — three clauses, one thread, all of the highest De's non-doing. Gain and loss do not move the heart: the work done, the body withdraws, and the De does not cease; self-boasting and self-flaunting have no place in the man of the Dao. The bow hits because it does not lean on its full draw; the Dao lasts because it does not dwell in its merit. The sage knows "presence gives the advantage, absence gives the use" — so he steps back and the work endures, sets himself aside and his De spreads wide. The world knows only that taking is profit; it does not know that letting go is use — far from the sage indeed.
