The Book of Dao · Chapter Twenty-three · Holding the One

English Translation

Bent, then whole (曲則金, 金 an error for 全). Crooked, then straight. Hollow, then full. Worn, then new. Little, then gain. Much, then bewilderment.

Hence the sage holds the One (執一), to shepherd all-under-heaven (為天下牧).

Not self-seeing (不自視), hence shining-forth (章); not self-displaying, hence clear (明); not self-boasting, hence merit; not self-exalting, hence enduring.

Only because he does not contend — no one can contend with him.

What the ancients meant by "the bent are made whole" — were those mere empty words (幾語才)? Wholeness truly comes home to him (誠金歸之, 金 again for 全).

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

The text follows Manuscript A (MS A); the lines 不自視故章不自見也故明 and 古之所胃曲全者幾語才, damaged in MS A, are supplied from Manuscript B (MS B). Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 曲則金: Follows MS A. 金 is a scribal error for 全 "whole", which the Jicheng corrects to 〈全〉; MS B reads 曲則全, as does Wang Bi.
  • 枉則定: Follows MS A, with 定 read as 正 "straight"; MS B reads 汪則正, with 汪 read as 枉 "crooked". Wang Bi has 枉則直.
  • 洼則盈 / 敝則新: Follows MS A, with 洼 read as 窪 "hollow". MS B writes 敝 as 𧝬 (敝 over 衣), as the plates, the 1980 edition and Gao Ming's transcription all show.
  • 是以聲人執一以為天下牧: Follows MS A, with 聲 read as 聖 "sage"; MS B writes 聖 as 𦔻 (耳 beside 口). Wang Bi has 是以聖人抱一為天下式, with 抱 "embrace" for 執 "hold" and 式 "pattern" for 牧 "shepherd".
  • 不自視故章不自見也故明: Follows MS B. MS A reads 不【自】視故明，不自見故章; MS B reads 不自視故章，不自見也故明. The two manuscripts thus exchange 明 and 章, and MS B has 也 in the second line. Since 自 is lost in MS A, both clauses follow MS B, so that 明 and 章 are not mixed. The Jicheng reads 視 as 示 "to display" and 章 as 彰 "to be manifest". Wang Bi has 不自見故明，不自是故彰.
  • 弗矜故能長: The manuscripts agree; Wang Bi has 不自矜故長.
  • 故莫能與之爭: The manuscripts agree; Wang Bi has 故天下莫能與之爭.
  • 古之所胃曲全者幾語才: Lost after 古 in MS A; the line follows MS B. MS A's 語 was read directly in the 1980 edition, but the Jicheng finds that the fragment bearing its left side is not the 言 radical, removes it, and reads 唔（語）; the graph above it keeps some traces, and the Jicheng withdraws the 1980 restoration 幾 (taken from MS B) in favor of ☐. MS B's 才 is read as 哉. Wang Bi has 古之所謂曲則全者，豈虛言哉. (Jicheng IV, p. 51, n. 90)
  • 誠金歸之: Follows MS A, where 金 is again an error for 全; MS B reads 誠全歸之. Wang Bi has 誠全而歸之.

Commentary

The point of this chapter is the way of achieving by reversal — and it comes home to "holding the One."

Things are born taking their shape from the Dao, leaning on the Dao for their charge. Bent, then whole; crooked, then straight; hollow, then full; worn, then new; little, then gain; much, then bewilderment — six sayings, one law: the full must spill; the brimming must tip; contraries complete each other — the constant rule of the Dao. Bend yourself to follow things, and wholeness is gained — that is the point. Bending is not scheming: it is bowing the self to make things whole. Crooked is not crushed: it curves and comes back straight. The hollowed receives; the worn gives birth; the little keeps; the much bewilders. The sage, watching this, knows pride and boasting will not do, and contention profits nothing.

Then the four "nots": not self-seeing, hence shining-forth; not self-displaying, hence clear; not self-boasting, hence merit; not self-exalting, hence enduring (so manuscript B; manuscript A reads 不自視故明，不自見故章, with 明 and 章 exchanged). The silk text's 不自視 against Wang Bi's 不自是 — the graphs differ. The silk text's "not self-seeing" is not being dazzled by one's own view (the Jicheng reads 視 as 示, "not putting oneself on show," a near sense); Wang Bi's "not self-asserting" is not gripping one's own rightness. The two texts face each other, and the senses complete one another — all saying the sage wings the ten thousand things along without claiming the work. "Not self-boasting, hence merit": boast, and the merit breaks; boast not, and the merit is whole — just this. Not self-exalting is not prizing one's own ability — only so, enduring: one light with "water benefits the ten thousand things and keeps still."

Only because he does not contend — no one can contend with him (Wang Bi: "no one in all-under-heaven can contend with him"). This De of not contending is no timidity: it is standing on ground where contention cannot reach. To contend there must be an opposite party; he who does not contend has no one in all-under-heaven to be his enemy — the highest wit of the Daoists.

The close: "what the ancients meant by 'the bent are made whole' — were those mere empty words? Wholeness truly comes home to him." The silk text's 幾語才 says: is this an empty saying? 誠全歸之 says: he who bends himself to follow things finds wholeness come home in the end. The Doctrine of the Mean: "sincerity is the way of heaven" — and Laozi's 誠全歸之 speaks the reality of the bent-made-whole. Confucian and Daoist were never two roads: different ways up, one arrival.

Wang Bi's 抱一, "cradle the One," against the silk text's 執一, "hold the One" — holding is keeping fast, cradling is nourishing, different words, one point: keeping the Dao in purity, holding De entire, and by it serving the Way of Heaven and steering the governance of men. The shepherd (牧) rears and tends — he does not cow with majesty: that is the sage as shepherd of all-under-heaven.
