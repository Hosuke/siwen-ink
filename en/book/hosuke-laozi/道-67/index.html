The Book of Dao · Chapter Twenty-three · Holding the One

English Translation

Bent, then whole (曲則全). Crooked, then straight. Hollow, then full. Worn, then new. Little, then gain. Much, then bewilderment.

Hence the sage holds the One (執一), to shepherd all-under-heaven (為天下牧).

Not self-seeing, hence clear; not self-displaying, hence shining-forth; not self-boasting, hence merit; not self-exalting, hence enduring.

Only because he does not contend — no one can contend with him.

What the ancients meant by "the bent are made whole" — were those mere empty words (幾語才)? Wholeness truly comes home to him (誠全歸之).

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

The point of this chapter is the way of achieving by reversal — and it comes home to "holding the One."

Things are born taking their shape from the Dao, leaning on the Dao for their charge. Bent, then whole; crooked, then straight; hollow, then full; worn, then new; little, then gain; much, then bewilderment — six sayings, one law: the full must spill; the brimming must tip; contraries complete each other — the constant rule of the Dao. Bend yourself to follow things, and wholeness is gained — that is the point. Bending is not scheming: it is bowing the self to make things whole. Crooked is not crushed: it curves and comes back straight. The hollowed receives; the worn gives birth; the little keeps; the much bewilders. The sage, watching this, knows pride and boasting will not do, and contention profits nothing.

Then the four "nots": not self-seeing, and so clear; not self-displaying, and so shown; not self-boasting, and so meritorious; not self-exalting, and so lasting. The silk text's 不自視故明 against Wang Bi's 不自是故彰 — the graphs differ. The silk text's "not self-seeing" is not being dazzled by one's own view; Wang Bi's "not self-asserting" is not gripping one's own rightness. The two texts face each other, and the senses complete one another — all saying the sage wings the ten thousand things along without claiming the work. "Not self-boasting, and so meritorious": boast, and the merit breaks; boast not, and the merit is whole — just this. Not self-exalting is not prizing one's own ability — only so, lasting: one light with "water benefits the ten thousand things and keeps still."

Only because he does not contend, no one in all-under-heaven can contend with him. This De of not contending is no timidity: it is standing on ground where contention cannot reach. To contend there must be an opposite party; he who does not contend has no one in all-under-heaven to be his enemy — the highest wit of the Daoists.

The close: "what the ancients meant by 'the bent are made whole' — were those idle words? Wholeness truly comes home to him." The silk text's 幾語才 says: is this an empty saying? 誠全歸之 says: he who bends himself to follow things finds wholeness come home in the end. The Doctrine of the Mean: "sincerity is the way of heaven" — and Laozi's 誠全歸之 speaks the reality of the bent-made-whole. Confucian and Daoist were never two roads: different ways up, one arrival.

Wang Bi's 抱一, "cradle the One," against the silk text's 執一, "hold the One": holding is keeping fast, cradling is nourishing — different words, one point: keeping the Dao in purity, holding De entire, and by it serving the Way of Heaven and steering the governance of men. The shepherd （牧） rears and tends — he does not cow with majesty: that is the sage as shepherd of all-under-heaven.
