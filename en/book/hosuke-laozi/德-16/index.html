The Book of De · Chapter Sixteen · The Thieving Piper (盜竽)

English Translation

If I held fast (𢴲, read as 挈) to a measure of knowing and walked the great Dao (大道), I would fear only the setting-up of schemes (佗, read as 施).

The great Dao is utterly level; yet the people dearly love to pick it apart (𠎿; manuscript A writes 解).

The court swept spotless; the fields gone to weed; the granaries standing empty. Robes of patterned color, sharp swords at the belt, glutted with food, carrying wealth to spare: this is called the thieving piper (盜竽). It is not the Dao.

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

This chapter follows Manuscript A (MS A) for its first line and for 朝甚除 through 服文采; 行於大道 through 民甚好𠎿, 帶利劍 and what follows are supplied from Manuscript B (MS B), and the last two lines are damaged in both. Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 使我𢴲有知也: Follows MS A. 𢴲 (手 beside 絜) is read as 挈 "to hold fast"; the final 也 is read by the Jicheng. MS B reads 使我介有知, without 也. Wang Bi reads 使我介然有知.
  • 行於大道: In MS A 行於 is lost and is supplied from MS B.
  • 唯佗是畏: Follows MS B; 佗 is read as 施. In MS A everything after 唯 is lost; the original editors restored 施, and the Jicheng follows them. The Peking University manuscript has 蛇. Wang Bi reads 唯施是畏. (Jicheng IV, p. 22, n. 84)
  • 大道甚夷民甚好𠎿: Follows MS B. In MS A 大道 is lost, check marks follow 夷 and 解, and MS A writes 解 where MS B has 𠎿 (人 beside 解). The Peking University manuscript reads 而民好街, most received texts 而民好徑; Wang Bi reads 大道甚夷，而民好徑, without 甚 and with 而. The original editors read 解 as 嶰 "mountain gully", or alternatively as 徑 "bypath"; the Jicheng argues that 解, 𠎿 and 街 should all be read as 奊 "crooked", a slanting byroad, close in sense to 施. (Jicheng IV, p. 23, n. 85)
  • 朝甚除 / 田甚芜: Follows MS A, with a check mark after each line; MS B agrees. Wang Bi writes 蕪 for 芜.
  • 服文采: Both manuscripts agree; Wang Bi reads 服文綵.
  • 帶利劍: In MS A 劍 is lost and is supplied from MS B.
  • 猒食而齎財有□: Follows MS B. MS A reads 猒食，貨□□□, without 而齎. MS A's 猒 was bracketed as a restoration in the 1980 edition; the Jicheng reads it directly from the lower end of 犬 still visible on the right, and the lacuna after 貨 was restored by the original editors from Fu Yi and other texts as 財有餘. In MS B, 齎 is read as 資; 有 was restored by the 1980 edition at the head of the lower half of the line, but the Jicheng reads it at the foot of the upper half from a fragment stuck to the backing silk; 餘 is lost. Wang Bi reads 厭飲食，財貨有餘. (Jicheng IV, p. 23, n. 86; p. 201, n. 71)
  • □□盜: Damaged in both. MS A is wholly lost and MS B keeps only 盜. Of the next graph MS B keeps the left-hand 木 (the original note wrongly took it for the right-hand side); the Jicheng prints a graph with 木 on the left and reads it as 竽 "reed pipe", the reading quoted in the "Jie Lao" chapter of the Han Feizi. Wang Bi reads 是為盜夸. (Jicheng IV, p. 23, n. 87; p. 201, n. 72)
  • □非□也: Damaged in both; MS B keeps 非 and 也. The 1980 edition restored MS A as 是謂盜夸。盜夸，非道也, with a repetition; following Gao Ming, the Jicheng takes neither manuscript to have the repetition. Wang Bi reads 非道也哉; the silk has no 哉. (Jicheng IV, p. 23, n. 87)

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

The point of this chapter: the failing of the man who would walk the Dao, and the wrong of those above — the two entangled, closing on a warning against the theft of the Dao itself.

First, 使我挈有知也: silk manuscript A writes 𢴲, read as 挈, "to hold fast in the hand" (manuscript B has 介), where Wang Bi has 介然, "for a moment." Yaomiao ventures that 挈 is the stronger. He who walks the great Dao does not wake to it once by accident; he holds and hoists this knowing, ever alert, as one carries a thing by its handle, never daring to slacken — the sage's real work. A knowing that flickers on "for a moment" flickers off as fast; its work is shallow.

"Walking the great way, my one fear is the setting-up" (manuscript B writes 佗, read as 施): 施 is contrivance, institution. The knowing man walks the way and fears the set-up scheme above all, dreading that one wrong institution breeds harm without end. Heshang Gong's comment on this chapter dwells on the danger of reckless 施: the same point.

"The great Dao is utterly level; yet the people dearly love to pick it apart." This line rewards the longest chewing. The great Dao is level and simple in the extreme, easy to walk. But the people do not love the Dao: they take its very plainness for a difficulty, and bore into it, splitting and explaining; this is 好解, the love of picking-apart. Manuscript A writes 解, manuscript B 𠎿 (解 with 人 beside it), the Peking University slips 街, and the Wang Bi recension 徑: "the people love bypaths." The Jicheng reads 解, 𠎿 and 街 all as 奊, a crooked path, which brings them near to 徑. Yaomiao ventures that the silk graph may also be taken as it stands, and that 好解 runs deeper than 好徑: a bypath is still a road, only hastier; but picking-apart takes clever wit as its measuring-line, breaks the whole of the great Dao into rubble, and turns the utterly simple into the endlessly involved — not merely failing to follow the Dao, but presuming to lay human cleverness over it. As Laozi said earlier: "Foreknowing is the flower of the Dao and the beginning of folly": the foreknower's disease is exactly this boring and guessing, taking the Dao's flower and leaving its substance behind. The people who love picking-apart are sick with the same sickness.

"The court swept spotless, the fields gone to weed, the granaries standing empty; robes of patterned color, sharp swords at the belt, glutted with food, wealth to spare": the wrong of those above. Polished above and stripped below; ornamented without and greedy within. Such men do not steal another's goods — they steal the Dao's name to run their private ends.

"This is called the thieving piper." Both silk manuscripts are damaged at this graph; the collated text reads 竽, Wang Bi 夸, "boast." The thieving piper steals the name of the Dao to toot himself, cheating the age with a pipe that has no breath in it — hence, not the Dao. Yaomiao ventures 竽 runs deeper than 夸: the boaster merely puffs, and his words stay empty; the false piper occupies the seat itself without the substance — a man of no substance filling the count of men of substance, and the rot runs deeper. Wang Bi's 夸 turns real usurpation into hollow bragging and files the edge off the phrase. A pity.

And the close: 非道也 — the silk text carries no exclamatory 哉. Spare words, deep cut. Not a sigh but a plain verdict: such men, such conduct, run flat against the Dao, beyond doubt. Are they few today, who speak the Dao with their mouths and live the thieving piper's life? Patterned robes to please the crowd, sharp swords to cow it, glutted past appetite and heaping up wealth: the substance of stealing the Dao, wearing the Dao's name. "Because he does not contend, no one in all-under-heaven can contend with him" — but these contend for name, contend for gain, contend for show: thieves of the Dao, not walkers of it. That Laozi cuts them off with the flat "not the Dao," sparing not even the breath of a sigh, is because the fraud has gone too far to shield. Ah — that the Dao does not walk the world, and the thieving pipers multiply: a lament as old as the ages.
