The Book of De · Chapter Sixteen · The Thieving Piper (盜竽)

English Translation

If I held fast (挈) to a measure of knowing and walked the great Dao (大道), I would fear only the setting-up of schemes (施).

The great Dao is utterly level; yet the people dearly love to pick it apart (解).

The court swept spotless; the fields gone to weed; the granaries standing empty. Robes of patterned color, sharp swords at the belt, glutted with food, carrying wealth to spare: this is called the thieving piper (盜竽). It is not the Dao.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

The point of this chapter: the failing of the man who would walk the Dao, and the wrong of those above — the two entangled, closing on a warning against the theft of the Dao itself.

First, 使我挈有知也: silk manuscript A writes 挈, "to hold fast in the hand" (manuscript B has 介), where Wang Bi has 介然, "for a moment." Yaomiao ventures that 挈 is the stronger. He who walks the great Dao does not wake to it once by accident; he holds and hoists this knowing, ever alert, as one carries a thing by its handle, never daring to slacken — the sage's real work. A knowing that flickers on "for a moment" flickers off as fast; its work is shallow.

"Walking the great way, my one fear is the setting-up": 施 is contrivance, institution. The knowing man walks the way and fears the set-up scheme above all, dreading that one wrong institution breeds harm without end. Heshang Gong's comment on this chapter dwells on the danger of reckless 施: the same point.

"The great Dao is utterly level; yet the people dearly love to pick it apart." This line rewards the longest chewing. The great Dao is level and simple in the extreme, easy to walk. But the people do not love the Dao: they take its very plainness for a difficulty, and bore into it, splitting and explaining; this is 好解, the love of picking-apart. Manuscript B and the Wang Bi recension read 徑: "the people love bypaths." Yaomiao ventures that 好解 runs deeper than 好徑: a bypath is still a road, only hastier; but picking-apart takes clever wit as its measuring-line, breaks the whole of the great Dao into rubble, and turns the utterly simple into the endlessly involved — not merely failing to follow the Dao, but presuming to lay human cleverness over it. As Laozi said earlier: "Foreknowing is the flower of the Dao and the beginning of folly": the foreknower's disease is exactly this boring and guessing, taking the Dao's flower and leaving its substance behind. The people who love picking-apart are sick with the same sickness.

"The court swept spotless, the fields gone to weed, the granaries standing empty; robes of patterned color, sharp swords at the belt, glutted with food, wealth to spare": the wrong of those above. Polished above and stripped below; ornamented without and greedy within. Such men do not steal another's goods — they steal the Dao's name to run their private ends.

"This is called the thieving piper." Both silk manuscripts are damaged at this graph; the collated text reads 竽, Wang Bi 夸, "boast." The thieving piper steals the name of the Dao to toot himself, cheating the age with a pipe that has no breath in it — hence, not the Dao. Yaomiao ventures 竽 runs deeper than 夸: the boaster merely puffs, and his words stay empty; the false piper occupies the seat itself without the substance — a man of no substance filling the count of men of substance, and the rot runs deeper. Wang Bi's 夸 turns real usurpation into hollow bragging and files the edge off the phrase. A pity.

And the close: 非道也 — the silk text carries no exclamatory 哉. Spare words, deep cut. Not a sigh but a plain verdict: such men, such conduct, run flat against the Dao, beyond doubt. Are they few today, who speak the Dao with their mouths and live the thieving piper's life? Patterned robes to please the crowd, sharp swords to cow it, glutted past appetite and heaping up wealth: the substance of stealing the Dao, wearing the Dao's name. "Because he does not contend, no one in all-under-heaven can contend with him" — but these contend for name, contend for gain, contend for show: thieves of the Dao, not walkers of it. That Laozi cuts them off with the flat "not the Dao," sparing not even the breath of a sigh, is because the fraud has gone too far to shield. Ah — that the Dao does not walk the world, and the thieving pipers multiply: a lament as old as the ages.
