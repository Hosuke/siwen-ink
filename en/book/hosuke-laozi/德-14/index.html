The Book of De · Chapter Fourteen · Honored and Prized

English Translation

The Dao (道) gives them birth and De (德) rears them; material form shapes them, and vessels bring them to completion.

Hence the ten thousand things honor the Dao and prize De.

The Dao's honor, De's prize — no one confers the title (爵): it is constantly self-so (自然).

The Dao gives birth to them, rears them, grows them, brings them through, steadies them, ripens them, feeds them, shelters them.

To give birth yet refuse to possess; to act yet refuse to lean on the work; to rear yet refuse to rule: this is called dark De (玄德).

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

The Dao is that by which the ten thousand things are so, that in which their patterns are checked. Yet the Dao gives birth to things and does not own the work; and this is called dark De (玄德).

Collating the chapter: the silk text reads 道生之而德畜之，物形之而器成之, where the Wang Bi recension has 勢, "circumstance," for 器, "vessel." 器 is a thing's embodied form; 勢 is its field of force. The silk text weighs shape and substance; Wang Bi weighs momentum and occasion. Both pass; but 器 follows on from "things give them shape" in proper file. Again the silk text: 道之尊也，德之貴也，夫莫之爵也，而恆自然也, where Wang Bi has 命, "mandate," for 爵, "title." Title is the name of enfeoffment; mandate is the word of decree: the two are near in sense. Laozi's point is that the Dao's honor and De's prize issue wholly from the self-so: no ruler stands behind them, no willing Heaven, no maker. This is the taproot of the doctrine of the Dao, and it must not be blurred.

The Dao gives birth, rears, grows, brings through, steadies, ripens, feeds, shelters; the generative work lacks nothing. But the crux is the three refusals (弗): to give birth yet refuse to possess, to act yet refuse to lean on the work, to rear yet refuse to rule. Three refusals, each peeling one grip away: generating without claiming ownership, working without leaning on the merit, rearing without sitting as master: the root of dark De. 玄 is the deep-hidden that does not show itself; dark De is the work completed and not dwelt in, the bounty spread wide and never flaunted; it is everything the world's strivers, scrambling to have, to lean, to master, are not.

Someone asks: how does the Great Learning's "illuminating the bright De" stand to this? Yaomiao says: the Confucian illuminates the bright De, first the light, then the extension, the practice in ordered steps. The Daoist's dark De illuminates and transforms in a single act, rooted wholly in the self-so. They arrive at one place by different doors. The Confucian enters by presence, the Daoist returns by absence: two leaves of one door, into the same room. The world, dazzled by name and rank, does not know that the De of not contending is the greatest De of all: because he does not contend, no one in all-under-heaven can contend with him. The sense of dark De only grows clearer here.
