The Book of De · Chapter Fourteen · Honored and Prized

English Translation

The Dao (道) gives them birth and De (德) rears them; material form shapes them, and vessels bring them to completion.

Hence the ten thousand things honor the Dao and prize De.

The Dao's honor, De's prize — no one confers the title (爵): it is constantly self-so (自然).

The Dao gives birth to them, rears them, grows them, brings them through, steadies them, ripens them, feeds them, shelters them.

To shelter yet refuse to possess; to act yet refuse to lean on the work; to rear yet refuse to rule: this is called dark De (玄德).

Notes on Silk Manuscripts A and B

This chapter follows Manuscript A (MS A); where MS A is damaged (道之尊也, 亭之毒之, 養之 and 復之), it is supplied from Manuscript B (MS B). Graphs are those outside the brackets in the Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng (2014; hereafter Jicheng).

  • 道生之而德畜之: Follows MS A, which opens with a chapter-division dot; MS B reads 故道生之德畜之, without 而. MS B's 故, read by the Jicheng from the offset on the backing silk, is found in no other witness; since MS A and the Peking University manuscript both mark a chapter division before 道生之, and the received texts also begin a new chapter here, the Jicheng takes it as superfluous, though some attach it to the preceding sentence. (Jicheng IV, p. 20, n. 69; p. 200, n. 55)
  • 物刑之而器成之: Both manuscripts agree. The silk texts and the Peking University manuscript use 刑 for 形 "form". MS B also has 器 "vessel", while the Peking University manuscript and the received texts have 勢 "circumstance": Wang Bi reads 物形之，勢成之. (Jicheng IV, p. 20, n. 70)
  • 是以萬物尊道而貴德: Both manuscripts agree. In MS A the lower part of 尊 is written 廾, and 德 is read directly by the Jicheng. Most received texts add 莫不 after 萬物; the Yan Zun text and Dunhuang MS Ji lack it, in agreement with the silk. Wang Bi reads 萬物莫不尊道而貴德. (Jicheng IV, p. 20, n. 71)
  • 道之尊也: 道 is lost in MS A, which also lacks the final 也; the line follows MS B. Wang Bi has no 也. (Jicheng IV, p. 20, n. 72)
  • 夫莫之爵而恒自然也: Follows MS A; MS B reads 夫莫之爵也而恆自然也. MS A writes 爵 in an abbreviated form, 目 on the left and 寸 on the right (printed 时 in the Jicheng, read as 爵); MS B writes the full 爵. MS A's 然 is a corrupt form with 肰 above 示, which the 1980 edition silently normalized. Wang Bi and Heshang Gong read 夫莫之命而常自然, with 命 "mandate"; but Yan Zun, Fu Yi and most other received texts have 爵 "rank", agreeing with the silk. (Jicheng IV, p. 20, n. 72)
  • 道生之 / 畜之: Both manuscripts agree. MS A marks another chapter-division dot before this 道生之, thus dividing the received chapter 51 into two. Neither manuscript has 德 before 畜之, where Wang Bi reads 故道生之，德畜之. The 之 of MS B's 畜之 is read directly by the Jicheng from a fragment stuck to the backing silk. (Jicheng IV, p. 20, n. 69; p. 200, n. 56)
  • 長之遂之: Follows MS A; MS B reads □□育之, its 育 bracketed as a restoration in the 1980 edition and now read directly from the offset. Wang Bi reads 長之育之. (Jicheng IV, p. 200, n. 57)
  • 亭之毒之: Follows MS B. In MS A the traces in the position of 毒 resemble neither 毒 nor 孰, and the original editors left the graph unrestored, as does the Jicheng. (Jicheng IV, p. 21, n. 73)
  • 養之 / 復之弗有也: In MS A 養之覆 is lost and only the final 之 is read directly; MS B reads 養之，復之, 復 being read as 覆 "to shelter", its 之 read directly from surviving traces. MS A has two further graphs lost before 弗有也, where MS B is damaged; Wang Bi reads 蓋之覆之，生而不有. (Jicheng IV, p. 200, n. 58)
  • 為而弗寺也: Follows MS A; 寺 is read as 恃 "to lean on". MS B's 而弗之心, restored as 而弗恃 in the 1980 edition, is read by the Jicheng from the offset on the backing silk; Chen Jian takes 之心 as the graph 志 mistakenly split in two, 志 being read as 恃. Wang Bi reads 為而不恃. (Jicheng IV, p. 200, n. 59)
  • 長而弗宰也: Follows MS A; MS B lacks 也, and its 長而 was bracketed as a restoration in the 1980 edition and is now read directly from the offset. Wang Bi reads 長而不宰. (Jicheng IV, p. 201, n. 60)
  • 此之謂玄德: Follows MS A, which writes 謂, not 胃; MS B reads 是胃玄德, and Wang Bi 是謂玄德.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

The Dao is that by which the ten thousand things are so, that in which their patterns are checked. Yet the Dao gives birth to things and does not own the work; and this is called dark De (玄德).

Collating the chapter: the silk text reads 道生之而德畜之，物刑之而器成之, where the Wang Bi recension has 勢, "circumstance," for 器, "vessel." 器 is a thing's embodied form; 勢 is its field of force. The silk text weighs shape and substance; Wang Bi weighs momentum and occasion. Both pass; but 器 follows on from "things give them shape" in proper file. Again the silk text: 道之尊也，德之貴也，夫莫之爵也，而恆自然也, where Wang Bi has 命, "mandate," for 爵, "title." Title is the name of enfeoffment; mandate is the word of decree: the two are near in sense. Laozi's point is that the Dao's honor and De's prize issue wholly from the self-so: no ruler stands behind them, no willing Heaven, no maker. This is the taproot of the doctrine of the Dao, and it must not be blurred.

The Dao gives birth, rears, grows, brings through, steadies, ripens, feeds, shelters; the generative work lacks nothing. But the crux is the three refusals (弗): to give birth yet refuse to possess, to act yet refuse to lean on the work, to rear yet refuse to rule. Three refusals, each peeling one grip away: generating without claiming ownership, working without leaning on the merit, rearing without sitting as master: the root of dark De. 玄 is the deep-hidden that does not show itself; dark De is the work completed and not dwelt in, the bounty spread wide and never flaunted; it is everything the world's strivers, scrambling to have, to lean, to master, are not.

Someone asks: how does the Great Learning's "illuminating the bright De" stand to this? Yaomiao says: the Confucian illuminates the bright De, first the light, then the extension, the practice in ordered steps. The Daoist's dark De illuminates and transforms in a single act, rooted wholly in the self-so. They arrive at one place by different doors. The Confucian enters by presence, the Daoist returns by absence: two leaves of one door, into the same room. The world, dazzled by name and rank, does not know that the De of not contending is the greatest De of all: because he does not contend, no one in all-under-heaven can contend with him. The sense of dark De only grows clearer here.
