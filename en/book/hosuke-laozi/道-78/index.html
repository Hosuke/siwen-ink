The Book of Dao · Chapter Thirty-four · Completing the Great

English Translation

The Dao drifts everywhere (道渢呵) — it can go left, can go right.

It completes the work, finishes the thing — and does not name it as its own (弗名有). The ten thousand things come home to it, and it does not play their lord.

Being constantly without desire (恆无欲) — it may be named small. The ten thousand things coming home and it not playing their lord — it may be named great.

Hence the sage can complete the great: because he does not make greatness his undertaking (不為大), he can complete the great.

Commentary

Yaomiao says:

This chapter sets out how the Dao completes the great. The silk text writes 道渢呵亓可左右也, "the Dao drifts everywhere — it can go left, can go right," where the Wang Bi recension writes 大道氾兮其可左右. 渢 has the wind-sign, 氾 the water-sign — different graphs, one sense: the Dao flowing unhindered, spread shoreless. Note that the silk text does not say "great": the Dao's drifting is neither great nor small, everywhere alike, and so it can go left and right — the Dao's universality.

"It completes the work, finishes the thing — and does not name it as its own": Wang Bi writes 功成不名有. The silk text adds 遂事 — covering the work and the affair both, the fuller sense. And the silk text's 弗名有 against Wang Bi's 不名有: 弗 is the flat never, steeper than 不. 不 merely negates the act; with 弗, the very thought never arises — the Dao completes the ten thousand things and the naming-it-mine never crosses its mind: the utmost of dark De.

"The ten thousand things come home to it, and it does not play their lord": the Dao does not force itself into lordship. Things lean on the Dao and live, and the Dao does not decline, does not dwell. Yet Wang Bi's text carries the clauses "does not decline" and "clothing and nourishing without being master," which the silk text lacks: the silk text runs straight — "completes the work, finishes the thing, and does not name it as its own" — spare and steep. I suspect later hands padded Wang Bi's text; take the silk text as the standard.

Weightiest of all: the silk text has the line 則恆无欲也, "being constantly without desire," which the Wang Bi text lacks. Without-desire is the backbone of the whole chapter. Desire is having something to seek; without desire, nothing is sought. Only without desire can one complete the great; only without the thought of greatness can greatness be completed. The Zhuangzi: "the utmost man has no self; the spirit-man has no work; the sage has no name" — no-self, no-work, no-name: all the effects of without-desire. And the book's second chapter: "the sage dwells in the work of non-doing and walks the teaching without words: the ten thousand things arise and he does not start them; gives them life and does not possess them; works and does not lean on it; completes the work and does not dwell in it" — answering this chapter.

The silk text's close: 是以聖人之能成大也，以亓不為大也，故能成大 — "hence the sage can complete the great: because he does not make greatness his undertaking, he can complete the great" — against Wang Bi's 以其終不自為大，故能成其大. The silk text's 不為大 against Wang Bi's 終不自為大 — the breath differs. Not-greatening-oneself still waits on the self's humility to achieve its greatness — a self still held. Not-making-greatness-one's-undertaking: greatness is never the business at all — it completes itself, of itself, with no thought of greatness in it. Laozi's point: not making the great, and the great completes itself — as water benefits the ten thousand things and keeps still, and no one in all-under-heaven can contend with it. That is the utmost art of completing the great.

This chapter's completing-the-great answers the Book of De's "the Way of Heaven has no favorites: it sides, constantly, with the good" — both set out the Dao's selflessness and its completing. The Dao births the ten thousand things and does not name them his; completes the work and does not play lord — greatness completed exactly by never making greatness the thought. Let the reader watch this achieving-by-reversal, and Laozi's Dao stands complete in his own heart.
