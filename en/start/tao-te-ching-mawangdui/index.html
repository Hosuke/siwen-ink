The Tao Te Ching from Mawangdui: Silk Manuscripts A and B

Two silk witnesses, three texts in conversation

Three texts, one welcoming place to begin

The Tao Te Ching, also called the Daodejing or Laozi, asks how we might live with greater simplicity, govern with less interference, and understand the source of things. Its short passages invite repeated reading. On Siwen, you can explore them through something especially valuable: a line-by-line comparison of the Mawangdui silk manuscripts A and B with the received Wang Bi text, accompanied by Yaomiao’s commentary and English translation.

This brings textual discovery and philosophical reading together. A difference of one word becomes visible beside the sentence it changes. A damaged passage can be read alongside another witness. An English interpretation can be followed back to its Chinese wording. You can begin with a single chapter and let these companions deepen your understanding at a comfortable pace.

The manuscripts from Changsha

In 1973, archaeologists excavated two silk copies of the Laozi from tomb 3 at Mawangdui, near Changsha in Hunan. The tomb was sealed in 168 BCE, so both copies had been written by that date. Scholars call them manuscript A and manuscript B, or jia and yi in Chinese. Together they offer direct evidence of the book as it circulated in the early Han period.

Both manuscripts place the De section before the Dao section. Their opening corresponds to chapter 38 of the familiar received edition; the passage beginning with the Dao comes later. The received book has eighty-one chapters, with chapters 1–37 in the Dao section and 38–81 in the De section. The silk copies also arrange some individual passages differently. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s account of the Laozi gives a helpful overview of these discoveries and the book’s transmission.

The silk has suffered damage, and the two copies preserve different amounts of individual passages. This makes their partnership especially useful. On Siwen’s comparison rows, squares mark missing characters, while the neighbouring text lets you continue following the thought. The experience is both intimate and concrete: a book known across the world comes into view through two particular surviving copies.

Begin with de: virtue in action

Open the first chapter of Yaomiao’s Laozi, corresponding to Wang Bi chapter 38. Its subject is de: virtue, potency, or the way the Dao takes effect in a life. The passage begins by setting higher and lower virtue beside one another, then moves through humaneness, rightness, and ritual. It ends by choosing substance and depth over outward display.

Read the English once without stopping. On a second reading, notice how intention enters the sequence. When does an action arise freely? When does a person begin to insist upon a response? The image of someone baring an arm to compel participation makes the question vivid. Even before defining every Chinese term, you can follow the movement from unforced conduct towards increasing effort and pressure.

Yaomiao reads this opening as an invitation to approach the Dao through lived conduct. His commentary attends to the difference between possessing virtue and holding tightly to the idea of being virtuous. That offers a useful question to carry into daily life: how does a good action change when recognition becomes its aim? Starting here gives the whole book a practical point of entry.

Watch the three witnesses speak

Now choose the Chinese view and the manuscript comparison. The aligned rows bring A, B, and Wang Bi together sentence by sentence. In the opening rows of this chapter, A has substantial gaps, while B preserves the words about higher and lower virtue. Later, Wang Bi’s text contains an additional clause about lower virtue acting with a purpose; the corresponding A and B cells are empty.

This is an excellent first exercise because the difference is easy to locate. Read the sequence once with the additional clause and once without it. Ask how explicitly each version builds its contrast. Then look at the repeated sentence endings in the silk text. Small words and pauses help shape the rhythm of an argument, as surely as its larger philosophical terms do.

The Wang Bi tradition is itself a rich companion. Wang Bi lived from 226 to 249 CE, and his commentary became central to the interpretation of the Laozi. Siwen’s Wang Bi edition gives access to this transmitted text. Reading it beside the manuscripts allows you to appreciate both the early witnesses and the tradition through which generations encountered the work.

A second beginning: the Dao and naming

Next open the beginning of the Dao section, numbered 45 in Siwen’s silk sequence and 1 in the received sequence. Here the book turns directly to the Dao, naming, desire, and the emergence of things. Read its paired statements slowly. Notice what each pair shares, and where the second half changes direction.

Two comparisons offer a rewarding start. The silk reading uses heng, “constant” or “enduring,” where Wang Bi’s text has chang. In the statement about the nameless beginning, Siwen’s continuous silk text speaks of the “myriad things,” while the Wang Bi text speaks of “heaven and earth.” Look across the manuscript rows to see the surviving wording behind the continuous reading, then follow how the English handles each phrase.

Yaomiao’s note draws attention to these differences and explains why he values the parallel wording about the myriad things. This lets you see commentary at work: a reader attends to a variant, hears a relationship between clauses, and develops its implications. Put your own question beside his. Does the passage invite you to think first about the origin of the world, the limits of names, or the way attention is shaped by desire?

Reading with Yaomiao

The Yaomiao Laozi volume brings together the manuscript comparison, a continuous reading text, the Wang Bi comparison, and Yaomiao’s Chinese commentary and English translation. The Chinese view provides the manuscript controls; English and parallel views help you move between the translated passages and their Chinese counterparts. Choose the view that serves the question in front of you.

For a first session, give one chapter three short readings. Begin with the English to hear the thought as a whole. Move to A, B, and Wang Bi to choose one difference worth examining. Finish with Yaomiao’s commentary, then reread the passage in light of what you have noticed. This approach makes even a little Chinese useful: you can recognise a repeated character, a missing clause, or a changed image.

Keep both chapter numbers in your notes, such as “silk 1 / Wang Bi 38.” Over time, those small comparisons build a personal map of the book. The reward is a growing acquaintance with its voice: its economy, its unexpected turns, and its patient questions about how to live.
