The Guanyinzi (Wenshi zhenjing): The Daoist Classic of Master Guanyin

Nine images for reading the Way

A meeting at the pass

Tradition holds that Yin Xi, the keeper of Hangu Pass, recognized Laozi as a teacher and asked him to leave a written account of his teaching before departing. Laozi entrusted him with the work known as the Daodejing. The name Guanyin here means the keeper of the pass; the figure is a Daoist master, distinct from the Buddhist bodhisattva whose name is also commonly romanized as Guanyin.

Sima Qian's Records of the Historian, chapter sixty-three, tells of Yin Xi's request and Laozi's composition of a work in two parts concerning the Dao and virtue. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's account of Laozi introduces this influential story and its place in the tradition. Hangu Pass belongs to the story's traditional geography; Sima Qian's brief account calls it simply the pass.

The Guanyinzi, also called the Wenshi zhenjing, bears this master's name. Scholars debate the date and authorship of the received text, often treating it as a later work rather than the surviving words of a pre-Qin author. Its nine chapters offer a rich reading experience in their own right: brief sayings, vivid comparisons, and reflections on the mind's relationship with the Dao.

Nine chapters, nine concrete images

Each chapter begins by explaining its title. Read these opening definitions together before attempting the whole book. They provide a compact map, though the sayings within each chapter range beyond a single topic.

  • One: Yu 宇, Space or the Cosmos. Its opening identifies yu with the Dao. The chapter explores the limits of naming and grasping.
  • Two: Zhu 柱, Pillar. The pillar establishes heaven and earth; the sayings explore the cosmos and how it is apprehended.
  • Three: Ji 極, the Ultimate or Pole. The opening honours the sage, followed by accounts of the sage's conduct and responsiveness.
  • Four: Fu 符, Tally. Its opening names essence, spirit, and the hun and po aspects of life.
  • Five: Jian 鑒, Mirror. The mirror signifies the heart or mind, xin.
  • Six: Bi 匕, Spoon. Its opening names eating; the chapter considers embodied life, perception, and the boundaries of self.
  • Seven: Fu 釜, Cauldron. The cauldron signifies transformation.
  • Eight: Chou 籌, Counting Sticks. The opening names things, followed by reflections on multiplicity, patterns, and change.
  • Nine: Yao 藥, Medicine. The opening describes varied remedies, introducing practical counsel for different circumstances.

The English titles are working glosses. Keep the Chinese characters beside them, particularly for the two chapters romanized Fu. A tally and a cauldron invite quite different lines of thought. Try imagining each object before asking what philosophical work it performs.

Start with the water in the first chapter

One of the first chapter's most approachable sayings describes someone looking for water. Finding a pond insufficient, the seeker turns to rivers and then the sea, while overlooking the water in bodily fluids and tears. Read the movement from the distant and immense to the near and ordinary. The image offers a way of asking whether the search itself has hidden what is already present.

A neighbouring saying compares the Dao's relationship to things with a potter's relationship to vessels. Another likens the Dao to a sea receiving gold, stones, and impurities without becoming full. Read these as successive experiments in comparison. What can the potter image express? What becomes easier to see through the sea?

The same chapter contrasts skills such as archery, driving a chariot, music, and board games, which require learning, with the possibility of realizing the Dao in a single breath. The text presents direct realization as possible because the Dao has no fixed shape or location. Sit with that claim beside the water image: both passages invite attention to the assumptions brought to seeking.

The mirror and the familiar world

Turn next to chapter five. Its opening identifies the mirror with the mind, then describes ways the mind becomes occupied or obscured. Further on, a boat and a cart provide another pair of images: water carries the boat, and an ox moves the cart. The passage uses these familiar arrangements to question what moves thought.

Read one comparison twice. On the first reading, describe the physical scene. On the second, follow the turn towards the mind. This small separation is helpful when the language becomes compressed: you have a clear image to return to without needing to settle every term immediately.

Chapter six extends the inquiry through dreams, bodily sensation, and the distinction between oneself and another. Its opening asks who counts as “I” and who counts as “other” when the people encountered in a dream also seem to have different thoughts and pains. Compare this with the Zhuangzi, especially the dream passages in “Equalizing Things.” Let each text's particular question remain visible as you notice the resemblance.

The Wenshi tradition

Wenshi is also the name of a Daoist lineage tradition that looks to Yin Xi as an ancestral teacher. Tradition holds that its transmission passes through figures including Chen Tuan and Zhang Sanfeng. In accounts of this tradition, direct realization and a return to openness receive particular emphasis. A study published by Huafan University's Department of Philosophy in 2000, section 3.22, records this lineage account and its description of practice.

For a beginning reader, this connection offers a useful question to bring back to the book: how does a teaching of immediate realization coexist with counsel about everyday conduct? Read the first chapter's single-breath passage beside the ninth chapter's advice. The pairing gives the question textual substance without requiring a reconstruction of the entire lineage.

Medicine for particular occasions

Chapter nine starts with small things: a small opening can sink a boat, and small matters deserve care. Later sayings advise attention to circumstances, modesty in one's judgments, and generosity towards others. One passage warns against treating a sage's apparent clumsiness or obscurity as an excuse for one's own shortcomings.

Another compares sayings to medicines: an ordinary remedy used appropriately can help, while something precious used inappropriately can harm. The comparison supplies a useful reading practice. Before extracting a maxim, ask what difficulty it addresses and what kind of attachment it seeks to loosen. Then read the neighbouring saying, which may change the balance.

This practical ending gives the earlier reflections a human setting. After reading about the Dao and transformation, you return to speech, work, other people, and the need to respond with care.

Reading with the Yaomiao commentary

Siwen's Yaomiao commentary on the Wenshi classic provides a Chinese-language introduction and commentary on all nine chapters. The linked page has an English interface, but the commentary itself remains in literary Chinese; an English translation is not currently available. The opening introduction discusses the work's transmission and attribution, while the chapter pages place the original text beside Yaomiao's interpretation.

If you read Chinese, begin with the introduction, then compare the first chapter's water passage with the commentary. If you are learning, use the nine chapter titles as landmarks and work through a short original passage with language support. Keeping the original and the modern commentary distinct allows both voices to be heard more clearly.
