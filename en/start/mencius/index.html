Mencius: Human Nature, Benevolence and Government

Growing a humane heart in a difficult world

Meet a teacher in conversation

The Mencius, or Mengzi, introduces its teacher through conversations: a ruler asks how to enrich his state, a disciple asks about courage, an opponent challenges his account of human nature. Mencius replies with questions, examples, and stories. A frightened ox, a child beside a well, and a mountain stripped of trees become occasions for thinking carefully about human life.

Mencius, whose personal name was Meng Ke, lived in the fourth century BCE, during the Warring States period. He came from Zou, in present-day Shandong, and visited rulers in search of an opportunity to put humane government into practice. He understood himself as continuing Confucius's teaching. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry on Mencius gives a helpful introduction to his life and intellectual setting.

Begin with one conversation rather than a collection of famous quotations. Notice what the other person wants, how Mencius responds, and whether the discussion changes the original question. His account of goodness often becomes clearest in these movements between two speakers.

Finding passages in the seven books

The received Mencius has seven books, each divided into an upper and a lower part. English references usually call these A and B. Thus 2A6 means book two, part A, passage six; 6A8 means book six, part A, passage eight. These references are useful when comparing translations.

The books take their names from a person or opening expression: King Hui of Liang, Gongsun Chou, Duke Wen of Teng, Li Lou, Wan Zhang, Gaozi, and Exhausting the Heart, Jin xin. In the library, 上 indicates the upper part and 下 the lower. You can use the title alongside the numerical reference to find a passage without knowing much Chinese.

For a first route, read 1A1, 2A6, 1A7, and 6A8. They move from a ruler's ambitions to spontaneous concern, then to the extension and cultivation of that concern. Keep the order flexible: the stories reward returning after another passage has made a question more vivid.

The child at the well and the four beginnings

In 2A6, Mencius asks us to imagine suddenly seeing a young child about to fall into a well. The immediate response is alarm and compassion. He sets aside calculations about pleasing the child's parents, gaining praise, or escaping a bad reputation. His attention is on a response that arises before such calculations.

He identifies four beginnings, also translated as sprouts: compassion, shame and aversion to wrongdoing, deference, and the sense of right and wrong. These are the beginnings of benevolence (ren), righteousness (yi), ritual propriety (li), and wisdom (zhi). The image of a beginning matters. It points towards something that can develop through attention and practice.

Read the end of the passage as carefully as its opening. Mencius compares development with a fire beginning to burn and a spring beginning to flow. Ask what these images add to the child's predicament. A moment of concern has a direction, but its fuller expression requires more than the moment itself.

For your notes, place each beginning beside its corresponding virtue. Then choose an ordinary example: recognizing another person's distress, regretting an unkind remark, making room for someone, or judging whether an action is fair. These are reading exercises for seeing the distinctions, rather than a checklist by which to declare a person good.

What it means for human nature to be good

In book six, Mencius debates Gaozi about human nature, xing. At 6A2, he compares its tendency towards goodness with water's tendency to flow downwards. Water can be forced upwards, but that does not describe its own tendency. The analogy invites the reader to distinguish capacities and tendencies from the effects of pressure.

The mountain in 6A8 gives this thought a landscape. Ox Mountain once had flourishing trees. Repeated cutting and the grazing of animals left it bare; an observer might then suppose it had never been capable of growing trees. Mencius applies the image to the human heart. Repeated damage can conceal the capacity one is trying to understand.

Read the account of rain, dew, and renewed growth alongside the cutting and grazing. The passage asks you to attend to conditions as well as appearances. In this reading, care for moral life includes noticing what repeatedly nourishes or exhausts it. The argument also helps explain why Mencius can affirm a good human nature while taking cruelty and failure seriously.

The ruler who spared an ox

At 1A7, King Xuan of Qi recalls seeing an ox led to sacrifice and being unable to bear its frightened appearance. He ordered that it be spared and a sheep substituted. Mencius recognizes in the episode a capacity for compassion that could reach the king's people.

Follow the dialogue's changes of scale. It moves from one animal seen at close quarters to people beyond the court. Mencius asks about the difference between being unable to act and failing to act, and about extending care beyond its immediate object. The ruler's response to the ox becomes the starting point for a demanding conversation about government.

This is a useful passage to read aloud with another person. Give each speaker a distinct voice and pause when the king changes direction. The teaching unfolds through encouragement, challenge, and recognition; its force depends partly on helping the king see something already present in his own experience.

Benevolence has material responsibilities

The book opens with King Hui of Liang asking what benefit Mencius can bring his state. In 1A1, Mencius redirects attention to benevolence and righteousness. Read this beside 1A3, where he discusses agricultural seasons, fishing, forests, food, and support for older people. His concern for humane rule reaches into the conditions that allow households to live securely.

In 1A7 he argues that people need a steady livelihood if they are to sustain a steady heart. A ruler who leaves them exposed to deprivation and then punishes their resulting offences has failed in a prior responsibility. The passage joins moral education to the provision of ordinary necessities.

At 7B14, Mencius places the people first, the altars of soil and grain next, and the ruler last in importance. Read it as a claim within his world of kingship about the weight of political responsibilities. Set it beside the earlier conversations: the brief pronouncement gains substance from the discussions of food, care, and the proper use of power.

Learning without pulling up the shoots

One more story makes a gentle ending. At 2A2, a man from Song tries to help his shoots grow by pulling them upwards. His exertion ruins them. The image appears in a discussion of cultivating qi, and it warns against forcing a process whose conditions require patient care.

Return from this story to the four beginnings. Read one passage at a time, write down the situation it describes, and allow another conversation to complicate your first understanding. Mencius's examples become companions in inquiry when they remain connected to the people and questions around them.
