The Analects of Confucius: A Reader’s Guide

Short conversations, questions that take time

Enter through a conversation

The Analects, or Lunyu, invites you into the company of a teacher and his students. A pupil asks about learning, another about caring for people, another about governing a state. Confucius answers with a saying, a question, an example, or a few words tailored to the person before him. The book's short passages reward the kind of attention we bring to a thoughtful conversation.

Begin with one exchange and read it twice. On the first reading, follow what is said. On the second, notice who asks the question and what the answer asks that person to do. A notebook with three headings—speaker, key term, and question—will soon become a useful companion. There is room to begin with the passages that speak most directly to your own life.

Confucius and his world

Confucius, known in Chinese as Kongzi, “Master Kong,” is traditionally dated to 551–479 BCE. He came from the region of present-day Shandong and taught in the cultural world of the state of Lu. The authority of the Zhou kings had weakened, and competing states and powerful families struggled for influence. His concern with trustworthy rulers, cultivated conduct, and the obligations of office belongs to this unsettled political setting.

The Analects preserves sayings, conversations, and recollections transmitted after his death. Tradition holds that his disciples and their successors gathered his teaching. Scholars debate the collection's stages of formation. The received text has twenty books, containing both brief sayings and longer scenes. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's account of Confucius provides further historical context.

References give a book and passage number: 1.1 means the first passage of Book 1. Chinese book titles usually come from their opening words, so Xue er, “Learning and…,” names the first book. This guide uses the numbering in Eno's English translation, linked below; our Chinese text labels its 7.22 as 7.21 and its 15.24 as 15.23. The opening words will help you find both passages.

Learning that becomes a way of living

Start with 1.1. It brings together the pleasure of learning and repeated practice, the joy of friends arriving from afar, and the composure of someone who remains unresentful when unrecognized. The three sentences describe a life shaped by learning: practice gives it continuity, friendship gives it companionship, and patience carries it through periods without praise.

Read the Chinese opening, 學而時習之, alongside your translation. Xue means learning; xi points toward practice or repeated engagement. Choose something small to revisit after a day: a passage, a promise, or the manner in which you listened to someone. This makes a useful personal experiment in the relationship between understanding and practice.

Now turn to 2.15, where learning and reflection need one another. Then read 7.22, the saying beginning 三人行, about finding teachers among one's companions. What is good in another person offers something to follow; what is troubling offers an occasion for examining oneself. These passages make learning available in study, friendship, and ordinary encounters.

Humaneness in particular relationships

Ren 仁 is often translated as humaneness, benevolence, or humanity. It is one of the central concerns of the Analects, and several students ask Confucius about it. Read two answers together: the response to Yan Hui in 12.1 and the response to Fan Chi in 12.22.

To Yan Hui, Confucius connects ren with mastering oneself and returning to ritual propriety. When Yan Hui asks for particulars, the answer turns to seeing, hearing, speaking, and acting. To Fan Chi, the initial reply is wonderfully brief: care for people. Together, these exchanges give a learner both a generous direction and concrete places to begin working on conduct.

In 15.24, Zigong asks for a word that can guide a whole life. Confucius offers shu, commonly translated as reciprocity, and explains it through refraining from imposing on others what one would not want for oneself. Bring this passage beside the two answers about ren. The comparison suggests a daily practice: attend to another person's position before acting on the first impulse.

Ritual, respect, and feeling

Li 禮, ritual or ritual propriety, includes ceremonies, forms of courtesy, mourning, and conduct within relationships. In Confucius's world, these practices carried the inherited culture of the Zhou. They gave respect a visible form and provided repeated occasions for cultivating it.

Passage 3.3 asks what ritual and music can accomplish in someone who lacks ren. Read it beside 12.1, where returning to ritual helps define humane conduct. Each passage lends something to the other: cultivated feeling finds expression in form, while the form calls for a humane person to give it life.

For a familiar example, turn to 2.7. Ziyou asks about filial conduct, and Confucius makes respect central to the care of parents. The question reaches beyond providing food and other necessities to the manner of caring. As you read, consider how tone of voice, patience, and attention can change the same outward action. The Book of Rites offers a wider setting for exploring such relationships between feeling and form.

Government begins with responsibility

The Analects frequently moves between personal cultivation and public life. In 2.3, Confucius compares governing by administrative measures and punishment with guiding through virtue and ritual. He distinguishes the wish to escape penalties from the development of a sense of shame and an inclination toward good conduct. Read slowly enough to notice the different kinds of response each approach encourages.

In 12.7, Zigong asks about government. Food, military resources, and the people's trust enter the answer. As Zigong asks which could be relinquished under necessity, trust emerges as indispensable. The dialogue gives an unusually clear reading exercise: follow each question and watch how a list becomes an ordering of priorities.

Passage 13.3 introduces the “rectification of names.” Asked by Zilu what he would address first in government, Confucius links the proper use of names to speech, action, ritual and music, punishment, and the people's ability to find their bearings. Trace that sequence before drawing a conclusion. It invites a concrete question about public responsibility: how well do the names of offices and duties correspond to what their holders actually do?

A first week with the book

Give the first session to 1.1, 2.15, and 7.22; the second to 12.1, 12.22, and 15.24; and the third to 3.3, 2.7, 2.3, and 12.7. End the week by rereading the passage that stayed with you. Compare your first notes with what you now notice about its speaker, setting, or key term.

For an English companion, Robert Eno's open teaching translation, hosted by Indiana University, supplies notes, a glossary, and an introduction to the principal disciples. Keep the Chinese passage nearby as you use it. A growing familiarity with a few words—ren, li, learning, and trust—will gradually make the conversations feel like a connected world.

Where to go next

  • The Analects: begin with Book 1 and return to the passages in this guide.
  • Mencius: try the opening conversation with King Hui of Liang, where benefit and humane government come into view.
  • The Great Learning: follow its opening sequence from personal cultivation to the ordering of public life.
  • The Doctrine of the Mean: read the opening discussion of the Way and cultivation.
  • Confucian and Daoist Classics: choose a further route through the library.

Texts to read

  • Analects 論語

    A collection of sayings and conversations associated with Confucius and his disciples, concerning learning, ritual, humane conduct, and government.

  • Mencius 孟子

    A collection of dialogues and arguments associated with Mengzi, addressing humane government, moral development, human nature, and the responsibilities of rulers.

  • Book of Rites 禮記

    A Confucian collection of writings on ritual, mourning, music, education, and social relationships, transmitted as one of the major ritual classics.

  • Great Learning 大學

    A Confucian text linking self-cultivation with the ordering of family, state, and world.

  • Doctrine of the Mean 中庸

    A Confucian text on sincerity, harmony, and moral cultivation, traditionally associated with Zisi.
