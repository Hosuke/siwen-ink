Start Here: The Confucian and Daoist Classics

A welcoming path into a living tradition of reading

Chinese classical learning can begin with a very small encounter: a teacher welcoming a friend, a poem about longing, or a cook explaining his work. Each offers something recognisable before opening a larger question. How do we become more humane? What makes a life well ordered? How can we act with care in a world that continually changes?

Siwen brings Confucian and Daoist writings together in a library where you can follow these questions through original texts and commentary. This guide introduces the shelves and suggests several first readings. Choose the passage that speaks to your curiosity. A few lines read attentively can become the beginning of a lasting relationship with a book.

Finding your way around the shelves

Traditional Chinese libraries organise books into four broad categories, called sibu: jing, classics; shi, histories; zi, masters; and ji, literary collections. The scheme developed from the third century CE and became standard in the sixth. The USC Libraries introduction to the Four Branches explains their scope.

The Classics category gathers the Confucian canon and its commentaries, together with related learning. Histories includes chronicles, biographies, geography, and other records. Masters contains philosophical writings alongside medicine, agriculture, and many forms of specialised knowledge. Literary Collections brings together poetry, prose, and collected works. These categories offer several routes through the same civilisation: its teachings, remembered events, arguments, and literary voices.

For a first visit, begin with the Classics and Masters shelves. The Analects belongs to the Confucian canon; the Zhuangzi belongs to Masters. Both are enduring classics in the ordinary English sense. Knowing the catalogue terms helps you find related works and recognise the kinds of learning that grew around them.

The Thirteen Classics and the Four Books

The Thirteen Classics form the received Confucian canon. They comprise the Book of Changes, Book of Documents, Book of Odes, Rites of Zhou, Book of Etiquette and Ceremonial, Book of Rites, the Zuo, Gongyang, and Guliang commentaries on the Spring and Autumn Annals, the Analects, Classic of Filial Piety, Erya, and Mencius. The Chinese University of Hong Kong's classical text research centre identifies these works in its study of early citations.

Their variety is part of their richness. Songs, ritual arrangements, speeches, historical narratives, and conversations all contribute to an education in how to live with others. A reader interested in poetry may enter through the Odes; someone interested in government may prefer Mencius. You can acquire the larger map gradually as one text sends you to another.

The Four Books offer a more compact course: the Analects, Mencius, Great Learning, and Doctrine of the Mean. The last two are also chapters of the Book of Rites. Zhu Xi, the Song scholar who lived from 1130 to 1200, brought these works together with influential commentaries. His educational project is introduced in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Zhu Xi.

For an accessible sequence, read a little of the Analects, continue to a complete conversation in Mencius, and then try the opening of the Great Learning. You will move from particular encounters toward an account of cultivation that connects person, family, and government.

Begin with learning and human feeling

Open Analects 1.1. Its three sentences bring together learning and practice, the arrival of friends, and composure when others fail to recognise us. Read them as a sequence. What changes when learning is joined to companionship? What kind of strength enables a person to continue learning without depending upon recognition?

Then read 1.4, where Zengzi examines his conduct in serving others, dealing with friends, and practising what has been transmitted to him. The two passages give a manageable first session: joy in learning, followed by an honest look at daily conduct. Keep the names of the speakers beside your notes; you are entering a community of teachers and pupils.

For Mencius, begin with 2A6, in the first part of “Gongsun Chou.” Mencius asks us to imagine suddenly seeing a child about to fall into a well. He takes the immediate feeling of alarm and concern as a starting point for moral development. The passage goes on to identify four beginnings: compassion, shame, deference, and a sense of right and wrong. Notice the movement from an ordinary response to the work of extending and nourishing it.

Next, open the Great Learning within the Book of Rites. Its opening traces connections among knowledge, intention, the heart, personal cultivation, family, state, and the wider world. Draw the sequence in your notebook. Then ask how the smallest part of that sequence might matter in an everyday relationship.

Make room for poetry, ritual, and change

The Book of Odes offers a different pace. Begin with “Guan ju,” the opening poem. Birds call beside a river; a desired companion occupies the speaker's thoughts; repeated images carry longing toward imagined companionship and music. Read the poem aloud, even in translation. Listen for the return of a phrase and notice what changes around it.

In the Book of Rites, try the opening of “The Record of Music,” or Yueji. It connects sound with the movements of the human heart in response to things. Read this beside the poem: what does each suggest about the way feeling becomes audible and shareable? Such a pairing makes the relationship between poetry and cultivation tangible.

The Book of Changes contains sixty-four hexagrams, each made of six lines, with statements attached to the figures and their individual lines. Begin with Qian, the first hexagram. Follow its dragon images from the hidden dragon to the dragon in flight. Note how timing and position affect what action is fitting. Read the accompanying explanation afterward, giving yourself time to see the sequence first.

Entering Daoist writing

The Tao Te Ching in the Wang Bi tradition contains eighty-one short chapters. Chapter 8 offers a welcoming beginning through its image of water: benefiting things, dwelling in low places, and moving without contention. Read it twice, first for the image and then for the forms of conduct it illuminates. Chapter 11, on the spaces within a wheel, vessel, and room, makes a fruitful companion.

The Zhuangzi invites you into longer scenes. Begin with Cook Ding in chapter 3, “The Secret of Caring for Life.” His knife moves through the spaces in an ox's structure, and his account joins practised ease with care at difficult places. Compare this with the water of the Tao Te Ching: how does each image help you imagine responsive action?

The Guanyinzi, also known as the Wenshi zhenjing, offers another voice in Daoist learning. Tradition attributes it to Yin Xi, the keeper of the pass associated with Laozi. Scholars debate the dating of its received text. Begin with chapter 1, where looking for water in ponds, rivers, and seas leads back to the water in one's own body. The image invites a return from distant searching to what is close at hand.

Texts to read

  • Analects 論語

    A collection of sayings and conversations associated with Confucius and his disciples, concerning learning, ritual, humane conduct, and government.

  • Mencius 孟子

    A collection of dialogues and arguments associated with Mengzi, addressing humane government, moral development, human nature, and the responsibilities of rulers.

  • Book of Changes (Yijing) 周易

    A classical collection of hexagrams, line statements, and interpretive writings.

  • Book of Odes 詩經

    An early Chinese anthology of songs, court poems, and ritual hymns.

  • Book of Rites 禮記

    A Confucian collection of writings on ritual, mourning, music, education, and social relationships, transmitted as one of the major ritual classics.

  • Zhuangzi 莊子

    A Daoist collection of stories, dialogues, and reflections on freedom, knowledge, transformation, and cultivation.

  • Tao Te Ching (Wang Bi Edition) 道德經 (王弼本)

    The received Laozi text with Wang Bi's third-century commentary, discussing the Dao, non-action, government, and the relation between being and nonbeing.

  • Guanyinzi (Wenshi zhenjing) 關尹子

    A Daoist work traditionally attributed to Yin Xi, the keeper of the pass.
