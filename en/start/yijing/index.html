The Book of Changes (Yijing): Hexagrams, Judgments and Commentaries

Images, situations, and the art of timely action

A book to read one situation at a time

Open the Book of Changes, or Yijing, and you meet a distinctive kind of writing: six-line figures, brief judgments, dragons, journeys, marriages, and warnings about what may happen next. Its other familiar name, Zhouyi, means the Changes of Zhou. The book began in a divinatory setting and became a major resource for reflection on conduct, government, and the changing world.

A useful first aim is modest: learn how one hexagram is arranged, then read it slowly. The symbols become less forbidding when you can distinguish a statement about an entire situation from advice attached to one position within it. You can begin this kind of reading without first learning a method of casting an oracle.

The layers of the book

The received work brings together a core of sixty-four hexagrams, their hexagram statements and line statements, and a body of commentaries called the Ten Wings. Tradition holds that Fu Xi originated the trigrams, King Wen composed the hexagram judgments, the Duke of Zhou composed the line statements, and Confucius supplied the Wings. Scholars debate the dating and formation of these layers, which developed over many centuries.

The traditional attributions introduce the book's place in Chinese cultural memory. They join cosmic pattern, the founding of the Zhou, and the teaching of the sages within one work. For a historical orientation, the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's account of the Yijing explains how the received classic developed and how its readers connected divination with philosophical reflection.

On a first visit, keep three questions beside you: Which words belong to the hexagram judgment? Which belong to an individual line? Where does a commentary begin? These distinctions make a crowded page much easier to follow.

How a hexagram works

A hexagram consists of six lines, read from the bottom upwards. A line is either solid, associated with yang, or broken, associated with yin. Three lines form a trigram; a hexagram combines a lower and an upper trigram. The eight trigrams are associated with heaven, earth, thunder, wind, water, fire, mountain, and lake. Combining them gives sixty-four possible figures.

Each hexagram has a name and a short general statement, often called its judgment. Six further statements address its six line positions. In the received text, nine designates a yang line and six a yin line: thus Qian's opening line is “initial nine,” while Kun's is “initial six.” The position matters as much as the kind of line. Something suitable at the beginning of an undertaking may become excessive at its height.

Try sketching one figure and numbering its positions from one to six. Beside each position, write a few words describing its image or action. This turns an unfamiliar diagram into a sequence you can examine. Leave room for ambiguity: a dragon, a river crossing, or an approaching guest can carry more than one implication.

Qian: learning to notice the moment

Begin with Qian, the first hexagram, made of six solid lines. Its opening line presents a hidden dragon and advises against action. The second brings the dragon into the field; the fifth places it in the sky. The final line warns of regret when the dragon rises too far. Between these images, the third line describes sustained effort accompanied by evening vigilance.

Read the six statements together before turning to their explanations. Ask what changes between concealment, emergence, activity, and excess. The same impressive creature appears in different circumstances, and the advice changes with its position. A helpful exercise is to describe each line without using the word “success.” What is possible here? What requires patience? What has reached its limit?

Then read the Image commentary, introduced by Xiang yue, “the Image says.” It connects heaven's vigorous movement with the cultivated person's unceasing effort. You can now see an interpretive step taking place: a cosmic image becomes a model for human conduct. The later explanation adds a perspective to the earlier language of dragons and omens.

Kun and Modesty: two companion readings

Kun, the second hexagram, consists of six broken lines and is associated with earth. Its first line moves from frost underfoot to the coming of hard ice. This is an especially clear invitation to notice small beginnings. Its Image commentary presents the earth's capacity to bear things as a model for generous, sustaining virtue.

Read Qian and Kun side by side. List their verbs and images before deciding how to translate their relationship into abstract terms. Qian's exertion and Kun's bearing capacity offer different ways of responding. Their commentaries repeatedly connect human activity with the transformations of heaven and earth.

Next try hexagram fifteen, Qian 謙, usually translated as Modesty or Humility; it is a different character from the first hexagram's Qian 乾. Its image places a mountain within the earth. The Image commentary speaks of reducing excess, supplying deficiency, and weighing things fairly. Here a familiar virtue becomes a question of proportion and distribution. Read the upper lines too: their references to military action show how much more varied the text is than a collection of reassuring maxims.

Finding your way through the commentaries

The Ten Wings comprise the two parts each of the Tuan commentary, the Xiang commentary, and the Xici, together with the Wenyan, Shuogua, Xugua, and Zagua. These names respectively introduce explanations of judgments and images, the Appended Statements, comments on Qian and Kun, discussions of trigrams, the sequence of hexagrams, and paired or contrasting hexagrams.

Start the Xici, also called the Great Commentary, with its opening chapters. In the second chapter of its first part, the cultivated reader contemplates images and attends to the words attached to the lines. Chapter five relates the Way to yin and yang and describes change through continuing generation. Read these passages after Qian and Kun: the larger claims now have concrete examples behind them.

For a further encounter with the commentarial tradition, open the library's Zhouyi commentary associated with Wang Bi. Choose a hexagram you already know and compare the explanations line by line. Keep the classic's statement in one column and the commentary's reasoning in another. This allows you to see both the continuity of reading and the choices made by an interpreter.

An ending that leaves room to continue

The received sequence closes with After Completion, hexagram sixty-three, followed by Before Completion, sixty-four. The first warns of disorder at the end; the second pictures a young fox wetting its tail while crossing water. Read their judgments together, then their Image commentaries. Completion still calls for foresight, and an unfinished passage calls for care.

These two figures offer a satisfying close to a first reading session. Return to your opening hexagram and ask whether you now notice its transitions more clearly. A small notebook of images, positions, and questions will serve you better at this stage than an attempt to memorize every correspondence.
